## What it is
**Description:** Drag two numbers onto a column mat and find out what happens when they are lined up by the wrong ends.
**Why:** Put two long numbers one under the other so that every digit lands in its own column, then add them a column at a time starting from the right.
**Look:** A ruled mat with a heading over every column, two strips of digit cards that can be slid along it, and the answer building up under the rule.
**Context:** Grade 4, the first working topic of the addition chapter. An adult sees a column sum being filled in, which a child could do on squared paper. What the page is actually doing is settling the first step, which the printed six steps put before any arithmetic: arranging the numbers in the columns correctly. A child who lines the strips up by their left ends gets every column wrong for a reason that has nothing to do with adding, and on paper that is invisible until the answer is marked.
**For the child:** Slide each number along the mat until its right end is in the ones column. Press check lining to see whether every digit is under its own heading. Then tap the ones column and work leftwards, one column at a time, until the sum is finished. Name the addends and the sum before you leave.
**Teaching notes:** Set the first sum out on squared paper by hand before the tool is opened, one digit to a square, and write the column headings in before any digit goes in. Then use the tool to do the thing paper cannot: push a strip out of line on purpose and ask what each digit is now worth. A child who has seen the headings change over a digit does not line numbers up by their left ends again. Keep the ones first control locked for the first few sums and make them say each column total out loud before the next one lights. Open the expanded strip once the columns are settled, so the expanded form is read as the same sum written sideways rather than as a separate exercise. Use the naming control every time and insist on the words: the addends are what you add, the sum is what you get. Those two words carry the rest of the chapter, and the vocabulary work near the end of the printed chapter asks for them back.

## Opening (first frame of every mount)
Every mount comes up already furnished for its phase with nothing in front of it. On the first phase the mat stands five columns wide with a five digit strip lying home in its columns and a shorter strip loose on the ledge below it, not standing in any column at all. On the second two strips of the same length are already home and every square under the rule is blank. On the third the mat stands six columns wide with a six digit strip on each of its two rows. On the fourth a finished sum stands with its total under the rule and the naming labels waiting at the left of the mat. The running count at the top left holds the columns closed against the columns the mat is ruled for, and the answer slot and the pad stand empty down the right. One thing moves, once: the loose strip, or the ones column, or the leftmost column, or the first naming label, gives one slow pulse while the voice asks for the two numbers to be lined up by their right ends. That pulse is the only invitation, and a finger on it is the whole way in.

## Phases (what each mount shows and does; the words are in tool.tsx)

### 1 · Columns stand for places
**Screen:** the mat with a five digit strip in place and a three digit strip loose beside it
**Does:** slides the shorter strip until its ones digit stands under the ones heading
**Win:** the check lining control finds nothing out of place and the empty columns stay empty
**Feels:** one number is shorter than the other, and the gap belongs at the left where nothing is standing
**Goal:** the addends are lined up by their right ends so each digit sits in its own column
**Observe:** The shorter number left its left columns empty instead of filling them.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- pushes the shorter strip to the left end of the mat so that the two numbers begin under one another. → the headings above the shorter strip are read out as it stands, so its leading digit is heard in the ten thousands column and the worth written under it is read out as tens of thousands; check lining then outlines every digit of that strip in amber and names the place each one is standing in. The strip is not moved for the child and nothing is cleared.
- fills the shorter number's empty left columns with zeros of its own and counts them as part of it. → the zeros the child wrote stay exactly where they are, drawn grey, and the strip is read out twice, once with them and once without, with the two readings written one under the other and coming to the same number; the zeros are then left standing where they were and the empty columns beside them are outlined.
**Misconception to break:** Pushing 362 under the left of 43,215 so its 3 lands in the ten thousands column.

### 2 · The ones column goes first
**Screen:** two five digit strips lined up, with every column still blank under the rule
**Does:** taps the ones column, then each column to its left in turn
**Win:** the columns fill from the right and the sum stands complete under the rule
**Feels:** the right hand column is the only one that can be finished for certain, so it goes first
**Goal:** the columns are worked from the right, one at a time
**Observe:** Pushing the short strip one column to the left made every digit in it worth ten times as much.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- taps the leftmost column first and works rightwards, the way a number is read. → every total the child wrote stays under the rule exactly where it was put. The ones first control then locks itself, the same two strips are worked again from the right with one column lighting at a time, and the two runs stand one under the other with the order of work numbered beside each column. Nothing is cleared.
- adds the two numbers in their head and types a whole answer without a column being touched. → the mat works the columns anyway from the right, each one lighting as it is added and writing its total in green under the rule, and the child's number stands in the answer slot beside the one the mat builds; where the two part, the first column from the right that parts them is lit amber and stays lit.
**Misconception to break:** Starting at the ten thousands column and working rightwards.

### 3 · More columns do not mean a new method
**Screen:** the mat grown to six columns with two six digit strips on it
**Does:** works the same way along a longer mat and counts the steps it took
**Win:** the last column on the left is worked and the mat stops, with no new rule needed
**Feels:** six columns and not one new thing to learn
**Goal:** a six digit sum is a shorter sum with more steps on the left
**Observe:** Nothing left any column on this mat, so the sum came out as long as the longer addend.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- asks for a new rule because the numbers are longer than any they have added before. → the six column sum stays where it is and the four column sum the child worked earlier is laid under it with their right ends aligned; the steps of each are counted along from the right, and each step of the longer sum is lit beside the step of the shorter one it matches, with two steps at the left end matching nothing at all.
- works five of the six columns and reads the sum as finished. → the leftmost column keeps both of its digits lit with the square under the rule left empty and ringed in amber, and the sum is read out place by place from the right until the reading arrives at nothing at all. Nothing is filled in for the child.
**Misconception to break:** Treating a six digit sum as a new kind of question and adding the leftmost digits first.

### 4 · The addends are what you add and the sum is what you get
**Screen:** a finished sum on the mat with the naming control waiting
**Does:** labels the two strips and the answer, then reads the whole thing out
**Win:** both labels land on the right rows and the finished sum is read aloud
**Feels:** the two names stay put however the numbers are swapped about
**Goal:** the parts of a written sum can be named
**Observe:** The columns were worked from the right and the sum filled from the right.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- calls the answer under the rule an addend, because it is the largest number on the mat. → the rule is drawn heavier and the two rows above it lift a little together while the word addend is written on each of them, and the row below the rule stays down and takes the word sum; the child's label stays exactly where it was put, so that the two readings can be held against each other.
- labels only the lower strip as an addend, because only one of the numbers looks as though it is doing anything. → the two strips swap rows on the mat, the columns are worked again from the right and the same total stands under the rule in green; both strips carry the word addend the whole time and neither of them is ever the one being added to.
**Misconception to break:** Calling the answer an addend, or calling one of the numbers above the rule the sum.

## Delight
The first phase, the moment the loose strip is dragged along the mat. It does not snap anywhere and nothing is marked. As it passes each column the heading over its leading digit is read out afresh, hundreds, then thousands, then ten thousands, and the worth written small underneath grows ten times at every step, so the same three digit cards are worth hundreds in one column and tens of thousands two columns along. Slide it back and the worths shrink again the same way. Nothing else moves and the voice says nothing while it happens.

**General miss line:** Slide them back and read the heading standing over each digit.

## Answer key
Line the addends up by their right ends, so that each digit stands in the column of its own place. A shorter addend leaves its left columns empty rather than filled. Add the ones column first and work leftwards, one column at a time, writing each total under the rule. The numbers being added are the addends and the answer under the rule is the sum.

## Student controls
- **Digit strips**: slides each addend left or right along the column mat
- **Check lining**: lights every digit that is not standing under its own place
- **Headings**: reads the column name over whichever digit is touched
- **Add this column**: works the lit column and writes its total under the rule
- **Ones first**: locks the working order so the rightmost column lights first
- **Expanded strip**: writes each addend as its thousands, hundreds, tens and ones
- **Name the parts**: labels the two strips as the addends and the answer as the sum

## Textbook (this topic)
**Pages:** 17, 18
**Key terms:** addend, sum, column, place
Five and six digit numbers are added in columns, with no column yet holding more than nine. Each column is a place, so a digit must sit in the column its place belongs to. Line the numbers up by their right ends and every digit lands in the right column. Line them up by their left ends and none of them do. Adding starts at the ones and works left. Anything that spills out of a column has to move one place to the left, so a column on the left cannot be finished until the one on its right is done. Nothing about the method changes. There are simply more columns to work through, and you still start at the ones and move left. A six digit sum is a four digit sum with two more steps at the end. The numbers being added together are called the addends. What they make is called the sum. Naming them makes it easier to say what a question is asking for.
