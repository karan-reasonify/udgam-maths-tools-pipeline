## What it is
**Description:** A problem in sentences, with every number liftable, and a mat that only takes the ones the question needs.
**Why:** Turn a problem written in sentences into a column sum, using only the numbers that answer what was asked.
**Look:** A short problem at the top with every number liftable, and an empty column mat waiting underneath it.
**Context:** Grade 4, and the method is finished by now, so these two pages are about getting from a sentence to a sum. An adult sees word problems. What the page is actually doing is naming two separate decisions: what is being asked, and which of the printed numbers answer it. The page says outright that a problem may print a number that is not needed, which is a thing most books leave for a child to discover by being marked wrong.
**For the child:** Read the problem and pick the card that says what it is asking. Lift only the numbers that answer it down onto the mat, and grey out any number that has no job. If a number is described rather than printed, build it first. Add the columns, then write your answer back into a sentence.
**Teaching notes:** Make the question card a real step and do not let anybody skip it. A child who has said out loud what is being asked has already sorted the numbers; a child who has not will add whatever is printed. Keep the spare number greyed rather than deleted, because the point is not that the number vanishes but that it was looked at and turned down. When a table comes up, read it across with a finger and count the rows before any of them come down, since a table read downwards is how the last line gets lost. The build bay is the hardest phase and the one most worth the time: an amount described as so many more than another is not on the page, and a child who adds the extra amount to the total at the end has answered a different question. Finish every problem with the answer sentence read aloud. A number with no sentence round it is not yet an answer.

## Phases (what each mount shows and does; the words are in tool.tsx)

### 1 · A word problem hides a column sum
**Screen:** a two sentence problem with two numbers in it and an empty mat
**Does:** picks the question card, lifts both numbers down and adds the columns
**Win:** the mat holds two addends and the answer is written back into a sentence
**Feels:** a paragraph turns into two numbers, one under the other, and the sentences stop mattering
**Goal:** the amounts being put together come down onto the mat as addends
**Observe:** Picking the question card sorted the numbers before a single one had been lifted.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- takes the numbers down in the order the sentences print them without ever saying what the question asks. → the question cards lift together and the last sentence of the problem lights on the board, while every number card goes back up into the sentence it came from; no card will lift again until a question card has been picked. Nothing is marked and nothing is cleared.
- lines a four digit addend up with a five digit one by their left ends, so every column is out by a place. → the heading over every digit of the shorter card is named as it slides, and the card carries on sliding right until its last digit stands in the ones column, leaving its left hand column standing empty. The rule and the sum row stay empty while it moves, so nothing is added until the places agree.
**Misconception to break:** Lining 12,480 and 9,365 up by their left ends, because one has five digits and the other four.

### 2 · A table is a list of addends
**Screen:** a table of three rows with the mat empty beneath it
**Does:** taps each row down in turn and adds the three addends
**Win:** all three rows sit on the mat and none of them is left behind
**Feels:** a table turns into a column of addends, and the rows are suddenly the thing worth counting
**Goal:** each row of a table is one addend
**Observe:** A table came down as three addends and the mat looked exactly like the mat the sentences made.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- brings the first two rows down, works the sum and stops, leaving the third row up in the table. → the running count reads the rows that are down against the rows in the table and the two numbers do not agree; the rows that have come down are ruled through on the board while the row still up there keeps its full ink, so the one that was left behind is the only dark row on the table.
- brings a heading down onto the mat as though it were an amount to add. → the row lifts whole and parts in the air above the mat: the heading half turns and settles on the mat's left margin as a label, and the amount half comes down into the columns by its right hand end, so each row gives one label and one addend and there is nowhere for a second number from that row to go.
**Misconception to break:** Adding the first two years and stopping, because a table read downwards can lose its last line.

### 3 · Not every number printed is needed
**Screen:** a three number problem where one number answers nothing
**Does:** greys the spare number and takes down only the two that are needed
**Win:** the spare number dims in its sentence and the mat holds only two addends
**Feels:** a number can be perfectly true and still have no work to do in this sum
**Goal:** a number can be printed and still have no job in the question
**Observe:** The spare number stayed on the board in grey and the answer was right without it.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- adds the hall's seating capacity in along with the two counts of people who came. → the question card stays lit and each number card is held up against it in turn with the sentence it came from printed underneath; the capacity card is the only one whose sentence says what the hall could hold rather than who came in, and it settles back into its place in grey. The child's total stays in the slot beside the new one.
- deletes the spare number off the board so the problem looks tidy. → the number comes back into its sentence in grey with its outline still drawn round it, and the whole problem is read through once with it standing there; grey is the colour this chapter keeps for something holding a place and bringing nothing to the sum, which is precisely the job that number is doing.
**Misconception to break:** Adding the 5,000 seats in as well, because the number is printed in the problem.

### 4 · Sometimes an addend has to be built first
**Screen:** a problem where one day is described as so many more than another
**Does:** opens the build bay, works the described amount out and brings it down
**Win:** a new card appears on the mat carrying the amount that was never printed
**Feels:** a number the problem needs is nowhere on the page, and you make it yourself before you can use it
**Goal:** a described amount is worked out before it can be added
**Observe:** One of the addends on the mat was never printed anywhere in the problem.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- adds the thousand more and the hundred more onto the first day's total and hands that in as the answer for both days. → the build bay shows what came out of it standing on a card of its own, and that card goes up onto the mat beside the first day's card so that two day cards are lying there, each with its own total printed on it; the mat plainly has two rows to add, and the child's number is printed beside the second of them. Nothing is marked.
- hunts the board for the second day's figure, waiting for it to be printed somewhere among the sentences. → every number card on the board lifts a little and sets down again, and the sentence that describes the amount underlines itself word by word; the build bay then opens with its two empty rows and the words more than standing between them, so the place where the missing number has to be made is drawn rather than said.
**Misconception to break:** Answering 12,060, which is the second day alone, because the two increases were added and the first day was never brought back in.

## Delight
The fourth phase, the moment the described amount is built. The sentence that describes it lights word by word as the build bay fills, the printed day going into the upper row and the amount it is more by into the lower, and the small sum works itself there in its own columns. Then the card drops out of the foot of the bay with a drawn edge, rises back up to the board and settles into the sentence where the description was standing, so the problem can be read straight through with every number now printed in it. Nothing else moves and the voice says nothing while it happens.

**General miss line:** Read the problem again, one sentence at a time, with your question card showing.

## Answer key
Say what is being asked before touching a number. Then take down only the numbers that answer it, leaving any spare number where it is. A table is a list of addends, one to a row. An amount that is described rather than printed, such as so many more than the day before, has to be worked out first and then used. Line the chosen addends up by their right ends and add the columns from the ones, and write the answer back into a sentence.

## Student controls
- **Question cards**: states what the problem is asking before any number moves
- **Lift a number**: takes a number out of the sentence and down onto the mat
- **Push it back**: returns a number to its sentence, so nothing is committed
- **Grey the spare**: dims a number that is not needed while leaving it readable
- **Table row**: taps a whole row of a table down to the mat as one addend
- **Build bay**: works out an amount that is described rather than printed
- **Answer sentence**: writes the finished sum back into a sentence that answers the question

## Textbook (this topic)
**Pages:** 23, 24
**Key terms:** word problem, addend, total, sum
Problems written in sentences are turned into column sums, and the numbers that are not needed are left out. Most addition problems say in sentences what a column sum says in digits. Find the amounts being put together, write them one under the other with the places lined up, and add. A table gives one number for each row or column heading. To find the total across all of them, take each number out of the table and add them as you would any other list. Some problems print a number that has nothing to do with the question. Work out what is being asked first, then choose only the numbers that answer it. Using an extra number because it is there gives a wrong answer. An amount may be described instead of printed. When a problem says so many more than before, you have to work that amount out by adding, and only then can you use it in the sum you were asked for.
