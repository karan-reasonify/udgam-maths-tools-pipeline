## What it is
**Description:** Round the addends first, add them the fast way, and stand that answer next to the exact one to see how close it landed.
**Why:** Get a quick answer by rounding the numbers first, then use it to decide whether the exact answer you worked out is sensible.
**Look:** A stage split down the middle, the exact sum building in columns on one side and the rounded sum on the other, with a bar between the answers.
**Context:** Grade 4, the closing topic, and it answers the question the opening page asked, which wanted an approximate number rather than an exact one. An adult sees rounding done twice and then added. What the page is actually doing is putting an estimate to work: it is not a worse answer, it is a fast one, and its job is to tell you whether the exact answer you worked out is anywhere near right.
**For the child:** Read which unit the question asks for and set the picker to it. Round both numbers first, then add the rounded ones. Work the exact sum on the other side and run the gap bar between the two answers. If they are far apart, go back and check your working.
**Teaching notes:** Round first and add second, every time, and say it in that order out loud, because adding first and rounding the answer is a different question that gives a different number. Read the unit out of the question before anything is rounded. The printed exercises name the nearest hundred in one place and the nearest thousand in another, and a child who rounds to whichever unit they like will be right about the method and wrong about the answer. Build the teaching on the worked example that adds 42,135 and 46,849: rounded to the nearest ten thousand those become 40,000 and 50,000, so the estimated sum is 90,000 against an actual sum of 88,984, and the two standing side by side are exactly the point. Read the two crowd figures beside the estimating heading as rounded to the nearest thousand, which is what the numbers printed next to them are; rounding either of them to the nearest ten thousand would give 40,000, so do not run that pair as a ten thousands example. Finish on the check bay. An estimate that is never used to check anything has not yet done its job, and this is where the opening question about approximately how many trains run daily is finally answered.

## Phases (what each mount shows and does; the words are in tool.tsx)

### 1 · Round each addend before adding
**Screen:** two addends with the estimate side empty and the unit already set
**Does:** rounds both addends and adds the rounded numbers
**Win:** the estimate lands without a single column being written
**Feels:** the adding turns out to be the easy half, and all of the work is in the rounding that comes first
**Goal:** the rounding happens first and the adding happens to the rounded numbers
**Observe:** The estimate landed without one column being written on that side of the stage.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- adds the two addends exactly and rounds the answer afterwards, handing that in as the estimate. → the two roads are drawn side by side on the estimate side with the order of their steps numbered beside them: rounding the two crowds of 23,501 and 32,546 to the nearest thousand first gives 24,000 and 33,000, which come to 57,000, while adding first gives 56,047 and rounding that afterwards gives 56,000. Both are printed, neither is crossed out, and the question's own wording is lit above them.
- rounds one addend, leaves the other exactly as it stands and adds the two together. → the number line strip opens under the addend that was left alone, with the two nearest multiples of the unit on plates at its ends and the pin standing between them; the estimate side takes rounded numbers only, so that addend waits out on its strip until it is sent to one end or the other. Nothing already written is cleared.
**Misconception to break:** Adding first and rounding the answer afterwards, which is a different question from the one asked.

### 2 · The question names the unit
**Screen:** the same pair with the unit picker offering the nearest hundred and the nearest thousand
**Does:** estimates the pair to one unit, then to the other, and compares
**Win:** two different estimates stand together and neither is crossed out
**Feels:** one pair of numbers and two different estimates, and both of them survive
**Goal:** the unit comes from the question, and a different unit gives a different estimate
**Observe:** The same pair gave a different estimate under each unit and neither was crossed out.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- rounds to whichever unit makes the numbers easiest, without reading the unit out of the question. → the question is reprinted across the top with its unit underlined, and the estimate side builds twice, once under each unit, the two estimates standing one above the other with the unit each was rounded to named under it. Neither is crossed out and the child's answer stays in the slot.
- says the estimate made under the other unit must be wrong, because it does not match the one just made. → two strips open together under the same addend, one zoomed to the hundreds either side of it and one to the thousands either side, with the same pin standing on both; the nearer end is a different number on each strip, and on each strip it really is the nearer end. Nothing is marked.
**Misconception to break:** Rounding to the nearest thousand when the question asked for the nearest hundred.

### 3 · An estimate sits close to the exact answer
**Screen:** both sides finished with the gap bar waiting between them
**Does:** runs the gap bar and reads how far apart the two answers are
**Win:** the gap bar is drawn short and labelled, and both answers stay standing
**Feels:** near is good enough, and near was what the question asked for all along
**Goal:** the estimate is near the exact sum rather than equal to it
**Observe:** The estimated sum of 90,000 never matched the exact sum of 88,984, and it was still a good estimate.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- calls the estimate wrong because it does not equal the exact sum. → the two answers drop onto one number line together, the exact sum of 88,984 and the estimated sum of 90,000 that the pair 42,135 and 46,849 gives, and the space between them is drawn and labelled; the line runs the whole length of the sum, so the gap is seen against what was being measured rather than on its own. Both answers stay standing.
- rounds the addends and then writes the exact sum into the estimate's place, because the exact sum is the one they trust. → the estimate side shows that it has no columns to clear away and adds its two rounded numbers straight across in one line, while the exact side keeps its six ruled columns with its carried digit still standing small above the column it joined. The two ways of arriving at an answer are left side by side with the work each one took still showing.
**Misconception to break:** Calling the estimate wrong because it does not come to 80,270.

### 4 · An estimate is how you check an exact answer
**Screen:** the check bay holding a written answer with the estimate building beside it
**Does:** builds the estimate and decides whether the written answer can be right
**Win:** the gap bar stretches long and the written answer is sent back to be worked again
**Feels:** a fast answer that is roughly right catches an exact answer that is badly wrong
**Goal:** an answer far from its estimate is the one to go back to
**Observe:** An answer sitting far below its estimate turned out to be wrong, and the estimate had taken no time at all to build.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- trusts a written answer because its columns look neat, and never builds the estimate beside it. → the estimate builds in a moment on the right, 28,450 going to 30,000 and 31,260 going to 30,000 for an estimate of 60,000, and the gap bar is run from there to the written answer of 5,971; the bar stretches right across the stage with how far apart they are printed on it, and the written sum is left standing exactly as it was with all its columns still showing.
- sees the gap stretch long and rubs the whole sum out to begin it again. → the columns of the written sum light one at a time from the ones, and the first column whose digit does not agree with the rebuilt one is the only one left lit; the rest of the sum stays standing, and the gap bar shortens as that single column is put right. Nothing is cleared and nothing is marked.
**Misconception to break:** Trusting a written answer because the columns look neat, instead of standing an estimate beside it.

## Delight
The third phase, the moment the gap bar is run. The two answers lift off their own sides of the stage and come down together onto one long line drawn from nothing up to the exact sum, and the distance between them, which read as a whole number of its own a moment ago, turns out to be no wider on that line than the pin that marks it. The printed label stays beside the gap, so the number that measures it and the size of it are both on the stage at once, and they do not look like the same thing at all. Nothing else moves and the voice says nothing while it happens.

**General miss line:** Look at the strip under each number again, and at which end it is nearer.

## Answer key
To estimate a sum, round every addend to the unit the question names and then add the rounded numbers. The unit comes from the question, so the same pair of addends gives different estimates for different units and each is right for the unit it was asked for. An estimate is not meant to equal the exact sum, only to land near it. If the exact answer and the estimate are far apart, one of the two is wrong and both are worth checking.

## Student controls
- **Unit picker**: sets the unit the rounding is to and re-rounds both addends
- **Round first**: rounds every addend before anything is added
- **Exact side**: works the addends in columns for the exact sum
- **Estimate side**: adds the rounded numbers, which needs no columns
- **Number line strip**: shows an addend between the two nearest multiples of the unit
- **Gap bar**: draws and labels the distance between the estimate and the exact sum
- **Check bay**: takes an answer somebody wrote and asks whether the estimate agrees with it

## Textbook (this topic)
**Pages:** 24, 25, 26
**Key terms:** estimate, rounding, sum, addend
Addends are rounded before they are added, which gives a quick answer that tells you whether the exact one is sensible. To estimate a sum, round every addend first and then add the rounded numbers. Rounded numbers end in zeros, so they can be added without writing out columns. Both could be. The unit to round to is set by the question, not chosen by you, and rounding to a different unit gives a different estimate. Always read what the question asks for before rounding. An estimate is not meant to match the exact sum. It is meant to land near it. Setting the two side by side shows how near, and they should never be far apart. If the exact answer and the estimate are far apart, something has gone wrong in one of them. That is what an estimate is for, and it is why it is worth doing even when an exact answer is wanted.
