## What it is
**Description:** Watch ten of one place tie themselves into one of the next, and put the carried digit where it has to go.
**Why:** Find out what happens when a column holds more than nine, and where the part that will not fit has to go.
**Look:** A column mat with a small circle drawn above every column, a tray of tokens beside it, and a tally of the trades along the top.
**Context:** Grade 4, the middle of the addition chapter and the half of column addition that actually goes wrong. An adult sees the same sums as the topic before with different digits. What the page is doing is asking for a trade: ten of one place for one of the next, stated four times in words before a single sum is worked. The carry is a quantity that moved, not a mark, and a child who writes it in the margin and adds it on at the end has learnt the mark.
**For the child:** Fill a column and watch what happens when it passes nine. Ten tokens tie into one and walk left into the circle. Write the digit that stays, then count the circle in when you work the next column. Try it with three numbers and see how big the carry can get.
**Teaching notes:** Say the four trades out loud from the page before the tool is opened, and get them said the long way every time: ten hundreds are one thousand, seventeen hundreds are one thousand and seven hundreds. Then use slow trade for the first few columns, because at full speed the bundle is invisible and what is learnt is a mark rather than a move. The close column control is the whole tool: a column that will not close until the circle above it has been counted in is the difference between a carry that is written down and a carry that is used. When the third strip goes on, stop and ask before anybody works it out whether the circle can hold more than one. The answer surprises them and it should. Come back to the opening railway table here, because the total length of the routes is exactly this: a column sum of seven numbers with trades in most columns, and it is the first point in the chapter at which the opening question can honestly be answered.

## Opening (first frame of every mount)
Every mount comes up already furnished for its phase with nothing in front of it. On the first phase the hundreds column of the mat stands full of tokens, more of them than the column is tall, with the thousands column beside it ruled and empty and the tray below holding the four kinds of token. On the second a ones column has already been counted past nine and the single square under the rule waits. On the third a tens column stands with two digits below it and the circle above it already filled. On the fourth three strips lie on the mat and the ones column of them has been counted past nineteen. The running count at the top left holds the columns that have traded against the columns closed so far, and the answer slot and the pad stand empty down the right. One thing moves, once: the tenth token in the full column, or the square under the rule, or the filled circle, or the third strip, gives one slow pulse while the voice says that a column holding more than nine will not close. That pulse is the only invitation, and a finger on it is the whole way in.

## Phases (what each mount shows and does; the words are in tool.tsx)

### 1 · Ten of one place make one of the next
**Screen:** a hundreds column holding sixteen tokens, with the thousands column empty
**Does:** runs the slow trade and reads off what the sixteen hundreds became
**Win:** ten tokens tie into one and walk left, leaving six behind
**Feels:** ten things you can count go in and one thing comes out, and nothing was lost on the way
**Goal:** ten of any place trade for one of the place to its left
**Observe:** Ten tokens went into the tie and one token came out of it.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- writes the whole count in the hundreds column, because that is what the column holds. → the column is drawn with its one square and all of its tokens standing in it, so the tokens stand higher than the column is tall; then ten of them tie into one thousand token at walking pace and walk left up the lane, six are left standing behind them, and the column can close. Nothing is cleared.
- trades ten hundreds for ten thousands, keeping the ten and changing only the name. → the ten hundred tokens go into the tie and exactly one thousand token comes out of it, and both are held on the stage together, the ten counted aloud on one side and the single token on the other; the tray's thousand token is then laid beside the ten hundred tokens in a row, so the two can be measured against each other.
**Misconception to break:** Writing 16 in the hundreds column, which no single column can hold.

### 2 · A column can only keep one digit
**Screen:** a ones column that has come to fourteen with one square to write in
**Does:** writes the digit that stays and sends the rest to the circle above the next column
**Win:** the column closes holding a single digit and the circle above the next one fills
**Feels:** the total will not fit in the square, and only one digit of it is allowed to stay
**Goal:** the right hand digit stays and the rest leaves the column
**Observe:** The column closed holding one digit, and it was the right hand digit of the total.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- writes the whole two digit total under the ones column, using two squares for it. → what the child wrote stays under the rule, and the mat rules the ones column back to its own single square, so the second digit is left standing outside the column it was written in; the heading above that stray digit lights, and the column's tokens are then counted into a tie of ten with the rest left over.
- writes the right hand digit and lets the rest go, because the right hand digit is what fits. → the token that was dropped is drawn back onto the stage, standing alone in the lane above the next column, and the sum is read out twice, once with it and once without; the two totals stand one under the other and what lies between them is exactly the token that was let go.
**Misconception to break:** Writing 14 under the ones column and carrying nothing.

### 3 · The carried digit joins the next column
**Screen:** a tens column with two digits below and a filled circle above
**Does:** counts the circle in with the column before closing it
**Win:** the column will not close until the circle has been counted, and then it does
**Feels:** the little digit above the column is not a note, it is one of the things you are adding
**Goal:** the carry is added in with that column, not after it
**Observe:** The next column would not close while the circle above it still held an uncounted digit.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- adds the column, writes its digit, and then adds the carried one on at the end of the whole sum. → the column will not close: its outline thickens and does not dim, the circle above it holds amber, and everything standing in the column is counted out one token at a time with the circle counted in among them. Nothing is cleared and nothing is marked.
- drops the carried digit into the circle above the column it came out of. → the digit is handed straight back and the column it belongs to is lit one place to the left with its heading read out; the circle it was dropped into is left visibly empty, and the lane between the two columns is drawn so that the way the token travelled can be followed with a finger.
**Misconception to break:** Adding the carried ones on at the end of the whole sum instead of into the next column.

### 4 · Three addends carry in the same way
**Screen:** the mat with three strips on it and a ones column past nineteen
**Does:** works the sum through and reads what each circle is holding
**Win:** a circle fills with a two and the sum still closes correctly
**Feels:** three numbers in one column, and the circle above it takes a two
**Goal:** nothing changes with three addends except how big the carry can be
**Observe:** With three addends a circle filled with a two.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- carries one out of a column that came to more than nineteen, because a carry has always been one. → the column is emptied back into tokens and tied slowly: ten tie and walk left, then the tokens still standing are counted and ten of those tie and walk left too, so two tokens arrive in the same circle and the circle writes a two. The child's digit stays in the answer slot beside it.
- adds the first two strips, writes their total under the rule, and then adds the third to that in a second sum. → both ways are worked on the same mat, the three strips in one column sum on the left of the working area and the two step working on the right, and the two totals stand side by side and agree; the number of columns that had to be counted is written under each of them.
**Misconception to break:** Carrying only 1 out of the tens column, because a carry is assumed always to be one.

## Delight
The fourth phase, the moment the tens column of the three addend sum is closed. Its tokens gather and ten of them tie into a single hundred and walk left up the lane. The tokens still standing are counted, and there are more than ten of them left, so ten of those tie as well and a second hundred follows the first into the same circle. The circle writes a two in vermilion, and a child who has watched every carry so far be a one sees a carry that is not. Nothing else moves and the voice says nothing while it happens.

**General miss line:** Empty it back into tokens and tie them again, slowly.

## Answer key
Ten of any place make one of the next place up, so a column that comes to more than nine is split: the right hand digit stays in the column and the rest is carried into the circle above the next column on the left. The carried digit is added in with everything else in that column, not added on at the end. With three addends a column can come to more than nineteen, so the carried digit can be larger than one.

## Student controls
- **Token tray**: fills a column with the loose tokens its digits stand for
- **Slow trade**: ties ten tokens into one of the next place up at walking pace
- **Carry circle**: takes the carried digit and holds it above the next column
- **Close column**: refuses to close a column until the circle above it has been counted in
- **Third addend**: adds a third strip to the mat, so a column can carry more than one
- **Carry count**: tallies how many columns had to trade in this sum
- **Replay carries**: walks the finished sum again showing only the trades

## Textbook (this topic)
**Pages:** 18, 19
**Key terms:** regrouping, carry, addend, sum
A column that adds past nine sends one across to the next place, and the chapter calls that regrouping. Ten ones make one ten, ten tens make one hundred, and ten hundreds make one thousand. Because of that, seventeen hundreds can be written as one thousand and seven hundreds. Trading like this is called regrouping. Every column holds a single digit, so a column that comes to more than nine has to be split. The right hand digit stays in the column. The rest moves one place to the left, where it is worth what that place is worth. The carried digit is written above the next column and added in with everything else in that column. It is not added on at the end and it is not left over. Nothing changes when there are three addends instead of two, except that a column can come to more, so the digit carried can be larger than one. You still write the right hand digit and carry the rest.
