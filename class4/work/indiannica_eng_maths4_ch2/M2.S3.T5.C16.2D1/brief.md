## What it is
**Description:** Swap the addends, regroup them, and add one digit at a time, with two sums running side by side to compare.
**Why:** Find out what you are allowed to change about a sum without changing its answer, and which single digit moves when you add a round amount.
**Look:** Two column mats side by side sharing one set of number cards, with a step bay and a ring of circles below them.
**Context:** Grade 4, and the chapter stops calculating here to look at what is always true. An adult sees two properties and four shortcuts. What the page is actually doing is separating a rule about places from a rule about shapes: adding a thousand raises the thousands digit, and a child who has learnt it as the digit third from the end will raise the wrong one the moment the number gets longer. The true or false question the page closes on is built to catch exactly that, and its printed wrong answer is the one most children reach for.
**For the child:** Put the same numbers on both mats in different orders and add them together. Close a bracket around whichever pair is easiest and add that pair first. In the step bay, say which digit will move before you add ten, a hundred or a thousand, then press and see whether you were right.
**Teaching notes:** Show each rule before naming it, exactly as the page does. Add the two tanks both ways on paper first, let the class see the answers match, and only then say what happened. For grouping, hand out three numbers where one pair makes a round total and let them choose, because a grouping property that is never used to make a sum easier is a sentence rather than a tool. In the step bay, make them name the digit that will move before they press. Naming it first is the whole difference between a place rule and a shape rule. Then spend real time on the true or false question the page closes on: the wrong answer printed in it is the one most children reach for, and the right correction is not that the arithmetic slipped but that the wrong place was raised. The ring bay carries the triangle puzzle from the earlier page, and it is a grouping question in disguise: the three corner numbers each count towards two sides, so the hunt is really about which three numbers go in the corners.

## Phases (what each mount shows and does; the words are in tool.tsx)

### 1 · The order of the addends does not matter
**Screen:** the same two cards on both mats, in opposite orders
**Does:** works both mats and reads the two answers against each other
**Win:** both answers land together and read the same
**Feels:** swapping the two addends over feels as though it must matter, and both sum rows fill with the same number at the same moment
**Goal:** swapping the addends leaves the sum alone
**Observe:** The two mats held the same two cards in opposite orders and their sum rows filled with the same number.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- writes the larger addend on top on both mats, tidying the swap away while setting the second sum out. → the right mat clears and refills from the tray in the order the cards were dealt, one card to a row, each ringed in ink as it lands so the order can be read off the mat; both sums are then worked down the columns from the ones and the two answers stand together in the compare rail. The child's answer stays in the slot.
- lines the two addends up by their left ends once the shorter one has been swapped to the top. → the heading over every digit of the shorter card is named under it as the card slides, and the card carries on sliding right until its last digit stands in the ones column, leaving its left hand column standing empty rather than closing the gap up. The rule and the sum row stay empty the whole time it moves, so nothing is added until the places agree.
**Misconception to break:** Believing the larger addend has to be written on top for the sum to come out right.

### 2 · The grouping of the addends does not matter either
**Screen:** three cards on each mat with brackets open around different pairs
**Does:** closes the brackets around the pair that makes a round number
**Win:** the two mats agree, and the bracketed pair on one of them makes a round number
**Feels:** you are allowed to choose, and one of the choices makes most of the sum disappear
**Goal:** any pair may be added first, and one pair is easier than the others
**Observe:** Closing a bracket turned three cards into two, and one of the two was a round number.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- adds the three cards strictly from left to right because that is the order they were dealt. → the bracket jaw opens under each possible pair in turn with that pair's total printed beside it, so the three first steps stand together on the stage: 2,600 and 7,400 make 10,000 and neither of the other pairs makes anything round, and the jaw under the round pair stays open while the others close. Nothing on the mat is cleared.
- closes the bracket and then adds the pair total in as well as the two cards it replaced. → the two bracketed cards turn face down and slide under the single card that replaced them, so three cards are plainly two; the running count at the top left drops to the cards now standing, and the sum row stays empty until the mat holds exactly the cards it is going to add.
**Misconception to break:** Thinking three addends have to be added strictly in the order they are written.

### 3 · Adding zero changes nothing and adding one steps forward
**Screen:** the step bay with a number in it and the amounts zero and one waiting
**Does:** adds each in turn and watches what moves
**Win:** the number sits unchanged after zero and stands at its successor after one
**Feels:** adding nothing turns out to be a real addition, and adding one turns out to be a step rather than a sum
**Goal:** zero leaves a number alone and one gives its successor
**Observe:** Adding nothing left every column exactly as it was, and the window printed the number twice the same.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- writes the zero onto the end of the number, so 84,376 is handed in as 84,3760. → the zero is carried down into the ones column where it belongs and sits there in grey, because it is holding a place and bringing no amount, and every column is then read out from the ones with nothing added to any of them. The number in the window is printed twice, before and after, and the two printings are the same.
- adds one to a number ending in nine and writes ten in the ones place. → the ones column opens to show the ten it has been handed, ten counters standing in a line, and they tie themselves into one bundle that will not fit in a column that holds a single digit; the bundle rides up as a small vermilion one above the tens column, the ones place is left with a grey zero, and the tens column is added again with that carried one in it.
**Misconception to break:** Writing 84,3760 for the sum with zero, by putting the zero on the end of the number.

### 4 · Adding ten, a hundred or a thousand lifts one digit
**Screen:** the step bay with the place chart behind the number and every other digit pinned
**Does:** names the digit that will move before pressing, then presses
**Win:** the named digit lifts, the rest stay pinned and the place chart agrees
**Feels:** the digit that moves is named by the chart and not by where it happens to sit on the line
**Goal:** each round amount raises the digit in its own place and no other
**Observe:** A thousand raised the fourth column counted in from the right in a five digit number and in a six digit one.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- adds a thousand by raising the hundreds digit, so 26,318 is handed in as 26,418. → the place chart slides in behind the number and the thousands column lights amber with its heading named aloud; the hundreds digit the child moved is put back and pinned with the rest, and the thousands digit alone lifts, rolls forward by one and drops, leaving 27,318 in the window. Both numbers stay printed, one under the other.
- adds a thousand to a six digit number by raising the second digit from the left, which is where the thousands digit stood in every five digit number so far. → a five digit number and a six digit one are stood one under the other with the place chart behind both, and a thousand is added to each; the thousands column lights amber in both, and the two amber digits are plainly not the same distance from the left hand end while both are the fourth column counted in from the right. Nothing is marked and nothing is cleared.
**Misconception to break:** Adding a thousand by raising the hundreds digit, which is exactly what gives 26,418.

## Delight
The third phase, the moment one is added to a number that ends in nines. The ones digit rolls forward, reaches ten, cannot stay in a column that holds one digit, and hands the step on: the four nines of 59,999 roll over one after another from the right, each leaving a grey zero behind it because a zero there is holding a place open and bringing nothing, and the roll runs out at the ten thousands column, where the five steps quietly up to six. The window is left reading 60,000, which is the number that comes straight after the one that was standing there. Nothing else moves and the voice says nothing while it happens.

**General miss line:** Work both mats again and read the two answers side by side.

## Answer key
The sum is the same whichever order the addends are written in, and the same however three or more of them are grouped, so any pair may be added first and the pair that makes a round number is worth choosing. Adding zero leaves a number exactly as it was. Adding one gives the number just after it, its successor. Adding ten raises the tens digit, adding a hundred raises the hundreds digit and adding a thousand raises the thousands digit, and which digit that is depends on the place, not on how long the number is.

## Student controls
- **Two mats**: works the same cards as two sums at once and stands the answers together
- **Swap**: reverses the order of the addends on one mat
- **Brackets**: closes a bracket around a pair and adds that pair first
- **Step bay**: adds zero, one, ten, a hundred or a thousand and lifts one digit
- **Place chart**: names the place of the digit that moved, behind the number
- **Hold the rest**: pins every other digit still while one lifts
- **Ring bay**: holds six cards in a triangle of circles and totals every side

## Textbook (this topic)
**Pages:** 20, 21, 22
**Key terms:** order, grouping, successor, addend
Order and grouping can be changed without changing the sum, and adding zero, one, ten, a hundred or a thousand has a shortcut. The sum is the same whichever order the addends are written in. That means you can always start with whichever one is easier to work with. When there are three or more addends, the sum stays the same however you group them. So you may add any two of them first and bring in the rest afterwards, and it is worth choosing the pair that makes a round number. Adding zero to any number leaves it exactly as it was. Adding one gives the number that comes straight after it, which is called its successor. Adding ten raises the tens digit by one. Adding a hundred raises the hundreds digit. Adding a thousand raises the thousands digit. The trick is knowing which place you are in, not which shape the number has.
