## What it is
**Description:** Draw a circle with a pair of compasses, name its centre, radius, chords and diameter, and turn a radius into a diameter and back.
**Why:** Draw a circle with a pair of compasses, name what joins its centre and its edge, and find out why a diameter is two radii long.
**Look:** A pair of compasses standing on a sheet of paper, a parts tray beside it, and a chord slider and a radius bar along the foot of the canvas.
**Context:** Grade 4, and the chapter's longest run of exercises. An adult sees a circle being drawn and its parts named. What is actually being settled is that every point of a circle is the same distance from its centre, which is why a pair of compasses works at all, and that a diameter is simply two radii end to end. The page's summary box says every chord is not a diameter, meaning that not every chord is a diameter; as printed it says no chord is one, which contradicts the diameter drawn beside it.
**For the child:** Fix the metal point. Stretch the pencil arm and turn it round. Name the centre. A radius starts at the centre. A chord joins two points on the circle. A diameter is a chord through the centre.
**Teaching notes:** Draw a circle on the ground first, with a string held tight by a child standing at the centre. The string is a radius, and walking round with it pulled tight is the whole reason every point is the same distance away. The compass steps on the page are fix the point, stretch the arm, turn the pencil; say them in that order. The summary box says every diameter is a chord, but every chord is not a diameter. It means not every chord is a diameter, and as printed it says the opposite of its own first half, so say the corrected form and never the printed one. The labelled circle on the page calls one segment a diameter in one figure and a radius in the next; keep one name for one thing in every figure drawn here. One printed blank, a circle can have many, has three right answers, radii, chords and diameters, so accept any of them. The Math Lab stacks four circles on one centre, which is where the word concentric comes from. Its last step suggests pencils as darts, and this plan leaves that step out: stick the rings on the board and name them instead.
**Placement:** Explore

## Phases (what each mount shows and does; the words are in tool.tsx)

### 1 · The same distance all the way round
**Screen:** a pair of compasses on a blank sheet with its metal point raised
**Does:** fixes the metal point, stretches the pencil arm, turns it round, then stretches wider and turns again
**Win:** the circle closes, the string from the centre holds one length all the way round, and a wider circle forms about the same centre
**Feels:** the string never changes length, so every point of the curve is the same distance from the centre
**Goal:** every point of a circle is the same distance from its centre, and the length of the boundary is the circumference
**Observe:** The string from the centre to the pencil held one length all the way round.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- takes the opening of the compass arms as the distance right across the circle, and draws a circle twice as wide as the one asked for. → the circle is drawn at the child's opening and a ruler is laid across it through the centre dot: it reads twice the opening. The string from the centre to the pencil is drawn at the same time, reading the opening itself, and both readings stay beside the child's answer, nothing marked.
- lets the metal point slide while turning, so the curve does not close up where it began. → the curve is drawn as the child turned it, with the amber string drawn from wherever the metal point stood at each moment, changing length as it goes, and the curve misses its own beginning. The metal point is then pinned and the turn made again, the string holding one length all the way round, and the circle closes exactly. Both curves stay drawn.
**Misconception to break:** Thinking the opening of the arms is the distance right across the circle rather than from its centre.

### 2 · Radius and chord
**Screen:** a circle with its centre named and the parts tray waiting
**Does:** draws one segment from the centre to the circle and one between two points on the circle, and names each
**Win:** the tray names the radius and the chord, and every other radius drawn lights at the same length
**Feels:** a radius always has one end on the centre dot, and a chord never needs it
**Goal:** a radius joins the centre to the circle and a chord joins two points on the circle
**Observe:** Every radius drawn lit at the same length, and the chords did not.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- calls a segment joining two points on the circle a radius, because it lies inside the circle. → the parts tray lights the centre dot and then the two ends of the child's segment: both glow on the curve and neither is at the centre, and the tray writes chord under it. A true radius is drawn from the centre dot beside it, and the child's name stays in the slot, nothing marked.
- draws a radius from a point near the middle of the circle rather than from the centre dot itself. → the child's segment is measured, and so is a radius drawn from the centre dot to the same place on the curve; the two lengths differ. Three more radii are drawn from the dot and all light at one length, which the child's segment does not match, and every segment stays drawn.
**Misconception to break:** Calling any segment inside a circle a radius, whether or not it starts at the centre.

### 3 · The chord through the centre
**Screen:** a circle with a chord near its edge and the chord slider waiting
**Does:** slides the chord across the circle, reading its length, and stops where it passes through the centre
**Win:** the chord lights as a diameter as it crosses the centre, and its length reading peaks there
**Feels:** the chord grows as it slides towards the centre, and is longest exactly there, where it becomes a diameter
**Goal:** a chord through the centre is a diameter and the longest chord, and not every chord is a diameter
**Observe:** The chord lit as a diameter only as it crossed the centre, and its length was greatest there.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- calls a long chord that just misses the centre a diameter. → the slider holds the child's chord where it is, with its length in the box, then slides it the last small step until it passes through the centre dot. It lights as a diameter only there, and its length climbs a little higher. Slid back, it loses the light and its length drops, and the child's chord stays drawn in sky blue beside the diameter, nothing marked.
- decides that no chord can be a diameter, because a diameter has a name of its own. → the diameter is drawn through the centre dot and its two ends light on the curve, where every chord has its ends, and the parts tray writes chord under it as well as diameter. A chord that misses the centre is drawn beside it with the one name only, and the rule is written under both: every diameter is a chord, and not every chord is a diameter.
**Misconception to break:** Thinking a chord and a diameter are different kinds of thing, so that a diameter cannot be a chord.

### 4 · Two radii make a diameter
**Screen:** a circle with one radius drawn and measured, and the radius bar waiting
**Does:** lays a second radius end to end with the first through the centre, then uses the slot both ways
**Win:** the two radii span the circle as a diameter, and the slot doubles the radius and halves the diameter back
**Feels:** a diameter is just a radius on each side of the centre, laid end to end
**Goal:** a diameter is two radii end to end, so it is twice the radius, and the radius is half the diameter
**Observe:** Two radii laid end to end through the centre reached exactly across the circle.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- halves the radius instead of doubling it, writing three for the diameter of a circle of radius six. → the radius bar lays a second radius of six end to end with the first through the centre, and together they reach from the circle across to the circle, twelve. The child's three is drawn as a bar from the centre, reaching only half way to the curve, and both stay drawn, nothing marked.
- doubles a diameter when the radius is asked for, giving a plate twenty-four centimetres across a radius of forty-eight. → the plate's diameter is laid across it through the centre and the slot halves it into two radii that meet at the centre dot, twelve each. The child's forty-eight is drawn as a bar from the centre, running far past the rim, and both stay drawn, nothing marked.
**Misconception to break:** Halving the radius instead of doubling it, and giving three centimetres.

## Delight
The third phase, the moment the sliding chord reaches the centre dot. The sky blue chord has been growing longer as it slides, its length climbing in the box, and as it touches the dot it turns deep blue and the word diameter lights under it; slide it one step on and it turns sky blue again and starts to shrink. The length in the box is at its greatest exactly while the chord is lit. It can be slid back and forth across the dot as often as a finger likes, nothing is marked, and the voice says nothing at all while it happens.

**General miss line:** Put a finger on the centre dot, and follow the segment from there.

## Answer key
Every point of a circle is the same distance from a fixed point, its centre, and a circle is named by its centre. The length of its boundary is its circumference. A segment joining the centre to a point on the circle is a radius, and every radius of one circle has the same length. A segment joining two points on the circle is a chord, and chords can have different lengths. A chord through the centre is a diameter, every diameter of one circle has the same length, and the diameter is the longest chord. Every diameter is a chord, but not every chord is a diameter. A diameter is a radius on each side of the centre, so the diameter is twice the radius and the radius is half the diameter. A circle has many radii, chords and diameters but only one centre. Circles with the same centre and different radii are concentric circles.

## Student controls
- **Metal point**: fixes the centre where it is pressed into the paper
- **Pencil arm**: stretches to a length read off the ruler along the arm
- **Turn**: draws the circle by moving the pencil arm round the fixed point
- **Centre string**: shows the distance from the centre to the pencil staying the same all the way round
- **Parts tray**: names a drawn segment as a radius or a chord
- **Chord slider**: moves a chord across the circle, reading its length, and lights it as a diameter at the centre
- **Radius bar**: lays two radii end to end through the centre
- **Double and halve slot**: turns a radius into a diameter and a diameter into a radius
