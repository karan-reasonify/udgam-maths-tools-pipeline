## What it is
**Description:** Name an angle by its vertex, sort it against a square corner, name the smaller angle between clock hands, and find a place on a grid by its row and column.
**Why:** Name an angle and sort it against a square corner, read the smaller angle between clock hands, and find a place on a grid by its row and its column.
**Look:** Hinged arms and a clock face on the left of the canvas, a square-corner card between them, and a grid of pictures with numbered rows and columns on the right.
**Context:** Grade 4, the closing topic, which gathers two sections the page runs together. An adult sees angles and a grid of pictures. What is actually being settled is two ways of saying exactly where something is. For angles, the page gives obtuse no upper limit, and clock hands always make two angles at once, so this tool always names the smaller one. For the grid, the page's section is headed MAP and promises position, direction and distance, but it never shows a map; what it shows is a grid read by rows and columns, and the one sentence that uses them says the pomegranate is three columns below the strawberry, when the two are three rows apart in one column. This tool teaches position on a grid by row and column, and nothing a map would add.
**For the child:** Name an angle with the meeting point in the middle. Lay the square corner in to sort it. On a clock, name the smaller angle. On the grid, moving down changes the row. Moving across changes the column.
**Teaching notes:** Make a square corner by folding a sheet of paper in half and then in half again, and use it to sort every angle in the room before the tool is opened. An angle smaller than it is acute and one larger is obtuse. The page gives obtuse no upper limit, and clock hands always make two angles, so a child who counts round the long way at ten o'clock will call a narrow angle obtuse. Always name the smaller angle between the hands, and lay the paper corner against it. The page heads the next section MAP and never shows one. It shows a grid of fruit read by rows and columns, and then says the pomegranate is three columns below the strawberry; the two are in the same column, three rows apart. Say rows every time a move goes down. The grid's model question can also be answered right by a child who miscounts, because the same fruit sits twice in that row, so every grid here holds each picture once. Do not add directions, distances or scales the page never teaches. The chessboard in the last exercise counts its rows up from the bottom, so say which end row one is before any move is made.

## Phases (what each mount shows and does; the words are in tool.tsx)

### 1 · Two arms and a vertex
**Screen:** two hinged arms with a letter at each end and at the point where they meet
**Does:** opens the arms, names the arms and the vertex, and builds the angle's name
**Win:** the name settles with the meeting letter in the middle and both arms lit from it
**Feels:** the letter where the arms meet sits in the middle of a three-letter name, or stands alone
**Goal:** an angle is two arms from one vertex, named with the vertex in the middle
**Observe:** The name settled only with the meeting letter in the middle.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- names the angle with the vertex first, writing angle BAC for the angle at B. → the name builder draws the angle the child's name describes, with its corner at A and its arms running to B and to C, shaded sky blue beside the angle at B, a different corner. The builder then writes the name with the meeting letter in the middle, and both names stay in the slot.
- takes the point at the end of one arm for the vertex, because it carries a letter like the rest. → the arms are traced from the child's point, and only one arm runs from it; from the meeting point both arms run out, and they light from there together. The meeting point takes its vermilion dot with the word vertex beside it, and the child's answer stays in the slot.
**Misconception to break:** Naming it angle BAC, with the vertex first, which names a different angle.

### 2 · Right, acute and obtuse
**Screen:** the hinged arms opened to an angle, with the square-corner card beside them
**Does:** lays the card into the angle, reads how it fits, and sorts the angle
**Win:** the card settles in the angle and its kind lands beside it: right, acute or obtuse
**Feels:** the square corner is the ruler for angles: it fits, it holds the arms inside, or it leaves a space
**Goal:** a right angle is a square corner, an acute angle is smaller and an obtuse angle is larger
**Observe:** The square-corner card fitted, held the arms inside it, or left a space, and that sorted every angle.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- calls an angle bigger than a right angle acute, because it is drawn small or its arms are short. → the square-corner card is laid into the angle with one edge along an arm, and a space opens between the card's other edge and the second arm, lit. The kind lands beside the angle as obtuse, and the child's answer stays in the slot.
- calls a narrow angle obtuse because its arms are drawn long. → the arms are made longer and then shorter while the opening between them stays exactly the same, and the card sits over the angle each time with both arms inside its edges. The kind stays acute throughout, and the child's answer stays in the slot.
**Misconception to break:** Calling a narrow angle obtuse because its arms are drawn long.

### 3 · The angle between clock hands
**Screen:** a clock face with its hands set to an hour and both angles shaded
**Does:** counts the hours between the hands each way round, picks the smaller angle, and sorts it
**Win:** the smaller angle is outlined, the square-corner card is laid against it, and its kind lands
**Feels:** the hands always make two angles, and the one to name is the gap between them the short way round
**Goal:** clock hands make two angles, and the one to name is the smaller
**Observe:** At every hour the hands made two angles, and only the smaller was outlined.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- names the larger angle, counting the long way round, and calls the angle at ten o'clock obtuse. → both angles between the hands are shaded, the larger in sky blue, and the hours are counted round each way, ten one way and two the other. The smaller angle is outlined and the square-corner card is laid against it: its three hours reach past the two, so the angle sits inside the card. The child's answer stays in the slot.
- counts the numbers the angle covers instead of the gaps between them, making two o'clock three hours wide and so a right angle. → the hour counter steps the gaps one at a time from one hand to the other, twelve to one and one to two, lighting each gap, and stops at two. The square-corner card is laid from the minute hand and reaches past the hour hand to three, and the child's answer stays in the slot.
**Misconception to break:** Counting from twelve round to ten, finding ten hours, and calling the angle obtuse.

### 4 · Rows across, columns down
**Screen:** the picture grid with its rows numbered down the left and its columns across the top, and the marker at a start
**Does:** moves the marker by rows and columns as told and names the picture it reaches
**Win:** the marker lands, its row lights across and its column lights down, and the picture it sits on is named
**Feels:** moving down, the row number changes and the column number stays, and moving across it is the other way round
**Goal:** a place on a grid is given by its row and its column, and moving down changes the row
**Observe:** A move down lit a new row across the grid and kept the same column lit.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- calls a move straight down a move of so many columns, though the column never changes. → the marker is moved down one step at a time from its start, and at each step a new row lights across the grid while its column stays lit down it, unchanged. The row number at the left edge climbs by one at each step, the column number at the top stays the same, and the child's answer stays in the slot beside them.
- names the picture that looks right at a glance without counting its row and column, so a miscount can still land on the right name. → the marker is set on the child's picture where it sits, and its row and column light at the edges beside the row and column the question gave; where they differ, both cells ring. The slot then asks for the row and the column as well as the picture, so an answer names the place and not only the thing.
**Misconception to break:** Counting the starting square as the first step, or moving down by columns, and landing in the wrong place.

## Delight
The fourth phase, the moment the marker moves straight down. Its whole row slides down with it, lighting a fresh line of pictures across the grid, while the lit column it travels along stays exactly where it was, and the row number at the left edge ticks on by one at each step with the column number at the top never changing. Move it across and the opposite happens. It can be walked round the grid as often as a finger likes, nothing is marked, and the voice says nothing at all while it happens.

**General miss line:** Lay the square corner in again, count the hours again, or walk the marker one step at a time.

## Answer key
An angle is formed where two rays meet. The rays are its arms and the point they share is its vertex, and the angle is named by three letters with the vertex in the middle, or by the vertex alone. A right angle is a square corner. An angle smaller than a right angle is acute, and one larger than a right angle is obtuse. Clock hands make two angles at once, and the one to name is the smaller: at three o'clock and at nine o'clock it is a right angle, when the hands are fewer than three hours apart it is acute, and when they are four or five hours apart it is obtuse. A place on a grid is given by its row and its column. Rows run across and columns run down, so moving down or up changes the row and keeps the column, and moving across changes the column and keeps the row.

## Student controls
- **Hinged arms**: open and close an angle by dragging either arm
- **Name builder**: builds the angle's name with the meeting letter in the middle
- **Square-corner card**: lays a right angle into any angle to compare
- **Clock hands**: set the hour and minute hands to point at the numbers
- **Two-angle shade**: shades both angles between the hands and outlines the smaller
- **Hour counter**: counts the hours between the hands each way round
- **Grid marker**: moves by rows down or up and by columns across
- **Row and column lights**: light the marker's whole row across and whole column down
