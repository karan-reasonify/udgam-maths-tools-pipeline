## What it is
**Description:** Trace a figure from a starting flag, find out whether it closes, and test whether a closed figure is a polygon by its straight sides.
**Why:** Trace a figure from a flag without lifting, find out whether it brings you back, and sort the closed figures with straight sides from the rest.
**Look:** A sheet with one outline drawn on it, a flag and a tracing finger beside it, and a polygon gate with a ruler standing at the right.
**Context:** Grade 4, and the figures this topic sorts are the ones every later topic measures. An adult sees shapes being sorted into piles. What is actually being settled is that closed is a test, done by tracing, and not a look: a figure with a gap too small to notice is still open. The printed definition of a polygon leaves the word closed out, so the open triangle and the open square printed a page earlier fit it as written. This tool will not test a figure for straight sides until its trace has come home.
**For the child:** Put the flag on the edge of the figure. Trace all the way round without lifting. If you come back to the flag, it is closed. A closed figure with straight sides only is a polygon. Count its sides and its corners.
**Teaching notes:** Lay a skipping rope on the floor before the tool is opened and have a child walk it from a chalk mark. A figure is closed when the walker comes home, and that is a test anyone can run, so run it rather than looking. Leave a gap in the rope the width of a shoe and let the class argue before the walk settles it. The page's polygon definition speaks of figures made up of only straight segments and leaves out the word closed. Read as printed, the open triangle and the open square on the page before would be polygons. Say the whole definition every time: a polygon is a closed figure made of straight line segments only. The exercise then asks for the number of vertices of two figures before the chapter has said what a vertex is, so say it here: a vertex is a corner, where two sides meet, and a polygon has as many of them as it has sides.

## Phases (what each mount shows and does; the words are in tool.tsx)

### 1 · Ending where you began
**Screen:** a figure on the sheet with the flag waiting in its tray
**Does:** plants the flag on the outline and runs the tracer round without lifting
**Win:** the tracer comes home to the flag and the inside floods with colour
**Feels:** the figure is closed when the trace comes home, not when it looks finished
**Goal:** a closed figure brings a tracing finger back to where it started, and an open one does not
**Observe:** The inside flooded only after the tracer came back to the flag.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- decides a figure is closed because it looks round and whole, without tracing it. → the flag is planted where the child left it and the tracer runs the outline at walking pace. This figure curls round without meeting itself, so the tracer stops at a free end, which glows, and nothing floods. The trail stays drawn from the flag to the free end, and the child's answer stays in the slot beside it, nothing marked.
- says an S is closed because the trace went a long way round before it stopped. → the trail is replayed from the flag at the top end, and the flag and the free end at the bottom both glow, far apart, with a near-black bracket spanning the space between them. The tracer waits at the bottom end with nothing flooded, and the child's answer stays in the slot.
**Misconception to break:** Deciding by the look of a figure instead of tracing it from a starting point.

### 2 · One gap makes it open
**Screen:** a figure that looks shut, with one break too small to see at first
**Does:** traces it, finds where the tracer stops, and passes the gap finder over that place
**Win:** the gap finder opens the break wide, the free end glows, and nothing floods
**Feels:** however small the gap, the tracer cannot step across it
**Goal:** any gap, however small, makes a figure open
**Observe:** A break too thin to see stopped the tracer, and nothing flooded.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- calls a figure closed because its gap is too small to notice. → the tracer runs from the flag and stops at the break, where the free end glows. The gap finder then passes over that place and opens the hairline into a plain gap with an end point on each side. The tracer waits at the gap with nothing flooded, and the child's answer stays in the slot.
- plants the flag at one side of the gap, traces round to the other side and calls that coming home, because the tracer ends up right beside where it began. → the flag and the tracer's finishing place are both lit, and the gap finder passes over the space between them and opens it into a plain gap. The tracer is asked to step across and cannot: its trail stops at one end point, the flag stands at the other, and nothing floods. The child's answer stays in the slot, nothing marked.
**Misconception to break:** Calling a figure closed because the gap is too small to notice at a glance.

### 3 · Closed and straight-sided
**Screen:** three closed figures waiting at the polygon gate, one of them with a curved part
**Does:** sends each figure through the gate and watches the ruler test each of its parts
**Win:** the ruler rings along every side of the polygon and leaves daylight under the curved part of the other
**Feels:** the gate asks two questions in order, and a figure has to pass both
**Goal:** a polygon is a closed figure made of straight line segments only
**Observe:** The gate turned an open triangle away before testing its sides, and turned away a closed figure with one curved part after testing them.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- calls an open triangle a polygon, because all three of its sides are straight. → the open triangle is dragged to the gate and carried back to the sheet before the straightedge touches it, its trail still stopping at the free end, which glows. The gate's first question, closed, lights above its second, and the child's answer stays in the slot. Nothing is marked.
- counts a half circle as a polygon because it has one straight side. → the half circle passes the first question, its trace coming home and its inside flooding, and the straightedge is laid along each part in turn. It rings along the straight side and leaves a sliver of sky blue daylight under the curved part, which stays lit, and the figure is turned back from the gate with the child's answer still in the slot.
**Misconception to break:** Counting a half circle as a polygon because one of its sides is straight.

### 4 · Counting sides and corners
**Screen:** a polygon through the gate with its sides and corners not yet counted
**Does:** taps each side and each corner, then picks the name card
**Win:** the two counters stop at the same number and the name card settles under the figure
**Feels:** going round a polygon, sides and corners take turns, so neither count can get ahead of the other
**Goal:** a polygon has as many corners as sides, and each corner, where two sides meet, is a vertex
**Observe:** The side counter and the corner counter stopped at the same number on every polygon.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- counts only the corners that look sharp and misses one where two sides meet at a wide angle. → the straightedge is laid along each side in turn, and at the wide corner it cannot lie flat along both sides at once: it rings along one side, then turns to ring along the next. A vermilion dot drops at the turn as a vertex, the corner counter climbs to match the side counter, and the child's count stays in the slot beside them.
- thinks only squares and rectangles are quadrilaterals, and calls a four-sided figure with no square corners something else. → the four sides of the slanting figure are tapped and counted, and a vertex dot drops at each of its four corners, where two sides meet. The name card turns to quadrilateral, which counts sides and nothing else, and a square and a rectangle are set beside it under the same card. The child's name stays in the slot, nothing marked.
**Misconception to break:** Counting only the corners that look sharp, or thinking only squares and rectangles are quadrilaterals.

## Delight
The second phase, the moment the gap finder passes over the place where the tracer stopped. The glass swells the hairline break until it is a plain gap with a glowing end point on each side, the tracer's trail waiting at one of them and the flag at the other, and a thin tide of green that had started to creep in from the middle runs out through the gap and away. Pass the glass off and the break shrinks back to a hairline, but both end points keep glowing, so the figure stays open however shut it looks again. It can be done as often as a finger likes, nothing is marked, and the voice says nothing at all while it happens.

**General miss line:** Put the flag back and trace again, slowly, without lifting.

## Answer key
A figure that starts and ends at the same place is closed, and one that starts at one place and ends at another is open. Tracing from any point without lifting settles it: a closed figure brings the tracer back, and any gap, however small, stops it. A polygon is a closed figure made of straight line segments only, so a figure with a gap is not a polygon and neither is a closed figure with any curved part. A polygon is named by its number of sides; a three-sided one is a triangle and a four-sided one is a quadrilateral. Each corner, where two sides meet, is a vertex, and a polygon has as many vertices as sides.

## Student controls
- **Flag**: plants the start anywhere on the outline
- **Tracer**: runs along the outline from the flag without lifting
- **Flood**: fills the inside of a figure the tracer brings home
- **Gap finder**: magnifies any break in an outline
- **Polygon gate**: takes a closed figure only and tests each of its parts
- **Straight test**: lays a ruler along one part, ringing if it lies flat
- **Side and corner counter**: counts the sides and dots each corner where two sides meet
- **Name card**: offers triangle and quadrilateral for three and four sides
