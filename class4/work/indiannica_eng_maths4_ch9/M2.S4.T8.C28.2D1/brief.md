## What it is
**Description:** Count a solid's faces, edges and vertices, tell a prism from a pyramid, fold a flat net into a solid, and look at a box from the top, the front and the side.
**Why:** Count what a solid is made of, sort it by its bases, fold one up from flat, and look at it from three sides.
**Look:** A solid on a turntable with three counters at the top left of the canvas, a net bench below it, and a view window with three small cameras at the right.
**Context:** Grade 4, and the chapter's only work in three dimensions. An adult sees shapes being counted and folded. What is actually being settled is what a face, an edge and a vertex are, well enough to count them on a solid the chapter's table leaves out, which is what the page's find-out task asks for. The printed pages need care in three places: they say a pyramid's faces are all triangular beside a square pyramid with a square base, they call a square prism also known as a cube without saying its height must match its base, and they draw a box's side view taller than its front view.
**For the child:** Spin the solid and count its faces. Then count its edges and its corners. Find its bases. Fold the net and see whether it closes. Look at the solid from the top, the front and the side.
**Teaching notes:** Bring a real box and count it before the tool is opened, faces first, then edges, then corners, touching each one. The table on the page does the counting for six solids, and the find-out task above the nets asks for more, so count a solid the table leaves out, such as a pyramid with a five-sided base. Three printed sentences need care. The page says a pyramid's faces are all triangular while its own table gives a square pyramid a square base: say the faces other than the base are triangles. It says a square prism is also known as a cube, which is true only when its height equals the side of its base: say that condition every time. And it defines a vertex as a point where two edges meet, then gives a cone one edge and one vertex: count the cone's tip as its one vertex and do not claim edges meet there. The two tables on that page print edges and vertices in different orders, so read the column heading and not the position. The three views of a box are printed with the side view taller than the front view, though both show the same height; the view window draws them at one scale.

## Phases (what each mount shows and does; the words are in tool.tsx)

### 1 · Faces, edges and vertices
**Screen:** a box on the turntable with its three counters empty
**Does:** spins the solid, taps every face, traces every edge and dots every corner
**Win:** the counts stop at six, twelve and eight, with every part of the box lit
**Feels:** faces, edges and corners are three different counts, and each one is kept in its own place
**Goal:** a face is a surface, an edge is where two faces meet, and a vertex is a corner, each counted on its own
**Observe:** The edge count reached twelve only after the turntable brought the hidden edges round.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- swaps the edge and vertex counts, writing eight edges and twelve vertices for a cube. → the counts are run again one at a time with their headings lit: every edge traced lights amber along its length and the edge count climbs to twelve, then every corner takes a vermilion dot and the vertex count climbs to eight. The child's two numbers stay in the slot beside the headings, nothing marked.
- gives the cone no vertex, because a vertex was defined as a point where two edges meet and a cone has only one edge. → the cone turns on the turntable and its tip is tapped: a vermilion dot drops there and the vertex count takes one, while the edge count, traced round the rim, stays at one. The tip is lit as the cone's corner, where its curved face comes to a point with no edges meeting, and the child's answer stays in the slot.
**Misconception to break:** Counting only the edges that can be seen from one side, and getting nine instead of twelve.

### 2 · Prisms and pyramids
**Screen:** a triangular prism and a square pyramid on the sorting bench with their bases dim
**Does:** lights the bases of each solid, counts them, and sets each under prism or pyramid
**Win:** the prism shows two matching bases with rectangles between, and the pyramid one square base with four triangles meeting at its tip
**Feels:** a prism has two matching ends, a pyramid has one base and a point, and the base keeps whatever shape it has
**Goal:** a prism has two matching bases joined by rectangles, and a pyramid has one base with triangles rising to a point
**Observe:** The square pyramid lit one square face and four triangles.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- says every face of a square pyramid is a triangle, and counts only the four that rise to the tip. → the base light turns the pyramid over and lights its base green: a square, with four sides and four corners. The face count climbs to five, the four triangles and the square, and the child's four stays in the slot beside it.
- calls a tall square prism a cube because its two ends are squares. → the size strip is laid up the prism's side and along one edge of its square end, and the two lengths stand side by side, the height plainly longer. The prism is shrunk down until the two lengths match, and only then do all six faces light as squares. The child's answer stays in the slot.
**Misconception to break:** Saying every face of a pyramid is a triangle and forgetting its square base.

### 3 · A net folds into a solid
**Screen:** six squares joined as one flat piece on the net bench
**Does:** folds the net up along its edges and watches whether every edge meets
**Win:** the net closes into a cube with every edge met, or shows the two faces that collide and the side left open
**Feels:** the count of squares is not enough; where each square hangs decides whether it closes
**Goal:** a net is a flat shape that folds along its edges into a solid, and some arrangements of squares do not fold into a cube
**Observe:** Six squares in a row wrapped round and two faces landed in one place, while other arrangements of six closed into a cube.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- thinks a net of a cube must be shaped like a cross, and says a different arrangement of six squares cannot fold into one. → the child's arrangement, a row of four with one square above the first and one below the last, is laid on the bench and folded up one crease at a time: the row wraps round into four sides and the other two squares fold over as the top and the bottom, closing with every edge met. The cross is folded beside it and closes too, and both cubes stand side by side with the child's answer in the slot.
- expects six squares in a straight row to fold into a cube, because there are six squares and a cube has six faces. → the row is folded up one crease at a time: it wraps round, and the fifth and sixth squares land on the first and second, glowing sky blue where two faces sit in one place, while the top and the bottom of the box stay open. The child's answer stays in the slot, nothing marked.
**Misconception to break:** Thinking a net of a cube must be shaped like a cross, so that any other arrangement cannot fold.

### 4 · Top, front and side
**Screen:** a box on the turntable with three cameras round it and the view window empty
**Does:** opens each camera in turn and matches the measurements the views share
**Win:** the three views settle at one scale, the front and side views standing the same height
**Feels:** each camera sees one side of the same box, so the views share its measurements
**Goal:** a solid looks different from the top, the front and the side, and every view of one solid shares its measurements
**Observe:** The front and side views of the box stood level against the same guide line.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- draws the side view of a box taller than its front view. → the size strip holds the box's height, and the front and side views are both drawn against it at one scale, with level guide lines running from the top of one to the top of the other. The child's taller side view stands beside them, its top above the guide line, and both are kept.
- gives an upright cylinder a rectangle as its top view. → the top camera is opened over the upright cylinder, and its view is drawn as a plain circle with no dot, the front camera's rectangle drawn beside it. The view window labels each drawing with the camera that saw it, and the child's rectangle stays in the slot.
**Misconception to break:** Drawing the side view taller or shorter than the front view, as though the box changed height.

## Delight
The third phase, the moment the last crease of a good net is folded. The squares have been rising one at a time along their edges, and as the last one swings up to close the top, every edge meets its partner with a soft click, the seams disappear, and the finished cube drops onto the turntable and turns once. Unfold it and the six squares lie flat again exactly where they began. It can be folded and unfolded as often as a finger likes, nothing is marked, and the voice says nothing at all while it happens.

**General miss line:** Spin it slowly and count again, one kind of part at a time.

## Answer key
A solid is a three-dimensional object that takes up space. A face is a surface of a solid, flat or curved; an edge is where two faces meet; and a vertex is a corner. A cube and a cuboid each have six faces, twelve edges and eight vertices. A cylinder has two flat faces, one curved face and two edges; a cone has one flat face, one curved face, one edge and one vertex, its tip; a sphere has one curved face. A prism has two matching bases and rectangles for its other faces, and it is named by the shape of its bases. A pyramid has one base, and the faces other than the base are triangles that meet at a point; a square pyramid has a square base. A square prism is a cube only when its height equals the side of its square base. A net is a flat shape that folds along its edges into a solid, and one solid can have several nets. A solid looks different from the top, the front and the side, and views of one solid share its measurements: the front and side views of a box are the same height.

## Student controls
- **Turntable**: spins the solid to show every side
- **Part counters**: light each face, edge or corner tapped and keep the three counts apart
- **Base light**: lights the base, or both bases, of a solid
- **Sorting bench**: sets a solid under prism or pyramid by its bases and other faces
- **Net bench**: lays a flat net on the table
- **Fold up**: folds the net along its edges into a solid, or shows where it fails
- **View window**: draws the solid from the top, the front and the side
- **Size strip**: holds the measurements every view of one solid shares
