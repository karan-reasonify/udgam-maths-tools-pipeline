## What it is
**Description:** Cover a shape with equal squares and count them, count a board by its rows, and set a shape's area beside its perimeter.
**Why:** Cover a shape with equal squares and count the space inside it, then set that count beside the length of the edge round it.
**Look:** A shape on a squared sheet, a tray of square tiles beside it, a counter at the top left of the canvas, and a compare bay at the right.
**Context:** Grade 4, and the first time the chapter measures a space rather than a length. An adult sees squares being counted, which a child can do on squared paper. What is actually being settled is the unit: area counts squares, so it is written in square units, and a square one centimetre on every side gives a square centimetre. The page's own squared figures in the perimeter exercise were built so that no shape's perimeter equals its area, and a child who counts squares when asked for the edge gives a different number, which is why the two counts stand side by side here.
**For the child:** Cover the shape with squares, edge to edge. Leave no gaps and put none on top of another. Count the squares. Write the area in square units. Count by rows when you can.
**Teaching notes:** Cover a real book with paper squares before the tool is opened, edge to edge, and count them. Then cover it again with squares of a bigger size, and let the class find that the count changed while the book did not; the squares must be one size for the count to mean anything. The page's tabletop is four rows of seven, twenty-eight squares, and its shaded shape is two rows of four, eight square centimetres; read both off together before anything is tiled here. Count by rows as early as possible. A chessboard counted one square at a time loses its place, and eight rows of eight does not. Keep the unit in every answer. Area is in square units or square centimetres because it counts squares, and the moment a child writes centimetres for an area, the compare bay shows that centimetres belong to the edge. The squared figures in the perimeter exercise are the best test of the difference: for every one of them the perimeter and the area are different numbers, so a child who counts squares when asked for the edge is caught at once.

## Phases (what each mount shows and does; the words are in tool.tsx)

### 1 · Covering with equal squares
**Screen:** a shape on the sheet and a tray of equal square tiles beside it
**Does:** drags tiles onto the shape until it is covered, then reads the count
**Win:** the last tile snaps in, the shape is covered edge to edge, and the count stands as its area in square units
**Feels:** the squares have to meet edge to edge and stay inside the edge, or the count means nothing
**Goal:** area is the space inside a boundary, counted in equal squares that leave no gaps and do not overlap
**Observe:** A tile laid over a gap or on another tile sprang back and was never counted.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- leaves gaps between the tiles or lays one tile over another, and counts whatever is on the shape. → the tile over the gap or on top of another springs back to the tray with the gap or the overlap lit where it sat, and the count does not take it. The tiles already laid stay where they are, the space still bare is drawn in outline so the covering can be finished edge to edge, and the child's count stays in the slot.
- counts every square of the sheet the shape sits on, instead of only the squares inside its boundary. → the tiles inside the boundary glow green and the squares of the sheet outside it stay the plain panel colour, with the deep blue boundary lit between them. The tiles are counted again one row at a time, only inside the boundary, and the child's larger number stays in the slot beside the new count.
**Misconception to break:** Counting tiles that overlap or leaving gaps, or writing the answer with no unit.

### 2 · Square centimetres
**Screen:** a shaded shape on a one-centimetre grid with the unit card face down
**Does:** counts the shaded squares, then turns the unit card and writes the area with its unit
**Win:** the unit card turns to square centimetres and settles beside the count
**Feels:** the unit is a square, so the answer counts squares and says so
**Goal:** a square one centimetre on every side gives a square centimetre, the unit of area
**Observe:** The unit card turned over to show one square with each side a centimetre.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- writes the area as seven centimetres, in a unit that measures a length. → the unit card turns over under the count and shows one of the grid's squares lifted out, a centimetre along each side, labelled one square centimetre. A centimetre is laid beside it as an amber bar along one side only, and the seven squares are counted again, each lifting a moment as the card's square does. The child's answer stays in the slot with the unit keys lit.
- gives the area as a bare number with no unit at all. → the unit tag under the slot stays open and lit, and the same count is shown twice beside the shape, once as squares of this grid and once as squares of a grid twice as wide, the second drawing plainly bigger. The bare number fits both drawings, and the unit card names which squares were counted.
**Misconception to break:** Writing the answer as seven centimetres, which is a length and not an area.

### 3 · Rows instead of one by one
**Screen:** a rectangle on the sheet with one row of tiles laid and the rows control waiting
**Does:** counts the first row, then copies it down and counts the rows
**Win:** the rows fill down one at a time and the total lands as the rows times the row
**Feels:** one row counted is every row counted, once you know how many rows there are
**Goal:** a shape of equal rows is counted as the rows times the squares in each
**Observe:** The rows control copied one row down, and the total grew by a whole row at each landing.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- counts one row and stops, giving seven for a tabletop covered in four rows of seven. → the rows control copies the first row down one at a time, each new row lighting as it lands and the count of rows climbing beside it, until four rows stand. The total is written beside each row as it lands, seven, fourteen, twenty-one and twenty-eight, and the child's seven stays in the slot beside the first row.
- adds the number of rows to the squares in one row, writing thirteen for six rows of seven. → the rows fill down one at a time with the total beside each: seven, fourteen, twenty-one, twenty-eight, thirty-five and forty-two. The child's thirteen is laid out in tiles too, one row of seven with six more tiles standing below its first square, and the two coverings stand side by side, the thirteen leaving most of the board bare.
**Misconception to break:** Adding six and seven to get thirteen instead of counting six rows of seven.

### 4 · Area and perimeter are different
**Screen:** a row of squares in the compare bay with both counts empty
**Does:** counts the squares inside with the tiles, then sends the edge walker round the outside
**Win:** the two counts stand side by side, one in square centimetres and one in centimetres, and they differ
**Feels:** the squares inside and the edges round them are two counts of one shape, and they do not have to agree
**Goal:** area counts the squares inside and perimeter measures the edge round them, and the two usually differ
**Observe:** For the row of squares and for the square block alike, the edge count and the square count came out different.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- counts the squares when asked for the perimeter, giving six for a row of six squares whose edge is fourteen. → the edge walker goes round the outside one square's edge at a time, each edge lighting amber as its count in the compare bay climbs to fourteen, while the tiles inside stay at six. The two counts stand side by side, one in square centimetres and one in centimetres, and the child's six stays in the slot under the edge count.
- expects the shape with the longer way round to have the bigger area, and picks a row of nine squares as bigger than a square three by three. → the tiles are counted inside both shapes and the two counts land level, nine and nine, while the edge walker's counts beside them come out at twenty and twelve. The compare bay stands all four numbers in its two columns, the areas equal and the perimeters not, and the child's answer stays in the slot.
**Misconception to break:** Giving the same number for both because both were counted on the same squares.

## Delight
The third phase, the moment the rows control is pressed. The first row lifts a finger's width, a copy of it slides down beneath and snaps in, then another, then another, each landing with a soft click and its row number written at its left, while the total beside the board jumps by a whole row at every landing. When the last row lands the shape is covered edge to edge and the total stands still. It can be run again as often as a finger likes, nothing is marked, and the voice says nothing at all while it happens.

**General miss line:** Lift the tiles and lay them again one row at a time, counting aloud.

## Answer key
Area is the amount of space inside the boundary of a shape, found by covering it with equal squares that meet edge to edge, with no gaps and no overlaps, and counting them. It is written in square units, and when each square is one centimetre on every side, in square centimetres. A shape made of equal rows can be counted by rows: the number of rows times the number of squares in each. Area and perimeter measure different things: the area counts the squares inside, and the perimeter measures the length of the edge round them, so the two numbers usually differ.

## Student controls
- **Tile tray**: holds equal square tiles to drag onto the shape
- **Snap**: sets each tile edge to edge with the one beside it
- **Gap and overlap light**: hands back a tile over a gap or on another tile, with the place lit
- **Tile counter**: counts the tiles at the top left of the canvas
- **Unit card**: reads square units, or square centimetres for one-centimetre squares
- **Rows control**: fills one row and copies it down, counting the rows and the squares in each
- **Edge walker**: goes round the outside one square edge at a time, counting
- **Compare bay**: sets the squares inside beside the edges round for one shape
