## What it is
**Description:** Build a table past ten one row at a time by adding, never by looking it up, then stand it beside another table and read the pattern.
**Why:** Build the tables past ten by adding rather than by remembering, and find the patterns that let you rebuild any row you forget.
**Look:** An empty table of ten rows with only its first row written in, a running count down the side and a rail below that holds two tables level.
**Context:** Grade 4, and the page prints six empty tables rather than six filled ones. An adult sees rote learning. What the page is actually doing is asking for the tables to be built by repeated addition, which is a different thing from being told them, and then asking for the patterns that fall out. Those patterns are the part that survives: a child who knows the table of twelve is the table of six doubled can rebuild any row they have forgotten.
**For the child:** Start at the number itself and add it again for every row. Do not look any row up. When your table is finished, stand it beside another one on the rail and read the two rows that sit level. Then open the even check and look at the last digit of every answer.
**Teaching notes:** The page prints six empty tables on purpose, and the purpose is lost the moment an answer is looked up. Keep the builder in adding mode and make every row come from the row above it. It is slow, and the slowness is the lesson. Once two tables are standing, open the compare rail and put the table of six beside the table of twelve, then the table of eight beside the table of sixteen. Do not name the pattern first. Ask what has happened to each row and wait for somebody to say doubled. The even check is the cheapest thing in this tool and the most used: a child who knows an even number cannot give an odd product can catch a slip without redoing any working. Finish on the steps counter. Work one product with the table of thirteen and the same product without it, and count the steps both ways, because the reason for learning a table past ten is not that it is printed but that it is shorter. The closing lab is this tool with the screen taken away: problem cards at one end of the room, answer cards at the other, and a clock running.

## Opening (first frame of every mount)
Every mount comes up already furnished for its phase with nothing in front of it. On the first phase an empty table of ten numbered rows stands on the canvas with only its own number written into the top row, and there is no lookup anywhere on the bench to fill the rest of it from. On the second two finished tables stand on the compare rail with their rows dead level and no pair lit yet. On the third a finished table stands with the even check open beneath it and two empty trays waiting below that. On the fourth one product is set out twice, once beside an open table past ten and once with its multiplier split into a ones row and a tens row, and neither route has been worked. The running count at the top left holds the rows filled against the rows the table is ruled for, and the answer slot and the pad stand empty down the right. One thing moves, once: the empty row under the number, or the first level pair, or the even tray, or the shorter of the two routes, gives one slow pulse while the voice asks for the next row. That pulse is the only invitation, and a finger on it is the whole way in.

## Phases (what each mount shows and does; the words are in tool.tsx)

### 1 · A table is repeated addition written down
**Screen:** an empty table of ten rows with the number itself in the first row and no lookup anywhere
**Does:** adds the number again for each row and watches the column fill downwards
**Win:** the table fills from the top and the running count keeps pace with the rows
**Feels:** slow, and every row arrives out of the one above it rather than out of memory
**Goal:** every row of a table is the row above with the same number added once more
**Observe:** Every row of the table was the row above it with the same number added, and the count down the side went up by one each time.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- adds the row count to the row above instead of the table's own number, so that the table of fourteen runs 14, then 16, then 19. → the row the child filled is emptied and the step is drawn again as an arrow off the row above it, with the table's own number written on the arrow itself, and the row count is moved out to the far side of the strip where it cannot be reached by accident. The child's row is redrawn faintly beside the rebuilt one so the two can be read together.
- asks for the rest of the table to be filled in at once, because the first few rows are already known by heart. → the rows the child already holds stay filled exactly as they were given, and the steps between them are drawn in afterwards, each arrow carrying the table's own number, so that the rows known by heart are seen to be the same additions. The tool then stops at the first row the child did not have, with only the arrow off the row above it offered.
**Misconception to break:** Adding the row number instead of the table's own number, so that 28 becomes 31.

### 2 · One table can be built from another
**Screen:** the compare rail holding two finished tables side by side with their rows level
**Does:** reads the two rows that sit level and says how the one on the right was made
**Win:** every pair of rows lights as a double and the rail names the pattern
**Feels:** a whole table arrives without a single row of it being added
**Goal:** the table of twelve is the table of six doubled, and sixteen is eight doubled
**Observe:** With the table of six standing beside the table of twelve, every product on the right was the product level with it on the left, doubled.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- adds the smaller table's number to its product instead of doubling it, so that 42 in the table of six is read as 48 rather than 84. → the two products standing level are lit together and read out, and the arrow between them is drawn as a second copy of the left hand product landing on top of itself rather than as a step along the table. The child's answer stays in the slot while the rail counts both readings out and shows which row of the table of six 48 actually is.
- doubles the row count as well as the product and reads the answer off the wrong row. → the row counts of the two tables are drawn out to the far edges of the rail and held still while the products alone are lit, and a finger drawn down the channel shows the one row count standing beside both tables at every level. The row the child read from is left lit in amber beside the row they were sent to.
**Misconception to break:** Adding six to 42 instead of doubling it, which gives 48.

### 3 · An even number gives an even product
**Screen:** a finished table with the even check open beneath it and two empty trays
**Does:** reads the last digit of every product and sorts them into odd and even
**Win:** every product lands in the even tray and an odd answer dropped in is pushed straight back out
**Feels:** the last digit on its own says whether an answer could possibly belong to this table
**Goal:** a table built on an even number has every product even
**Observe:** Every product in the table of fourteen ended in an even digit, while the table of fifteen ran odd, even, odd, even all the way down.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- sorts by the multiplier rather than by the product, so an odd row count sends an even answer into the odd tray. → the row counts are covered over on the strip and only the products are left standing, each with its last digit lit, and the products are then offered to the trays one at a time with nothing else in view. The child's earlier sorting is left drawn faintly under the tray it went into, and 16 x 9 is worked out in the open beside it as 144.
- carries the rule across to a table built on an odd number and pushes every odd product in it back out. → the odd table is stood beside the even one on the rail with both sets of last digits lit, and the two lists are read down together, so the odd table is heard running odd, even, odd, even while the even table does not change at all. The products the child pushed back are returned to the rows they came from.
**Misconception to break:** Deciding the product must be odd because the multiplier 9 is odd.

### 4 · A bigger table means fewer steps
**Screen:** the same product worked twice, once with the table open and once with the multiplier split
**Does:** works it both ways and counts the steps each route took
**Win:** the table side finishes in fewer steps and both sides land on the same product
**Feels:** the same answer twice, and one of the two routes took noticeably less writing
**Goal:** a table past ten turns a two digit multiplier into one fact for each column
**Observe:** 412 x 13 took three column steps with the table of thirteen open, and more than that when the multiplier was split into a ones row and a tens row that then had to be added.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- uses the 1 and the 3 of the multiplier as two separate single digit multipliers and multiplies by each of them in turn. → the multiplier is drawn out as its places, the tens part written in full as 10 and the ones part as 3, so that the 13 is seen to be one number made of a ten and a three and not two multipliers at all. Both routes are then worked side by side, the child's and the table's, and the first column where the two part is lit amber and stays lit.
- counts the steps of the two routes but counts the adding of the two rows as no step at all. → the two routes stand side by side with every line of writing numbered down its own margin, the addition of the two rows numbered like any other line, and the total of each margin written at its foot. The child's count stays exactly where it was written, beside the one the counter made.
**Misconception to break:** Using the 1 and the 3 of 13 as if they were two separate one digit multipliers, which loses the tens of the multiplier.

## Delight
The second phase, the first time the compare rail brings a second table alongside. The two tables do not slide into place past one another. The rail lifts the rows of the table already standing and threads the new table's rows in between them, so that for a moment every row of six has a row of twelve resting on it. Then they separate into two columns with their rows dead level, and a finger drawn down the channel between them lights one level pair after another, each pair reading itself out as a number and its double. Draw the finger back up the rail and the pairs unlight in the same order they came.

**General miss line:** Empty that row and build it again as a step off the row above it.

## Answer key
A table is built by starting at the number itself and adding that same number once more for every row, so each row is one step further on than the row above. Every product in the table of twelve is double the product in the same row of the table of six, and every product in the table of sixteen is double the product in the same row of the table of eight. A table built on an even number has every product even, which catches an answer that cannot be right without any working. A table past ten turns a two digit multiplier into a single fact for each column of the multiplicand, instead of two rows that then have to be added together.

## Student controls
- **Add a row**: fills the next row by adding the table's own number to the row above
- **Running count**: keeps the tally of how many times the number has been taken
- **Compare rail**: stands two finished tables side by side with their rows level
- **Light a pair**: lights two rows at the same level and names what links them
- **Even check**: sorts every product of a table by its last digit into odd and even
- **Steps counter**: works one product with a table and without one, and counts the steps
- **Pick a table**: opens any empty table from eleven to sixteen

## Textbook (this topic)
**Pages:** 43, 44
**Key terms:** table, repeated addition, pattern, double
The tables of eleven to sixteen are built by repeated addition, and each one hides a pattern. A multiplication table. Every row is the row above with the number added once more. Building a table this way shows where each product comes from instead of asking you to remember it. Each product in the table of twelve is double the one beside it. The same holds between the table of eight and the table of sixteen. Twelve is six doubled, so its whole table is doubled too. It is always even. An even number is made of twos, and taking it any number of times leaves it made of twos. This is a quick way to catch an answer that cannot be right. Without the table you must multiply twice and add the two results. With it, the multiplier is a single fact. Learning a table past ten is buying yourself a shorter piece of work.
