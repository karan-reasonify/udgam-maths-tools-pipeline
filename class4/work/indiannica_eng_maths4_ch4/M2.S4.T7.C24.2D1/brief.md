## What it is
**Description:** A problem in sentences with every number liftable, a bay that asks for one amount and how many, and a strip that works the same product the other way round.
**Why:** Turn a problem written in sentences into a multiplication, work it, and check it by working the same two numbers the other way round.
**Look:** A short problem across the top with every number liftable, a question row under it, and a bay split into one for each and how many.
**Context:** Grade 4, the closing topic, and it answers the question the opening page asked and never worked out. An adult sees word problems. What the page is actually doing is naming two decisions that a child normally makes in silence: what one thing is worth, and how many of those things there are. Every question in this section has that same shape under the words. A few of them refuse to hand the number over at all and describe it instead, which is a second job to be finished before the first one starts.
**For the child:** Read the problem and find the amount that goes with one thing, then how many of those things there are. Lift them into the two sides of the bay. Write the whole multiplication into the question row before you work any of it. If a number is only described, build it in the digits bay first. When you have an answer, swap the two numbers and work it again.
**Teaching notes:** Make the question row a real step and do not let anybody past it. A child who has written the multiplication down before working any of it has already done the hard half, and a child who starts multiplying digits straight out of the sentences is guessing. Work the chapter's own printed example together first: a printer that prints 114 pages a minute, running for 3698 minutes, gives three partial products, 14,792 from the ones, 36,980 from the tens and 3,69,800 from the hundreds, which add to 4,21,572 pages. Then go back to the very first page of the chapter, because that is what the linking box asks for. One saree takes 180 days and there are 112 of them, so the total the book asked for on its opening page and never printed is 20,160 days. Have the class work it, then work it the other way round, and let the two answers stand together. The swap and check strip is the habit worth leaving them with. It costs one more working and it catches the slips that reading your own working over never does, because the second route uses different steps. Finish every problem with the answer read aloud as a sentence. A number with no sentence round it is not yet an answer.

## Phases (what each mount shows and does; the words are in tool.tsx)

### 1 · One amount each, and many of them
**Screen:** a two sentence problem with its numbers drawn as cards and the bay below it empty
**Does:** lifts the amount for one thing into one side of the bay and the count into the other
**Win:** both sides of the bay fill and the sentences the two numbers came from light
**Feels:** the two numbers are obvious from the first reading, and which of them is which turns out to be the whole question
**Goal:** the amount for one thing and the number of things are picked out of the sentences
**Observe:** Both sides of the bay had to hold a card before anything could be multiplied, and the two sides are asking for different things.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- adds the two numbers, so a carton packed with 216 bottles and 8 such cartons is handed in as 224. → the two sentences are read again one at a time with a strip of eight cartons drawn underneath them, every carton the same size and holding the same 216 whether the child likes it or not. The eight are laid out equal and separate and the strip is read back as one amount taken eight times over, which is a multiplication and comes to 1728. Nothing is cleared and the answer stays in the slot.
- fills the two halves of the bay the other way round, putting the count of cartons into the side for the amount that goes with one and the bottles into the side for how many, and the product comes out as 1728 either way. → both answer sentences are written out under the bay and read back aloud. One of them says that eight cartons hold 1728 bottles between them, which is the problem. The other says that 216 cartons hold 8 bottles each, which is not, although its arithmetic is identical. The two bay headings light in turn while the sentences are read, and neither sentence is marked.
**Misconception to break:** Adding 145 and 12, because the words how much in all sound like a total to be added.

### 2 · Write the multiplication before working it
**Screen:** the two chosen numbers in the bay with the question row above the working area still empty
**Does:** writes the multiplication into the question row, and only then starts working
**Win:** the question row holds the multiplication and the working area unlocks beneath it
**Feels:** writing it out first feels like a delay, and then the thing you wrote turns out to be the thing you were about to get wrong
**Goal:** the multiplication is written down in full before any digit is multiplied
**Observe:** Filling the two sides the wrong way round gave exactly the same product and an answer sentence that described nothing at all.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- starts multiplying digits straight out of the sentences, so a number from the wrong sentence gets picked up halfway through and the working cannot be read back afterwards. → the working area locks again and the question row stands empty above it. The two cards in the bay are read up into the row one at a time with the multiplication sign written between them, and only when the row holds the whole thing does the working area open under it. Nothing already written is taken away.
- writes a number into the question row instead of a multiplication, because a blank row that has to be filled before anything else opens looks like the place an answer goes. → the question row is labelled again under its left hand end and the working area below it is named as the place an answer is allowed to land. The number the child wrote lifts out of the row and drops into the working area where it can stand, and the row fills instead with the two cards from the bay and the sign between them, at which point the working area opens.
**Misconception to break:** Starting to multiply digits before the multiplication has been written down, so the wrong pair of numbers gets used.

### 3 · Some numbers are described rather than printed
**Screen:** a problem naming a number by description, with an empty card where that number should be
**Does:** works the described number out in the digits bay and brings it back as a card
**Win:** the empty card fills with the number the description named and the digits bay closes
**Feels:** a number that is only described does not feel like a number until you have built it, and then it behaves like every other number on the board
**Goal:** a number given only in words is turned into digits before it is used
**Observe:** A number that was only described stayed as a blank card until it had been built in the digits bay.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- takes the smallest three digit number to be 111, so the product of the largest and the smallest three digit numbers comes out as 1,10,889 instead of 99,900. → a three place frame is drawn and filled from the left. The first place takes the smallest digit that can stand there, which is one, and then the frame asks what the second place may hold; a zero is allowed there and is smaller than a one, so in it goes, and so does the third. The frame reads 100, and 999 x 100 is worked beside it to 99,900. Nothing is cleared.
- reads a dozen as ten, so 45 trays of a dozen eggs comes out as 450 instead of 540. → one tray is drawn with its cups counted out, twelve of them, and the tray is copied until 45 of them stand equal and separate on the board. The digits bay writes twelve onto a card and sends it up into the blank in the sentence, and the working reads 12 x 45 = 540 with the tray still drawn beside it.
**Misconception to break:** Taking the smallest two digit number to be 0 or 1, neither of which has two digits.

### 4 · The other order is a free check
**Screen:** a finished product with the swap and check strip open beneath it
**Does:** swaps the two numbers, works it again and stands the two answers level
**Win:** both workings land on one number and the strip closes with the two rows level
**Feels:** the two workings look nothing like one another right down to the last line, and then they land on the same number
**Goal:** the same product is worked the other way round and the two answers are read together
**Observe:** 98 x 5569 opened four rows, 5569 x 98 opened two, and both landed on 5,45,762.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- checks by working the same product the same way a second time, gets the same answer and calls it checked. → the two workings are stood side by side and shown to be the same rows in the same order; a slip is put into the first row of both at the same moment and both totals move together, so the two agree with each other and are both wrong. The swapped working is then run beside them with rows that are nothing like theirs, and it does not move. Nothing is marked.
- swaps the two numbers, sees that the second working has nothing in common with the first, decides one of them must be wrong, and hands in the shorter one without finishing it. → both workings are run all the way to their totals with the multiplier that made each set of rows named in the margin. 98 x 5569 opens four rows, 882 and 5880 and 49,000 and 4,90,000, because its multiplier has four places. 5569 x 98 opens two, 44,552 and 5,01,210, because its multiplier has two. The rows are counted off against the multipliers that made them, and both totals land on 5,45,762 and are drawn level.
**Misconception to break:** Checking by repeating the same working, which repeats the same slip and agrees with itself.

## Delight
The last phase, the moment the swap and check strip opens on 98 x 5569. The first working runs down the left of the strip in four rose rows, 882 and 5880 and 49,000 and 4,90,000, one for each place of a four digit multiplier. The second runs down beside it in two, 44,552 and 5,01,210, because the multiplier swapped is only two digits long. Nothing about the two looks alike, not the count of rows, not one row in either of them, not the length of the rule underneath. Then both totals drop onto a single line and read 5,45,762, and the line closes as one bar across the strip. The voice says nothing while it happens.

**General miss line:** Read the sentences again with both sides of the bay still showing.

## Answer key
Find the amount that goes with one thing, then find how many of those things there are. Those two numbers are the whole question, and one amount repeated once for each thing is a multiplication. Write the multiplication down in full before any of it is worked, because deciding what to work out and working it out are two separate jobs. A number that is described rather than printed, such as the largest three digit number or a dozen, has to be turned into digits first and then used like any other number. Any method from this chapter may be used on the product once it is written. Check by working the same two numbers the other way round, which must give the same answer by different steps, and write the answer back into a sentence.

## Student controls
- **Lift a number**: takes a number card out of its sentence and down to the bay
- **Push it back**: returns a number to its sentence, so nothing is committed
- **One for each**: holds the amount that goes with a single thing
- **How many**: holds the number of those things there are
- **Question row**: takes the whole multiplication in writing and unlocks the working area
- **Digits bay**: turns a number that is only described into a card of digits
- **Swap and check**: works the same two numbers the other way round and stands the answers level
