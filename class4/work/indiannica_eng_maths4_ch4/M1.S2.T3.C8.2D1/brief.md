## What it is
**Description:** Two trays that hold the same numbers in different arrangements, a strip that breaks a number up before multiplying it, and a rail where zeros do the work.
**Why:** Find out what you are allowed to change about a multiplication without changing its answer, and let the zeros of a round number do the work.
**Look:** Two trays side by side sharing one set of number cards, an expand and add strip below them and a rail of opening zeros along the foot.
**Context:** Grade 4, and the chapter stops calculating here to ask what may be changed without spoiling the answer. An adult sees five properties and a handful of shortcuts. What the page is actually doing is showing that the shortcuts are the properties being used on purpose: multiplying by a multiple of ten is the expanding rule, and pairing two of three numbers to make a round number is the grouping rule. A child who meets them as separate tricks has six things to remember instead of two.
**For the child:** Put the same numbers in both trays in different arrangements and work them both. Bracket whichever pair is easiest and multiply that pair first. Open the expand strip and break the long number into its places before you multiply it. On the zeros rail, say how many zeros will follow before you press.
**Teaching notes:** Show each rule before naming it, the way the page does. Multiply something by one and then by zero and let the class say what happened before the sentence is read out. For the expand strip, work the chapter's own example first: 625 x 5 is 600 x 5 + 20 x 5 + 5 x 5, which is 3000 + 100 + 25, and it comes to 3125 whichever way it is worked. Then the zeros rail, and here make them say how many zeros will follow before they press, because counting the zeros of the multiplier is the whole skill and counting the zeros of the short fact is the whole mistake. The money example on the same page is the best argument for the rail: fifty notes of a hundred rupees and twenty-five notes of ten rupees come to 5000 and 250, which is Rs 5250, and nobody counted a single note. Leave the pairing bay until last. Three numbers to multiply and a free choice of which two go first is a real decision, and a child who has made it once stops working strictly from the left every time.

## Opening (first frame of every mount)
Every mount comes up already furnished for its phase with nothing in front of it. On the first phase one long number lies in the left tray with a one card and a zero card waiting on the corner of the bench beside it. On the second the same cards lie in both trays in two different arrangements and neither tray has been worked. On the third the expand and add strip stands open under the trays with a three digit multiplicand already broken into its hundreds, tens and ones and every small row beneath it empty. On the fourth the zeros rail runs along the foot of the bench with a number sitting above it and the places to its right still closed. The running count at the top left holds the arrangements worked against the arrangements standing on the bench, and the answer slot and the pad stand empty down the right. One thing moves, once: the one card, or the right hand tray, or the first broken part, or the first closed place on the rail, gives one slow pulse while the voice asks what the bench is going to do. That pulse is the only invitation, and a finger on it is the whole way in.

## Phases (what each mount shows and does; the words are in tool.tsx)

### 1 · One leaves it alone and zero flattens it
**Screen:** a tray holding one long number, with a one card and a zero card waiting beside it
**Does:** drops each card in turn and reads what came back
**Win:** the first tray hands the number back unchanged and the second collapses to nothing
**Feels:** two cards that need no working at all, whatever number happens to be in the tray
**Goal:** a product with one gives the number back and a product with zero gives nothing
**Observe:** Both trays landed on the same product, and the working drawn under the two of them did not look the same.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- gives the number itself as the answer to the zero, by treating zero the way one had just behaved. → the tray holding the number is emptied slowly out onto the bench as equal groups, and then the zero card asks for that many groups to be taken: the bench counts out no groups at all and stands bare, with the child's answer left in the slot beside the empty bench and nothing marked.
- answers zero for the product with one as well, because the two cards were dropped into the tray in the same breath. → the two cards separate and are worked in two rows one above the other, each with a tray of its own: the one row hands the long number back digit for digit with the two readings stood together and matching, and the zero row collapses to nothing. Neither row is cleared while the other runs.
**Misconception to break:** Writing 4826 for the product with zero, by treating zero the way one behaves.

### 2 · The order is yours to choose
**Screen:** the same cards in both trays, arranged differently in each
**Does:** works both trays and reads the two products against each other
**Win:** both trays land on the same product and the pairing bay lights the pair that makes a round number
**Feels:** the arrangement is a free choice, and the answer does not care which choice was made
**Goal:** swapping the cards, or bracketing a different pair, leaves the product alone
**Observe:** The one card handed the number straight back and the zero card emptied the tray, whatever length of number was in it.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- works only the left tray and copies its answer into the right one, because the cards are the same and the answer is bound to be. → the right tray works its own arrangement anyway, slowly, every step drawn where the child could have drawn it, and the two products land side by side on the ledge and match. The two pieces of working are then stood one above the other, where they are plainly not the same shape, with the rows of each counted along the margin. The copied answer is left exactly where it was put and nothing is marked.
- changes one of the cards as well as the order, because swapping and rewriting feel like the same tidying up. → the changed card is drawn back to the bench edge and the card it replaced returns to its tray, and both trays are then worked on the cards they share. The product the changed card would have given is worked in a third small row beneath the ledge and stood beside the other two, where it does not match either of them.
**Misconception to break:** Believing the number written first must be the one that goes on the mat.

### 3 · A number can be broken up before it is multiplied
**Screen:** the expand and add strip with the multiplicand broken into its hundreds, tens and ones
**Does:** multiplies each part in its own row, then adds the rows up
**Win:** the parts add to the same product the whole number gave, standing beside it
**Feels:** the pieces are small enough to do in the head, and they still add back to the whole thing
**Goal:** a multiplicand split by place gives parts that multiply separately and add back
**Observe:** The parts of 625 broken up and multiplied came to 3000 and 100 and 25, and those add back to the same 3125 the whole number gave.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- multiplies the hundreds part, stops there, and reads that as the answer. → the two parts that were never taken stay on the strip drawn grey with their small rows left empty and ringed, and the total the child gave is stood against the total of all three rows, with the gap between them written out beside it as the two parts that were left behind: 3000 given, 3125 wanted, and 100 and 25 never taken.
- breaks the multiplier into parts as well, pairs each part off with the part standing level with it, and adds just those products. → a line is drawn from every part of the one number to every part of the other, so the pairings the child never made show up as lines with nothing written on them; the strip then closes the multiplier back into a single card and each part of the multiplicand is taken by the whole of it in turn. The child's rows stay on the bench while both readings are worked.
**Misconception to break:** Multiplying only the hundreds part and forgetting that the tens and the ones still have to be taken.

### 4 · Zeros at the end do the work for you
**Screen:** the zeros rail with the number above it and empty places opening to its right
**Does:** multiplies by ten, then by a hundred, then by a multiple, counting the zeros each time
**Win:** the digits stay put, the zeros open beneath them and the rail names the places they fill
**Feels:** the digits stay exactly as they were and the only thing that changes is how many places have opened under them
**Goal:** a round multiplier is handled by a short fact and the zeros it carries
**Observe:** On the rail the digits never changed. The only thing that changed was how many places had opened underneath them.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- counts the zeros already sitting in the short fact and writes those, instead of the zeros the multiplier carries. → the multiplier is drawn on its own above the rail with its zeros ringed and counted out loud, and then that many empty places open under the right hand end of the short fact one at a time, each naming itself as it opens. The child's answer stands beside the rail with the places it is short of left open and ringed, and neither number is cleared.
- opens the new places in front of the number instead of after it, so the zeros land at the leading end. → the number is read out place by place with the zeros where the child put them, and then again with them at the other end, and the two readings are written one under the other along the rail with the place names lit under every digit of both. The leading zeros are heard naming nothing at all, and the child's reading is left standing.
**Misconception to break:** Writing 1892 with only one zero after it, by counting the zeros of the short fact instead of the zeros of the multiplier.

## Delight
The fourth phase, the first time a number is sent along the zeros rail. The digits do not jump and nothing is redrawn. One empty place opens under the right hand end of the number and the whole number slides one place to the left into it, with the place names lighting under each digit as they pass, ones becoming tens, tens becoming hundreds, all the way along. Ask for a hundred and two places open and the same slide happens twice over. Send the number back and the places close one at a time and the digits walk home. The same cards are worth hundreds in one position and hundreds of thousands three positions along, and not one of them was ever rewritten.

**General miss line:** Put the cards back and let the trays work the two arrangements side by side.

## Answer key
Multiplying by one gives the number back, and multiplying by zero gives zero. The product does not change when the multiplicand and the multiplier are swapped, and it does not change however three or more numbers are grouped, so any arrangement may be chosen and the pair that makes a round number is the pair worth taking first. A multiplicand may be broken into its places, each place multiplied, and the parts added, and the total is the same as multiplying the whole number at once. Multiplying by ten, a hundred or a thousand writes one, two or three zeros after the number. For a multiple such as sixty or eight hundred, use the short fact first and then write the zeros the multiplier carries.

## Student controls
- **Two trays**: works the same cards as two products at once and stands the answers together
- **Swap**: reverses the order of the cards in one tray
- **Brackets**: closes a bracket around a pair so that pair is multiplied first
- **One and zero cards**: drops a one or a zero into either tray and shows what it does to the product
- **Expand strip**: splits the multiplicand into its places, multiplies each and adds the rows back
- **Zeros rail**: slides the digits left over opening zeros for ten, a hundred, a thousand and their multiples
- **Pairing bay**: lights whichever two of three cards multiply to a round number

## Textbook (this topic)
**Pages:** 41, 42, 43
**Key terms:** order, grouping, expanded form, multiple
Order, grouping and expanding can all be changed, and zeros at the end make some products quick. Multiplying by one gives the number back unchanged, because you have taken it once. Multiplying by zero gives zero, because you have taken it no times at all. The product is the same either way. With three or more numbers the order may be changed as freely as you like. So you are allowed to arrange them into whichever order is easiest to work. The total is the same as multiplying the whole number at once. Breaking a number into its parts makes each step small enough to do in your head, and nothing is lost by doing it. One zero, then two, then three are written after it. For a multiple such as sixty, use the short fact first and then write the zero. The digits never change, only how far left they sit.
