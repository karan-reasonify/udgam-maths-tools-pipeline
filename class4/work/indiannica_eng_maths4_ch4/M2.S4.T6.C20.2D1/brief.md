## What it is
**Description:** Rule a grid with one cell for every pair of digits, fill each cell with a single digit fact split by its diagonal, then sweep the strips from the bottom right.
**Why:** Find a product a second way, by giving every pair of digits its own square and reading the answer off the diagonals.
**Look:** A ruled grid of equal cells with a diagonal across each one, the digits of the two numbers outside it, and shaded strips running down to the bottom right corner.
**Context:** Grade 4, and the chapter gives a second method for a job the first method already does. An adult sees a novelty. What the page is actually doing is separating the two halves of a long multiplication so that each can go wrong on its own: the grid holds every digit pair apart, and the strips put the places back together afterwards. The page's own claim about carrying is true of the first half and not the second, and its own worked example happens to need no pass to the left while all four questions printed beneath it do, so the sweep is taught here as part of the method rather than met by surprise.
**For the child:** Rule the grid first, one square for every pair of digits, with a diagonal in each. Write one number along the top and the other down the side. Fill each square with its pair, tens above the line and ones below. Then sweep the strips from the bottom right corner, and pass one to the left whenever a strip comes to more than nine.
**Teaching notes:** Rule the grid with a ruler the first time and let it take as long as it takes. Drawn freehand it stops being a grid and the strips stop lining up. Work the chapter's own example all the way through before the tool is opened: 55 multiplied by 23 fills four cells, and the strips read five, six, two and one from the right, so the product is 1265. Then say the claim printed on that page carefully, because the page itself does not. Nothing is carried while you multiply, and that much is true: every cell is a single digit fact. The adding that follows is a different matter, and all four questions printed directly beneath that claim have a strip that comes to more than nine and has to pass one to its left. Work one of them with the class so the first pass happens where everybody can see it, and keep the sweep going strictly from the bottom right corner. Finish by working the same product in columns and standing the two answers together. A child who has seen both methods land on one number owns the second one instead of performing it.

## Phases (what each mount shows and does; the words are in tool.tsx)

### 1 · One cell for every pair of digits
**Screen:** an empty stage with the two numbers waiting outside it and the ruler control lit
**Does:** rules the grid to the size the two numbers ask for and sets the digits round its edges
**Win:** the grid stands with one cell for every pair and a diagonal drawn across each one
**Feels:** the grid looks like it is asking how many numbers there are, and it is really asking how many pairs of digits there are
**Goal:** the grid is ruled with one cell for each pair of digits and a diagonal in every cell
**Observe:** The grid took one cell for every pair of digits, so a three digit number against a two digit one wanted six cells and a four digit against a two digit wanted eight.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- rules two cells because there are two numbers, so the grid for 87 x 35 comes out one cell across and two cells down. → the digits step out of the two numbers one at a time and each pairs with every digit of the other, the pairings printed in a short list beside the bay: eight with three, seven with three, eight with five and seven with five, four of them for two digits against two. The grid redraws itself with one cell for each pairing and the list empties into it. Nothing is cleared and the answer stays in the slot.
- rules the grid to the right size but draws each diagonal whichever way that cell happens to suggest, so some run down to the right and some down to the left. → the sweep is tried and two strips cross in the middle of the grid, so one cell finds itself in two strips and another in none, and the answer rail is left with a square it cannot fill. The diagonals are then drawn again all running the same way and the strips lie parallel, each ending in its own square on the rail. Nothing is marked.
**Misconception to break:** Drawing two cells because there are two numbers, instead of one cell for every pair of digits.

### 2 · The diagonal splits tens from ones
**Screen:** the ruled grid with every cell empty and one pair of digits lit outside it
**Does:** multiplies the lit pair and writes the two digits on either side of the diagonal
**Win:** the cell fills on both sides of its diagonal and the next pair lights outside the grid
**Feels:** a square with one line through it turns out to be two places rather than one, and even a small product needs both of them
**Goal:** each cell holds one digit fact with its tens above the diagonal and its ones below
**Observe:** A pair coming to less than ten still filled both halves of its cell, with a grey zero sitting above the diagonal.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- gives a cell whose pair comes to less than ten a single digit, so 4 x 2 in the grid for 74 x 26 is written as a bare 8 with nothing beside it, and because the pen was coming down off the digit outside the grid the 8 lands above the diagonal. The sweep then reads 2644 instead of 1924. → the cell is opened large on its own with its diagonal across it and its two halves named, the upper one for tens and the lower one for ones. Eight has no tens, so the upper half takes a grey zero and the eight drops into the lower half where it belongs. The two strips that cell sits in are then swept again with it filled properly, and the rail reads 1924. Nothing is cleared.
- puts the tens digit below the diagonal and the ones digit above it in every cell, so 7 x 6 = 42 reads 2 above and 4 below, and the grid for 74 x 26 sweeps to 5182. → one cell is opened large with its halves named and the places of the answer printed along the answer rail beside it. The upper half is shown to lie in the strip one place further left than the lower half of the same cell, which is why the tens belong up there. Every cell is turned the right way round in place, one at a time, and the grid sweeps to 1924.
**Misconception to break:** Writing a single 8 in the cell for 4 x 2 and leaving the upper half of that cell empty.

### 3 · Nothing is carried while you multiply
**Screen:** the filled grid with the same product set out in columns beside it, its rings lit
**Does:** compares the two and says what the grid never once asked for
**Win:** the column sum shows its rings and the grid shows none of them while it is being filled
**Feels:** the grid really does ask for no carrying while you multiply, and then the adding turns up and wants one after all
**Goal:** filling the grid asks for no carrying at all, because every cell is one digit times one digit
**Observe:** Filling all four cells of the grid for 87 x 35 asked for no carrying at all, while the same product set out in columns beside it drew two carry rings before its rows had even been added.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- carries between cells while filling the grid, so a cell whose pair comes to 15 keeps its 5 and sends a 1 into the cell on its left. → the cell is drawn large on its own with its diagonal across it, and the tens of its product are shown dropping into the upper half of that same cell rather than leaving it, because that half is where the cell keeps its own tens. The cells on either side are shown untouched throughout. Nothing is carried while you multiply your way across a grid, and the grid is then redrawn with every cell holding its own pair and no traffic at all between them.
- hears that nothing is carried while you multiply and takes it to mean nothing is left to do, so the filled cells are read straight off from the top left and a string of their digits is handed in. → the word is split in two on the stage and each half is answered on its own. While you multiply, the grid asks for none, and that half is ticked off against the compare panel beside it. Then the strips are outlined as the second half of the job: they are the places of the answer and not one of them has been added yet. One strip shades from the bottom right corner to show what is still to come, and the cells stay exactly as the child left them.
**Misconception to break:** Taking the claim further than it goes and expecting the diagonal strips to need no pass to the left either.

### 4 · The answer is read down the diagonals
**Screen:** the filled grid with the diagonal strips shaded and the answer squares empty down its edge
**Does:** sweeps each strip in turn from the bottom right corner and writes its digit
**Win:** every strip lands a digit, the ones that passed sit on the strips they joined, and the product reads off the edge
**Feels:** the grid was finished and there is a whole second job standing there, and the second job turns out to be adding
**Goal:** the strips are swept from the bottom right, and a strip past nine passes one to its left
**Observe:** Sweeping 87 x 35 reached a strip holding four, four and two, which came to ten, so it wrote a zero, sent one to the strip on its left, and the rail read 3045.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- lets a strip that comes to ten put both its digits into one square of the answer rail, so 87 x 35 reads three, ten, four, five. → the four squares of the rail are named with their places, ones, tens, hundreds and thousands, and the square holding ten is opened. A place holds a single digit, so the zero stays where it is and the one belongs to the place on its left. A small vermilion one lifts out of the square, travels up the strip and lands above the strip to its left, and that strip is totalled again with it counted in, giving three. The rail reads 3045.
- sweeps from the top left corner instead of the bottom right, because that is the corner a page is read from. → the top left strip is shown to hold only the 2 from 3 x 8, and it is written as the first digit of the answer; two strips later one comes to ten and has nowhere to send its one, because the strip that should have taken it was written three moves ago. The answer rail is then named with its places, the bottom right corner is read as the ones place because it is the strip holding the two ones digits and nothing else, and the sweep is run again from there, the places climbing leftwards the way they do in every column working in the book.
**Misconception to break:** Writing 12 into the answer where a strip comes to twelve, instead of keeping 2 and passing one to the left.

## Delight
The third phase, the moment the grid and the column working finish together. The four cells of 87 x 35 fill one after another and not one ring appears anywhere on the grid, while an arm's length away in the compare panel the same product has drawn two of them in vermilion, each sitting small above the column it was joining. The two are left standing side by side for a beat. Then the sweep begins, and on the strip that comes to ten a vermilion one lifts off the grid for the first time and travels up to the strip on its left. The tool has been telling the truth in two halves, and the second half arrives with a ring of its own.

**General miss line:** Fill that square again and say which half each of its digits belongs in.

## Answer key
Rule a grid one cell across for each digit of the first number and one cell down for each digit of the second, with a diagonal across every cell. Write one number along the top and the other down the side. Fill each cell with its pair's product, the tens digit above the diagonal and the ones digit below it, and give every cell two digits even when the pair comes to less than ten. Nothing is carried while you are multiplying, because each cell holds a single digit fact on its own. Then add along the diagonal strips, starting at the bottom right corner. A strip that comes to more than nine keeps its right hand digit and passes one onto the strip to its left, which is counted in before that strip is totalled. Read the digits back off the edge and that is the product.

## Student controls
- **Ruler**: draws the grid to the size the two numbers ask for, with a diagonal in every cell
- **Set the digits**: places one number along the top and the other down the side
- **Light a pair**: lights the row digit and the column digit a touched cell belongs to
- **Fill a cell**: writes the tens digit above the diagonal and the ones digit below it
- **Compare panel**: stands the same product in columns beside the grid with its rings lit
- **Sweep a strip**: shades one diagonal strip from the bottom right and totals it
- **Pass to the left**: sends one onto the next strip when a strip comes to more than nine

## Textbook (this topic)
**Pages:** 47, 48
**Key terms:** lattice multiplication, diagonal, cell, grid
Lattice multiplication puts every digit pair in its own cell and reads the answer down the diagonals. One for each digit of the first with each digit of the second. The lattice gives every pair its own square, so no pair can be forgotten and none can be counted twice. The diagonal divides the cell in two. The tens digit goes in the upper part and the ones digit in the lower part. Every cell is filled the same way, even when the product is less than ten. Carry anything. Each cell is a single digit times a single digit, written down and left alone. That is what the method is for, and it is why the multiplying part is hard to get wrong. Add along each diagonal strip, beginning at the bottom right. Those totals, read back the other way, are the product. If a strip comes to more than nine, pass one to the strip on its left.
