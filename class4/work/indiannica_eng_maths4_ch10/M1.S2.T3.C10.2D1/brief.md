## What it is
**Description:** Find the step between neighbours on a track, carry a number or letter series on, and make a series of your own from a start and a rule.
**Why:** Find the step from each term to the next, check it all along, and carry a number or letter series on, or make one of your own from a start and a rule.
**Look:** A track of stepping stones with a jump arc over every pair of neighbours, a letter track under it, and a pairing tray and a rule maker at the side.
**Context:** Grade 4, and the chapter's central skill: carrying a series on by finding its rule. An adult sees next terms being written. What is actually being practised is checking the step between every pair of neighbours rather than only the first, which is the only way to notice that one of the six printed patterns on the exercise page has no fixed step at all. The rule maker also answers the opening page's closing task, create a number sequence of your own, which the Concept Linking box two pages later sends the child back to.
**For the child:** Find the jump between each pair of numbers. Check every jump. Use the same jump to fill the empty stones. Put the counters in pairs. If the jumps are not the same, look at where each jump comes from.
**Teaching notes:** Walk a chalk trail of numbers before the tool is opened, one child setting a start and a secret step and the next child finding it. Then insist on checking the step between every pair of neighbours, not only the first two, because that is the one habit the printed exercise needs and does not teach. Five of its six patterns keep a fixed step and a child reading only the first pair gets all five right. The sixth doubles: its steps are not equal and they get bigger. Let the class find that, then ask where each step comes from, and let them see that each step is the number already reached, so each number is added to itself. That is addition. Do not name a new rule for it; the summary box on the same page says patterns are made by skip counting, and the point is that this one is not. The page that introduces number patterns gives counting back in ones a skip counting name; call it counting back. Its list that goes down by fifty ends in dots, and the next term is zero, where the count stops. The opening page's list, each number the sum of the two before it, is never used again in the chapter, and the Concept Linking box sends the class back to it for a question that is not there. Use the rule maker for the opening page's own task instead: every child makes a sequence of their own and says its start and its rule. The book calls a list in order a series; keep its word, since it is one of the four vocabulary words, and say that it means a list.

## Phases (what each mount shows and does; the words are in tool.tsx)

### 1 · The step between neighbours
**Screen:** a number track with four terms written and three stones blank, its jump arcs unlabelled
**Does:** labels every arc with the step, checks that they match, then fills the blank stones
**Win:** every arc shows the same label and the blank stones fill one jump at a time
**Feels:** every jump the same size, checked all along, and a count down that has somewhere to stop
**Goal:** the step between neighbours is found, checked all along and used, and a count down stops at zero
**Observe:** The step finder labelled every arc, and the one that differed stood out without being looked for.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- reads the step off the first pair and carries it on without checking the pairs after it. → the step finder labels every arc again one at a time from the left, and any arc whose label differs is ringed in reddish purple. The child's stones stay where they were filled. Arc compare stands that arc beside the first, and both stay on the stage.
- carries a count down on past the zero stone, or stops at the last term printed before it. → a step that would pass zero is handed back with the zero stone lit, and nothing is drawn to the left of it. Where the child stopped short, the stone before zero stays drawn and open. The count down is then walked from the first term, one jump at a time, to zero, where it stops.
**Misconception to break:** Checking only the first pair, and so missing a series whose step changes further along.

### 2 · Letters can step too
**Screen:** the letter track with four letters lit and three places waiting
**Does:** counts the places between neighbouring letters, then steps on by the same amount
**Win:** the next letters light on the track, each with its place in the alphabet beneath it
**Feels:** letters line up like numbers, each with its own place, and a jump along them is counted by its landings
**Goal:** letters step along the alphabet the way numbers step along counting
**Observe:** The count down reached the zero stone and the next step was handed back.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- counts the letters in between instead of the jump, so A to D reads as a step of two and the track goes on J, L, N. → the letter track counts the jump from A one landing at a time, lighting B, then C, then D, and numbering each landing. The count stops on the letter it lands on, at three. The child's letters stay on their stones, and the same count is run again from J.
- steps the letter and its number by different amounts, writing I ten after H eight. → the place toggle writes each letter's place beneath it, and the child's I stands with nine beneath it, not ten. The jump from H is then counted landing by landing to J, and J's place is written under it. Both letters stay on the track.
**Misconception to break:** Stepping the letter and the number by different amounts, so the number stops matching the letter's place.

### 3 · Paired or one left over
**Screen:** the pairing tray holding a heap of counters, with an even or odd card waiting
**Does:** sets the counters out two by two and turns the card from what is left
**Win:** the pairs line up and the card turns, with any leftover counter standing on its own
**Feels:** the counters line up two by two, and either everyone has a partner or one is left on its own
**Goal:** a number that pairs up with none left over is even, and one that leaves one over is odd
**Observe:** Eleven counters made five pairs, and one counter stood on its own.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- calls eleven even because five pairs could be made, without looking at the button left over. → the pairing tray sets the counters out again two by two at walking pace, and the one left over stands apart at the end in reddish purple. The card waits face down until the leftover has been tapped. The child's answer stays in the slot.
- calls fourteen odd because its first digit, one, is odd. → fourteen counters are paired, first a ten making five pairs with none over, then the four making two pairs with none over. Seven pairs stand in a row with no counter left alone. The child's card stays beside the tray.
**Misconception to break:** Calling eleven even because five pairs could be made, without looking at the button left over.

### 4 · When the steps are not equal
**Screen:** a track whose jump arcs carry different labels, with the rule maker open beside it
**Does:** reads what each step is, fills the next stone, then builds a series of their own from a start and a rule
**Win:** the next stone fills and its arc is drawn out of the number already reached
**Feels:** the jumps grow, and each one is exactly as big as the number the series has already reached
**Goal:** when the steps are not equal each step is looked at, and a start and a rule make a series of your own
**Observe:** The arcs of the last track were not equal, and each one was as long as the stone it started from.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- calls the pattern skip counting and adds the last step again, writing seventy-two after forty-eight. → the step finder labels the three arcs six, twelve and twenty-four, and arc compare stands them side by side, plainly not equal. Each arc is then drawn up out of the stone it starts from, as long as that stone's number. The child's seventy-two stays on its stone.
- in a series of their own made by adding the two numbers before, adds each new number to the start instead, so three, four, seven goes on to ten. → the rule maker draws two arcs into each new stone, one from each of the two stones before it. The child's ten stands on its stone with its own two arcs, from the start and from the seven. The rule maker's stone lands beside it with its arcs from the four and the seven.
**Misconception to break:** Adding the last step, 24, again and writing 72.

## Delight
The fourth phase, when a stone fills on a track whose steps are not equal. The number already reached lifts off its stone as a rod of counters, and a copy of the rod lays itself along the next jump, where it fits exactly. The next stone lands at the rod's far end with its number written on it. On a track built in the rule maker from two starts, the rod lifts from the stone two back instead, and fits the jump just as exactly. It runs as often as a finger likes, nothing is marked, and the voice says nothing while it happens.

**General miss line:** Label each jump again from the left, and look for the one that differs.

## Answer key
A series is a list in order and each item in it is a term. To carry one on, find the step from each term to the next and check it between every pair of neighbours, not only the first. A step can add or take away. Counting back in ones is counting back, and a series that counts down stops at zero. Letters step along the alphabet the way numbers step along counting, and each letter has its place, A as one up to Z as twenty-six. A number that pairs up with none left over is even and one that leaves one over is odd, and the counting numbers take turns, odd, even, odd, even. When the steps are not equal, look at what each step is: where each step is the number already reached, each number is added to itself, and where each number is the sum of the two numbers before it, the steps grow as well. A series of your own needs a start and a rule.

## Student controls
- **Step finder**: labels every jump arc with the step between two neighbours
- **Next stone**: takes the next term and checks it against the step
- **Zero stone**: stops a counting-down track at zero and lights when a step would pass it
- **Letter track**: steps letters along the alphabet with each letter's place beneath it
- **Pairing tray**: sets counters out two by two and stands any leftover on its own
- **Arc compare**: stands two jump arcs side by side to show whether the steps are equal
- **Rule maker**: builds a series from a chosen start and a chosen rule
- **Rule list**: offers add a step, take a step away, add the number to itself, and add the two before
