## What it is
**Description:** Line up sums and products one under another, read the pattern down the lines, and check it by working one line in full.
**Why:** Read the pattern down a board of sums and products, carry it on, and check it by working one line in full.
**Look:** A board of ruled lines with the answers down the right, jump labels in the margin, and a slate beside it for working one line out.
**Context:** Grade 4, and the hardest topic in the chapter. An adult sees number tricks. What is actually being taught is how a pattern read off a few lines comes to be trusted: by working one more line in full. The printed exercise on the facing page leaves out one line of a multiplication pattern, so the answers it does print seem to go up by six hundred each time, and a child who carries that step on writes three wrong answers under an instruction that says not to calculate. The tool puts a missing line back, so the jumps can be seen to grow, and makes working one line in full the habit.
**For the child:** Read down the answers. Look at the jump between each pair. If the jumps grow, find how they grow. Work one line out in full to check. Put back any line that is missing.
**Teaching notes:** Add three dates side by side on a wall calendar before the tool is opened, and check the total against three times the middle date. The page calls its two adding patterns a shortcut, but working them is no shorter than adding, so use them as patterns that always hold and as a way to check a sum. In the table of nine, past ninety, talk about the number of tens, nine in ninety-nine and ten in one hundred and eight; the page's commentary calls ten and eleven digits, and they are not. Then the exercise on the facing page. Its multiplication pattern prints five fives, twenty-five twenty-fives and thirty-five thirty-fives, and leaves out fifteen fifteens. With that line missing, the three printed answers seem to go up by six hundred each time, and a child who carries that step on writes three wrong answers, while the instruction forbids the calculation that would show it. Before anyone completes it, have the class work one line in full, forty-five forty-fives, with the two partial products the multiplication chapter taught, and compare it with the guess. Then put fifteen fifteens back, two hundred and twenty-five, and read the jumps again: two hundred, four hundred, six hundred. They grow, and they are not equal. Do not teach a rule for multiplying a number ending in five by itself. The chapter never teaches one, and supplying it would stop this being a plan for this chapter.

## Phases (what each mount shows and does; the words are in tool.tsx)

### 1 · Adding numbers that sit side by side
**Screen:** the side-by-side bay with two rods of counters, one a counter longer than the other
**Does:** adds the two numbers, then doubles the longer rod, takes one counter away and compares
**Win:** the doubled rod, one counter short, lies exactly along the two rods joined end to end
**Feels:** two rods side by side are nearly two of the bigger one, short by just one counter
**Goal:** two consecutive numbers make one less than twice the greater, and three make three times the middle
**Observe:** The doubled rod, one counter short, lay exactly along the two rods joined end to end.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- doubles the smaller number and takes one away, getting twenty-three for twelve and thirteen. → the rod of twelve is doubled and one counter taken off, and the result is laid under the two rods joined end to end, where it falls two counters short. The rod of thirteen, doubled with one counter taken off, is then laid beside it and lies exactly along. Both stay on the bay.
- for three numbers side by side, multiplies the greatest by three, writing seventy-eight for twenty-four, twenty-five and twenty-six. → three rods of the greatest are laid under the three numbers' rods, and they overhang by three counters. The longest of the three rods then gives one counter to the shortest, and three level rods of the middle number stand. The child's seventy-eight stays in the slot.
**Misconception to break:** Doubling the smaller number instead of the greater, and getting 24.

### 2 · Digits marching down a times table
**Screen:** the digit bay writing a times table down the board, tens in one column and ones in another
**Does:** reads down each column, says what it does, then writes the next line
**Win:** each column lights from top to bottom as its pattern is named
**Feels:** one column counts down while the other counts up, and past ninety the tens just keep counting
**Goal:** the ones digits of the table of nine count down while the number of tens counts up
**Observe:** Past ninety the tens column kept counting, nine, ten, eleven, and never went back to zero.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- reads the tens digit of one hundred and eight as zero, and decides the pattern has broken past ninety. → the digit bay splits one hundred and eight into ten tens and eight ones, and the tens column reads ten under nine. The tens then carry on, eleven and twelve, with the ones counting down beside them. The child's zero stays written beside the line.
- expects the tens of the table of five to go up on every line, as they do in the table of nine. → the digit bay lights the tens of the table of five two lines at a time: none, one, one, two, two. The ones take turns beside them, five then zero. The child's line stays on the board with the bay's split under it.
**Misconception to break:** Reading the tens digit of 108 as 0 and deciding that the pattern has broken.

### 3 · When the jumps grow
**Screen:** a board of running totals with the jump labels written and the next line empty
**Does:** reads how much each jump grows by, finds the next jump, then writes the next total
**Win:** the next jump label writes itself in and the next total lands under it
**Feels:** the jumps get bigger, but they get bigger in a steady way that can be carried on
**Goal:** when the jumps between answers grow, the next jump is found from how they grow
**Observe:** The growth margin read the same all the way down while the jumps kept getting bigger.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- uses the last jump again, writing fourteen and eighteen after one, three, six, ten. → the jump margin reads two, three and four, and the growth margin writes one and one beside them. The next jump label is left empty with its growth already written. The child's fourteen stays on the next line.
- adds the growth to the last total instead of to the last jump, writing eleven after ten. → the growth label is carried from its margin to the jump margin first, where it joins the last jump and makes the next one. Only then is the next jump carried to the total. The child's eleven stays on the next line beside it.
**Misconception to break:** Using the last jump, 4, again, and writing 14 and 18.

### 4 · A missing line can hide the pattern
**Screen:** a board with one line hidden, its answers seeming to go up by equal jumps, and the slate waiting
**Does:** guesses the next answer from the equal jumps, works that line in full on the slate, then puts the hidden line back
**Win:** the hidden line slides back in and the equal jumps split into jumps that grow
**Feels:** the jumps only looked equal, and one line worked in full was enough to show it
**Goal:** a line left out can make jumps that grow look equal, so one line is worked in full to check
**Observe:** Putting the hidden line back split one of two equal jumps into two smaller jumps that grew.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- carries on the equal jumps of twenty-four read off three lines, and writes seventy-three for nine nines. → the child's seventy-three stays on the next line, and the slate beside it lights. Nine nines is worked in full there, and the two answers stand side by side. The hide control then lights on the place between one and twenty-five. When three threes slides back in, the first jump label is rewritten where it stands as eight and sixteen, and the jumps are seen to grow.
- works the line in full, gets a different answer from the guess, and decides the working must be the slip. → the slate works the line again with every part of the working written in its column, and lands on the same answer. The guess and the worked line stand together on the board. The numbers being multiplied are then read down the board aloud, one, five, seven, and the skipped three is found between them.
**Misconception to break:** Carrying on the equal jumps of 24 without noticing that a line is missing.

## Delight
The first phase, when three numbers side by side are laid out as rods. The longest rod gives up its last counter, which walks across and joins the end of the shortest. The three rods then stand level, each as long as the middle one, and the three together are seen to make three of the middle number. Lay out any three numbers in a row and it runs again, as often as a finger likes. Nothing is marked, and the voice says nothing while it happens.

**General miss line:** Work that line out in full on the slate, then read the jumps again.

## Answer key
Numbers that sit side by side in counting are consecutive. Two consecutive numbers add up to one less than twice the greater, and three add up to three times the middle one. Working either pattern is no shorter than adding, so it is used as a pattern that always holds and as a way to check a sum. In the table of five the ones digit takes turns, five then zero. In the table of nine the ones digit counts down from nine to zero while the number of tens counts up, and past ninety the number of tens carries on, ten tens in one hundred and eight and eleven in one hundred and seventeen. In the table of ten the ones digit is always zero and the number of tens is the number being multiplied. When the answers down a board go up by jumps that grow, find how the jumps grow and carry that on. A pattern read off a few lines is checked by working one line in full, and a line left out can make jumps that grow look equal.

## Student controls
- **Line board**: holds one calculation to a line with the answers down the right
- **Jump labels**: write the jump between each pair of neighbouring answers
- **Jump growth**: writes how much each jump grows by
- **Side-by-side bay**: sets consecutive numbers out as rods of counters
- **Digit bay**: splits each answer of a times table into its tens and its ones
- **Work it out**: works any one line in full on a slate beside the board
- **Hide or put back**: takes a line out of the board or slides it back in
- **Next line**: takes the child's answer for the next line down

## Textbook (this topic)
**Pages:** 116, 117
**Key terms:** consecutive, times table, pattern, check
Sums of consecutive numbers and the digits of the tables of five and nine follow patterns, and a pattern read off a few lines is checked by working one line out. Two consecutive numbers add up to one less than twice the greater, and three add up to three times the middle one. The ones digits of a times table make patterns, and when the jumps between answers grow, the next jump comes from how they grow. Work one line out in full before trusting a pattern. Seven add eight is fifteen, which is one less than two eights. Three numbers in a row work too: they add up to three times the middle one. They count down, nine, eight, seven, all the way to zero, while the tens count up. In the table of five, the ones digits take turns, five then zero. By four, then by six. The jump grows by two each time, so the next jump is eight, and the next total is twenty. They seem to, but only because fifteen fifteens is missing. Put back two hundred and twenty-five, and the jumps are two hundred, four hundred and six hundred. Work one line out before trusting a pattern.
