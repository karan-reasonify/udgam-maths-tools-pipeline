## What it is
**Description:** Lay copies of one shape side by side, test for gaps and for overlaps, carry a tiling on, and make a pattern from everyday things.
**Why:** Lay copies of one shape side by side, test the patch for gaps and for overlaps, carry a tiling on, and make a pattern out of everyday things.
**Look:** A bench of dotted paper with a tray of shapes along its top edge, a gap finder and an overlap finder, and an object tray at one side.
**Context:** Grade 4, the recap. An adult sees tiles being laid. What is actually being settled is what the test for a tessellation is. The page's first sentence says without any gaps or overlaps, its definition then drops the overlaps, and nothing on the page says what a gap is, so a child who reads the test literally can reject a correct tiling because its outline is bumpy. The gap finder draws that line for the child: a gap is a space with tiles closed round it, and the uneven outer edge is only where the tiling stops.
**For the child:** Lay copies of one shape side by side. Tap the gap finder. Tap the overlap finder. A bumpy outside edge is not a gap. Add the next copy the same way. Make a pattern with things from the tray.
**Teaching notes:** Cut squares, triangles, hexagons and circles to matching sizes before the tool is opened, and let groups try to cover a sheet with one kind of shape. Keep both halves of the test from the first sentence the page prints: no gaps and no overlaps. The page's definition and its summary box keep the gaps and drop the overlaps, so lay one tile half over another and ask whether that counts. Then say what a gap is, because the page never does: a space with tiles closed round it on every side. Lay a patch of paper hexagons, ask whether the bumps round its outline are gaps, and settle that the outline is only where the tiling stops. Two of the three printed figures in the exercise have notched outlines, and a child reading the test literally rejects both, which are the two correct answers. The tiles in the third figure are drawn taller than they are wide; do not call them squares. The last statement of the self-assessment says the child can make tessellation patterns, and no printed exercise asks for one, so let every child make one on the bench and tick the statement against it. The four vocabulary words are number pattern, series, secret code and tessellation, and only the last is given a definition on the page; give the other three a meaning on the back of each card in the class's own words.

## Phases (what each mount shows and does; the words are in tool.tsx)

### 1 · Copies that fit with no gaps
**Screen:** an empty bench with the shape tray loaded and both finders waiting
**Does:** lays copies of one shape side by side, then runs the gap finder and the overlap finder
**Win:** both finders come back empty and the patch is named a tessellation
**Feels:** a patch passes only when nothing is left uncovered and nothing lies on top of anything else
**Goal:** copies of one shape set side by side with no gaps and no overlaps make a tessellation
**Observe:** The patch of overlapping squares covered everything, and still the overlap finder shaded it.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- lays copies overlapping to cover every bit of the bench, and names the patch a tessellation because nothing is left uncovered. → the overlap finder shades every doubled part in reddish purple hatching, while the gap finder comes back empty. The patch is not named, and the child's tiles stay exactly where they were laid. The voice asks whether anything is doubled.
- pushes the circles harder together to close the spaces between them, and calls the gaps gone once every circle touches its neighbours. → the gap finder fills the curved spaces between the circles, and they stay however the circles are pushed. A circle pushed further lands over its neighbour, and the overlap finder shades that part. No circle is ever squeezed out of shape to close a space.
**Misconception to break:** Checking only for gaps, and forgetting that tiles lying over one another also break a tessellation.

### 2 · A gap or only the edge
**Screen:** a patch of tiles with a bumpy outline and, in some patches, a space closed in the middle
**Does:** runs the gap finder and decides whether each space it shows is a gap or only the edge
**Win:** a closed-in space fills while the outer edge is traced plain and left empty
**Feels:** a gap is shut in by tiles all round, and the bumpy outside edge is only where the tiles stop
**Goal:** a gap is a space with tiles closed round it, and the bumpy outer edge is not a gap
**Observe:** The wash filled the space closed in between four circles, and ran off the bumpy outline of the hexagons.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- calls the notches round the outer edge gaps, and rejects a patch that fits perfectly inside. → the gap finder washes over the patch and fills nothing, and the outer edge is traced in its plain line and left empty. A tracer is run into one notch and straight out again to the outside. The child's verdict stays in the slot, and the patch is named.
- calls a space closed in near the outline only the edge, because it sits close to where the tiling stops. → the gap finder fills that space solid, because tiles close round it on every side. The tracer runs round inside it and never reaches the outside. The child's answer stays beside the patch.
**Misconception to break:** Calling the notches round the edge gaps, and rejecting a patch that fits perfectly inside.

### 3 · Carrying a tiling on
**Screen:** two or three copies of a shape laid on the dotted paper, with room for more to the right
**Does:** sets down the next copies one at a time where the tiling repeats
**Win:** each new copy snaps into place against the last and both finders stay empty
**Feels:** each copy fits against the last one in the same way, so the tiling can go on across the bench
**Goal:** a tiling is carried on by repeating the same shape the same way, with no gap and no overlap
**Observe:** Each copy the carry on control offered touched the last one exactly, and both finders stayed empty.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- sets every new triangle point up, leaving a space between each pair. → the gap finder fills the triangular space between each pair in reddish purple. Carry on then offers the next copy as a dashed outline turned point down, fitted against the last. The child's triangles stay where they were set.
- starts the next copy one dot too far along, leaving a thin space, or one dot too near, so it lies over the last. → both finders run, and a thin space fills with the wash or a doubled strip is hatched. Carry on's dashed outline stands where the tiling repeats, and the child's copy stays beside it. The dots between the starts of two copies are counted aloud.
**Misconception to break:** Setting every new triangle point up, which leaves a space between each pair.

### 4 · Patterns made from things around you
**Screen:** the object tray open beside an empty string and two empty rows
**Does:** lays out things in a pattern, then brackets the group that repeats
**Win:** the bracket lights every copy of the group along the string
**Feels:** any things at all make a pattern, once a group of them comes round again
**Goal:** everyday things make a pattern when a group of them repeats
**Observe:** The bracket lit every copy of the group along the string of flowers and leaves.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- counts every flower as a marigold, writing six for a string of marigold, leaf, leaf, rose repeated three times. → the bracket over the group of four lights every copy along the string, and the marigold of each copy is lit on its own. The roses stay unlit. The child's six stays in the slot beside the three lit marigolds.
- starts the third row of caps with cream, carrying the second row straight on from its last cap. → the rows are bracketed one under another, and the colour at the start of each row is lit down the left edge: red, cream, and the one still to come. The child's third row stays drawn under the bench's own.
**Misconception to break:** Counting every flower as a marigold, and writing six.

## Delight
The second phase, the moment the gap finder is tapped. A wash pours down over the patch from the top of the bench like water. It runs off every notch and bump of the outline and away off the bench, and settles only in the spaces with tiles closed round them. Those fill slowly from the bottom up, and the outline is left traced in its plain line with nothing in its notches. It pours as often as a finger likes, nothing is marked, and the voice says nothing while it happens.

**General miss line:** Run both finders again, and look at what each one shows.

## Answer key
Copies of one shape placed side by side with no gaps and no overlaps make a tessellation, and shapes that can do it are said to tessellate. Both conditions matter: shapes lying over one another do not make a tessellation even when nothing is left uncovered. A gap is a space with tiles closed round it on every side. The uneven outline round the outside of a tiled patch is not a gap; it is only where the tiling stops. Circles cannot tessellate, because gaps are left between them however they are pushed together. A tiling is carried on by repeating the same shape the same way, each new copy touching the last with no gap and no overlap. Everyday things make a pattern when a group of them repeats.
