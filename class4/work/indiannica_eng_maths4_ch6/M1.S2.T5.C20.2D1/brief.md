## What it is
**Description:** Turn one exact division over and read it from both ends, and never say either word without naming the number it belongs to.
**Why:** Read one exact division from both ends at once, and learn to say what each number is a factor of, or a multiple of.
**Look:** A turning card in the middle with both of its readings held side by side, a size rail under it and a word pad at the edge.
**Context:** Grade 4, and this is the topic where the chapter stops adding methods and says what its two words actually mean. An adult sees the same division written twice. What is being fixed is a word with nothing attached to it: a child who says six is a factor, full stop, has said nothing, because six is a factor of thirty and is not a factor of twenty. The printed page is loose here in exactly that way, calling a multiple the product of two or more factors without ever saying of what, so the gate is doing the work the wording does not.
**For the child:** Read the division on the card. Turn it over and read it the other way. Keep both readings up together. Never say factor or multiple without saying which number it belongs to. Check the rail: the factor is always the smaller one.
**Teaching notes:** This is the shortest serves list of the six tools, and that is a fact about the printed chapter rather than about the idea: the section that relates the two words sets no exercise of its own at all. Treat it as the most important twenty minutes anyway, because everything after it depends on the relation being one thing. Make the naming a spoken rule from the first minute. Nobody says factor or multiple in this classroom without saying of what, and the gate should be backed up by insisting on it out loud, because the printed wording will not back it up. Turn one division over and leave both readings up on the stage while the class says them; taking one down turns a single fact back into two things to remember. The size rail is worth doing wrong on purpose. Ask for the bigger number as the factor, let the rail refuse it, and get somebody to say why in their own words. Finish at the own list bay, where a number closes one list and opens the other, and use the word pad for the flashcards with a partner, asking the four words in a different order each time.

## Phases (what each mount shows and does; the words are in tool.tsx)

### 1 · Two words for the two ends of one division
**Screen:** a card showing one exact division with both faces still blank
**Does:** turns the card, reads each face, and holds the two up together
**Win:** both readings stand in one frame and each names the number it belongs to
**Feels:** one division, and both sentences were true before either of them was written
**Goal:** one exact division makes a factor fact and a multiple fact at once
**Observe:** One division produced two sentences and no second piece of work was done for the second one.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- says that six is a factor and stops there, without ever naming what it is a factor of. → the gate holds the sentence open with its empty slot lit and nothing marked. Two further cards are laid beside it, one where six divides thirty with nothing left over and one where six is tried on twenty and two are set outside the rows, and both are read out as sentences with their numbers in. The child's half sentence stays exactly where it was built.
- writes it the other way round, calling seventy-eight a factor of six. → the size rail is drawn to scale with six standing near its left hand end and seventy-eight at its right, and the factor slot at the smaller end turns the bigger number back with the two lengths left drawn. Both faces of the card are then held up in the frame together, each naming its own number, and the child's sentence stays in the writing bay beside them.
**Misconception to break:** Saying that 6 is a factor and stopping there, without naming what it is a factor of.

### 2 · A factor pair and the number it builds
**Screen:** the pair bay holding two numbers with the built number still empty
**Does:** lays the pair out as rows and reads off the number they come to
**Win:** the built number appears labelled as the multiple of both numbers in the pair
**Feels:** the number a pair builds belongs to both of them at once
**Goal:** the product of a factor pair is the multiple those factors belong to
**Observe:** Neither word would go into a sentence until a number had been named for it.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- calls fifty-seven a factor of three and of nineteen, turning the relation the wrong way round. → the pair bay lays three rows of nineteen out on the ruled board and the built card is read out as what those rows come to. The size rail is drawn under it to scale with all three numbers standing along it, and the factor slot at the smaller end takes each of the two small numbers in turn and turns the built number back. Nothing the child wrote is cleared.
- says the number belongs only to the bigger of the two that built it, because nineteen is nearer to fifty-seven. → both divisions are run on the ruled board one under the other, fifty-seven into three rows and fifty-seven into nineteen rows, each drawn genuinely equal with the leftover shelf beside it staying empty. The two sentences are then written under their own boards, and the child's single sentence stays where it was put with its slot for the second number lit.
**Misconception to break:** Calling 57 a factor of 3 and 19, which turns the relation the wrong way round.

### 3 · A factor is never the bigger one
**Screen:** the size rail with two numbers on it and the factor slot waiting
**Does:** tries the bigger number in the factor slot, then puts the smaller one in
**Win:** the bigger number is turned back on the rail and the smaller one settles in the slot
**Feels:** the rail says it before any dividing does
**Goal:** a bigger number cannot be a factor of a smaller one
**Observe:** The rail turned the bigger number back every time it was offered as the factor.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- answers yes because seven divides both of them, so the two numbers look related enough for either word. → the rail is drawn to scale with seven near the left hand end and both of the others further along. Seven is dropped into the factor slot against thirty-five and settles, then against twenty-one and settles again, and the true sentence is written out for each. Thirty-five is then dropped into the factor slot against twenty-one and is turned back, with its length on the rail left drawn past the other number.
- refuses to let twenty-one be a factor of twenty-one, because a factor was said to be smaller. → the rail is drawn with both markers landing on exactly the same point along it, and the division runs on the board as one row holding all twenty-one with the leftover shelf beside it drawn empty and read out as empty. The rule on the word pad opens with the equal half lit, and the child's refusal stays in the answer slot beside it.
**Misconception to break:** Thinking any two numbers can be a factor and a multiple as long as they are related somehow.

### 4 · A number stands at both ends of its own list
**Screen:** the own list bay with a penned factor list and a running track side by side
**Does:** drops one number into both lists and reads where it lands in each
**Win:** the same number lights at the closed end of the pen and at the start of the track
**Feels:** the same number shuts one list and opens the other
**Goal:** a number is its own greatest factor and its own smallest multiple
**Observe:** The same number lit at the shut end of one list and at the first step of the other.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- leaves the number out of both of its own lists, because its own lists sound like lists of other numbers. → the own list bay lights the shut end of the pen and the first step of the track at the same instant, with the same number card standing in each. The two claims are then run in front of the child: the number divided by itself, drawn as one row with nothing outside it, and one step of the number, drawn landing on it. The child's two lists stay exactly as they were written.
- says a number cannot be in both lists at once, because factor and multiple sound like opposites, and drops it from whichever was written second. → the pen and the track are drawn side by side with the number lit in both and a hairline joining the two marks. The pen is read from its left post to its wall, ending on the number, and the track is read from its first step outward, starting on the number and leaving the frame. Nothing is removed from either list.
**Misconception to break:** Assuming a number cannot appear in both of its own lists, because the two words sound opposite.

## Delight
The first phase, at the turn. The card goes over once and the two faces do not take turns: the first one lifts clear and slides to the left of the frame while the second rises into the right of it, and for the length of that movement the division itself is drawn joining them, the same numbers running through both sentences. Drag either reading and the other comes with it, hinged, because neither was found without the other. Try to lift one out of the frame on its own and it will not come, and the hairline between the two lights instead of any warning being spoken.

**General miss line:** Say it again, and this time say which number it belongs to.

## Answer key
If one number divides another exactly, the first is a factor of the second and the second is a multiple of the first. That is one fact read from two ends, and no second piece of work is needed to get the other half. The product of a factor pair is the multiple those two factors belong to. A factor is always less than or equal to the number it divides, so a bigger number can never be a factor of a smaller one. A number is the greatest factor of itself and the smallest multiple of itself. Always say what a number is a factor of, or a multiple of, because neither word means anything on its own.

## Textbook (this topic)
**Pages:** 66, 67
**Key terms:** factor, multiple, factor pair, product
Factor and multiple are shown to be two names for the two ends of the same exact division. When one number divides another exactly, the smaller is a factor of the bigger, and the bigger is a multiple of the smaller. A factor pair multiplies to make a multiple of both. A number is its own greatest factor and its own smallest multiple. Six is the factor and thirty is the multiple. One exact division makes both true at once. The smaller number is the factor and the bigger one is the multiple, and no second fact is needed. Their multiple. The product of any factor pair is the number those two factors belong to. Multiply the pair and you are looking at their multiple. No. Nine cannot divide six and leave nothing over. A factor is always less than or equal to the number it divides. That is why factors run out and multiples do not. At the top of one and the bottom of the other. Eight is the greatest factor of eight and the smallest multiple of eight. The same number closes one list and opens the other.
