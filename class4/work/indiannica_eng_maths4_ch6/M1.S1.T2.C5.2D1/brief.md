## What it is
**Description:** Climb a number one trial at a time, keep every rung that fails, and watch each exact division hand you two factors.
**Why:** Find every factor of a number by dividing by each whole number in turn, and find out why the run must not skip one.
**Look:** A ladder of rungs up the middle with a tray of countable things above it, a shelf for whatever will not fit, and a fenced pen of factor cards beside it.
**Context:** Grade 4, the first working topic of this chapter. An adult sees a child dividing over and over. What is actually being settled is why the run has to be unbroken: a number never tried is a number that cannot be ruled out, and a list built by trying whichever divisors came to mind is a collection rather than a list. The printed run that this topic is built from promises serial order and then leaves one number out, which is exactly the move the ladder refuses.
**For the child:** Put a number in the tray. Step on every rung from the bottom up. Do not skip one. If something is left over, leave it on the shelf where you can see it. Each rung that comes out gives you two factors, so put both in the pen. Stop when an answer is one you already have.
**Teaching notes:** Share a real number of counters out onto plates by hand before the tool is opened, and make the leftovers the thing everybody looks at. Then use the tool for what paper will not do: stop at a trial that fails and refuse to move on until somebody has said, out loud, why the row still gets written. That is the whole idea of the topic, and the chapter's own worked run is missing it, so a class reading the page will not meet it unless it is staged. Keep the no skipping control locked for the first few numbers and let a child try to jump a rung on purpose. When a rung lights, ask how many factors it just gave before anybody touches the pen: the answer is two, every single time, and a child who says one is writing half the list. The tick and cross bay is worth a few minutes of its own, because judging one pair is a single division and children slow it down by listing factors they were never asked for. Finish by asking how they know the list is complete. Because the answers started repeating is the right answer; because it looks long enough is not.

## Phases (what each mount shows and does; the words are in tool.tsx)

### 1 · What makes a number a factor
**Screen:** a full tray with two rungs offered, one that comes out and one that does not
**Does:** steps on each rung in turn and looks at what is left on the shelf
**Win:** one rung lights with level rows and the other leaves its remainder standing outside them
**Feels:** a trial that leaves something over has answered, and the thing it left is the answer
**Goal:** a factor divides the number and leaves nothing over
**Observe:** The trials that failed stayed on the ladder with their leftovers standing beside them.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- calls six a factor of twenty because it very nearly went in, and brushes the two that would not fit off the side of the tray. → the sharing runs again at walking pace and the two are carried to the leftover shelf outside the rows, at full strength and countable, where they stay. The six rows are then counted out one at a time, three in each, and the board is read as eighteen; the tray it came from is read as twenty. The child's verdict stays in the answer slot the whole time and nothing is marked.
- shares the two left over out as well, splitting them between the rows so that every row comes out the same. → the two are drawn whole and set on the shelf, and the rows are rebuilt from the tray with only whole things in them. The tool asks how many whole things went into each row and the board answers three, with the two still standing outside; the four plate sharing is then laid beside it with its shelf empty, and the two boards are left standing together.
**Misconception to break:** Calling 6 a factor because it nearly goes in, and ignoring the 2 still standing outside.

### 2 · Working up through the numbers in order
**Screen:** the ladder from the foot upward with every rung blank and waiting
**Does:** climbs one rung at a time and tries to jump one, which is refused
**Win:** every rung carries either level rows or a leftover, and no rung is blank
**Feels:** a run with a hole in it looks exactly like a run without one
**Goal:** the run is unbroken, and a trial that fails is still written down
**Observe:** The ladder would not open a rung while a lower one was still blank.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- jumps from two straight to four, because three has an odd look to it and the run is long. → the ladder refuses to open the fourth rung and lights the third, naming it and giving nothing else away. When the third rung is taken it comes out at once, six rows of three with the shelf empty, and two cards drop into the pen. The pen is then read out with those two cards lifted and standing to one side, so that the list the skip would have produced and the list the run produced are up together.
- keeps only the rungs that came out and leaves the failed trials off the paper altogether. → two ladders are drawn side by side, one carrying every rung from one upward and one carrying only the rungs that lit. Both are read out and both give the same six numbers. A rung is then quietly taken out of the second ladder and it is read again: nothing on it has changed and nothing anywhere says a trial is missing. The first ladder keeps its blank rung lit.
**Misconception to break:** Jumping from 2 to 4 because 3 looks unlikely, which leaves a gap the list cannot account for.

### 3 · Every exact division hands you two
**Screen:** a rung that has just come out, with the empty pen beside it
**Does:** reads the number stepped on and the number in each row, and drops both into the pen
**Win:** two cards land in the pen from one rung and the pen count goes up by two
**Feels:** one sharing, and two cards land in the pen together
**Goal:** one exact division puts two numbers into the list
**Observe:** One exact division put two cards in the pen, never one.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- writes down the number stepped on and lets the number in each row go, so the pen fills one card at a time. → the board is turned a quarter turn in front of the child, the same twenty-seven counters reading as nine rows of three, and the ladder steps itself onto the ninth rung to show that rung coming out on the same tray. The card the child did put in the pen stays exactly where it was put, and the one that was let go is drawn lying on the board beside its row with the pen wall open beside it.
- drops two cards in for a rung where something was left over, because a division was still done and two numbers still came out of it. → the leftover lights on its shelf and is counted out. The rows are counted beside it, four rows of six, and the board is read as twenty-four against a tray of twenty-seven. The two cards spring back out of the pen with nothing marked, the pen count drops back to what it was, and the rung is left standing as a failure with its three still outside the rows.
**Misconception to break:** Writing down only the number you divided by, and losing the answer it gave.

### 4 · Knowing when to stop dividing
**Screen:** the ladder well up with the pen already holding most of the list
**Does:** carries on climbing until a card lands on one already in the pen
**Win:** the repeat light comes on, the ladder stops and the pen is read out as finished
**Feels:** the moment an answer is one you have already written, there is nothing new left to find
**Goal:** the list is finished when the answers repeat what is already there
**Observe:** The pen stopped filling at the moment a card landed on one already inside.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- stops after a couple of failures in a row and calls the list complete, because the pen already looks respectably full. → the ladder carries on from where the child stopped, one rung at a time, with every rung that was never climbed lit in order as it is reached. The pen the child left is kept drawn exactly as it stood, beside the pen the finished run builds, and the two are read against each other card by card so that the cards the early stop never had are the only ones standing alone. Nothing is marked.
- keeps climbing past the repeat all the way to the number itself, writing every pair down a second time. → the repeat light stays on and the ladder is allowed to climb. Every rung above the repeat does come out, and every card it sends lands on one already lying in the pen and is drawn settling on top of it rather than beside it. The pen count in the corner is held in view throughout and never moves, and the whole of the child's extra work stays drawn on the ladder.
**Misconception to break:** Carrying on to the number itself, writing every pair twice and calling it a longer list.

## Delight
The third phase, at the instant a rung comes out. The rows settle level on the board and the two cards leave together, one lifting off the rung the child stepped on and one lifting off the end of a row, and they travel side by side along a drawn path into the pen and land as a linked pair. Drag either card back out and the other follows it, because the two came from one division and neither of them was found on its own. The count in the corner goes up by two on the way in and down by two on the way back, the rows on the board never move, and the voice says nothing at all while it happens.

**General miss line:** Share them out again, slowly. What is standing outside the rows?

## Answer key
A factor of a number divides it and leaves nothing over. To find every factor, divide by one, then by two, then by three, and keep going up through the whole numbers without skipping any. A division that does not come out exactly means that number is not a factor, and the row is still written down. Every division that does come out gives two factors, the number divided by and the answer. Stop when the answers are numbers already on the list, because nothing new can come after that.

## Student controls
- **Number tray**: holds the number being taken apart as that many countable things
- **Step a rung**: shares the tray into that many equal rows and lights the rung if it comes out
- **Leftover shelf**: sets whatever will not fit outside the rows, countable and at full strength
- **No skipping**: names the rung that has not been tried and will not climb past it
- **Factor pen**: takes the two cards each exact division gives, the divisor and the answer
- **Repeat light**: comes on when a card lands on one already in the pen
- **Tick and cross bay**: runs the single trial that settles one pair of numbers

## Textbook (this topic)
**Pages:** 63, 64
**Key terms:** factor, divide exactly, remainder, quotient
A factor is named, and the factors of a number are found by dividing by one number after another. A factor divides a number exactly, with nothing left over. Divide by every whole number in order from one, and skip none. Each exact division gives two factors, and the list is finished when the answers start to repeat. Three goes in exactly and leaves nothing over, so three is a factor of twelve. Five does not, so it is not. Leaving nothing over is the whole test, and there is no other. Start at one and divide by every whole number in turn. Do not skip any. Working in order is what makes the list complete, because a number you never tried is a number you cannot rule out. Two of them. Four divides thirty-six exactly, and so does nine. The number you divided by and the answer you got are both factors, so the list grows two at a time. When the numbers coming out are ones you have already written down. Six divided into thirty-six gives six, and after that every answer repeats a factor you have. There is nothing new left to find.
