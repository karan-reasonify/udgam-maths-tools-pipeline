## What it is
**Description:** Find the pairs that multiply to the number, work up from one without missing any, and watch the list shut at both ends.
**Why:** Find the factors of a number by hunting the pairs that make it, and find out why that hunt always comes to an end.
**Look:** A long pen with a post at each end, two dials underneath, and a ruled board between them where a pair is laid out as rows.
**Context:** Grade 4, and this topic does the same job as the one before it by a quicker route while adding the idea the chapter actually wants. An adult sees multiplication facts being hunted. What is being built is the claim that a factor list is finite: it can be counted, because every factor is penned between one and the number and there are only so many whole numbers in that stretch. That claim is what the next topic needs, because multiples do the opposite and a child who has not watched one list shut cannot be shown the other one failing to.
**For the child:** Put up the two posts first, one and the number itself. Move the first dial up from one, a step at a time. Lay each pair out as rows and count them. Pin the pairs that make the number. Stop when a pair comes back the other way round, then count what is in the pen.
**Teaching notes:** Put both end posts up before the first pair is hunted, and say why they are there: every factor has to stand between them, and that single fact is what makes the list end. Otherwise finite arrives as a word to be remembered rather than a thing that was watched happening. Drive the first dial up one step at a time and refuse to let anybody jump, even when the class is certain a number cannot work, because the certainty is the thing being tested and it is wrong often enough to matter. Make a point of a number where several trials in a row fail, so that a run of failures stops looking like a mistake. The mirror light is the moment to slow right down: ask what is new about the pair that just came up, and get the answer that it is one they already have, written back to front. Finish on the count. A class that can say how many factors a number has, and why there are no more, is ready for the next topic, where the same question about multiples has no answer at all.

## Phases (what each mount shows and does; the words are in tool.tsx)

### 1 · Two numbers that make the number
**Screen:** a ruled board with one pair already laid out as rows and the pen empty
**Does:** counts the rows, counts what is in each, and pins the pair
**Win:** the pair pins into the pen and both numbers are read out as factors
**Feels:** two numbers, laid out as rows, and the rows come to exactly the number
**Goal:** two numbers whose rows come to the number are a factor pair
**Observe:** Both ends of the pen were standing before a single pair had been hunted.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- counts the rows and calls that the factor, leaving the number in each row unnamed. → the ruled board is turned a quarter turn in front of the child and the same counters are read the other way round, so that the number in each row becomes the number of rows without a single counter moving. Both numbers are then lifted into a linked card with the bar drawn between them, and the card is pinned whole.
- pins a pair that comes close, four rows of five for twenty-two, and lets the two spare counters sit on the end of a row. → the pair is laid out on the ruled board as exactly four rows of exactly five, with the two spare counters standing outside the rows on the same surface at full strength. The rows are counted to twenty and the gap between the last row and the right post is drawn, so the pair is seen to fall short rather than to be nearly right. The child's pair stays where it was put.
**Misconception to break:** Calling only the number of rows a factor and leaving out the number in each row.

### 2 · Going up in order, and knowing when the pairs repeat
**Screen:** the dials at one with the pen empty and every first number untried
**Does:** drives the first dial up one step at a time, pinning or failing each
**Win:** the mirror light comes on at the first repeat and the run stops there
**Feels:** the pairs come back to meet you, and that is the signal to stop
**Goal:** the first number climbs from one, and the repeat is the stop signal
**Observe:** Every pair that pinned had both of its numbers inside the two posts.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- drives the first dial straight past four, five and six because none of them looks at all likely. → the pen refuses the pair and the lowest blank place on the run lights and is named. Each skipped value is then laid out on the ruled board in turn, with its rows drawn and its shortfall or its overshoot drawn beside them, and every one of those places keeps its mark along the run afterwards, so the run reads as unbroken rather than as tidy.
- pins the pair that came back as though it were a new one, so the same two numbers stand in the pen twice. → the new card is drawn settling directly against its mirror rather than into a place of its own, and the mirror light fills. The pen is then counted out card by card with the count standing in the corner, and the count stops where it stopped before. Both cards stay drawn, one against the other.
**Misconception to break:** Skipping the numbers that look unlikely, which leaves a gap the list cannot account for.

### 3 · The two factors every number has
**Screen:** the pen empty with both end posts standing and no pair laid out yet
**Does:** pins the outer pair from the posts alone, without touching the board
**Win:** one and the number itself pin into the pen straight from the posts
**Feels:** two of them are already standing there before any work is done
**Goal:** one and the number itself are factors of it before any work is done
**Observe:** The pairs began coming back the other way round instead of bringing anything new.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- leaves one and the number itself out of the pen, because neither of them took any work. → both posts light and each is dropped through the board in front of the child: one row of fifty-seven, with nothing standing outside it, and fifty-seven rows of one, with nothing standing outside that either. The two cards pin, the pen is counted out with them in and then read again with them lifted to one side, and the two counts stand together.
- puts one into the pen but refuses the number itself, because a number cannot be a factor of itself. → the number is laid on the board as a single row holding all of it, and the leftover shelf beside the board is drawn empty and read out as empty. The rule on the word pad is opened to the half that says less than OR EQUAL TO, with the equal half lit, and the right post is read out as the greatest factor there is. Nothing the child pinned is moved.
**Misconception to break:** Thinking a number has to be tested before anything at all can be said about its factors.

### 4 · Why a factor list always ends
**Screen:** a full pen with both ends shut and the stretch between the posts shaded
**Does:** counts the pinned pairs and looks at where every one of them sits
**Win:** the pen reads out a finished count and nothing stands outside the two posts
**Feels:** you can count them, because there is nowhere else for a factor to be
**Goal:** every factor lies between one and the number, so the list can be counted
**Observe:** Reading the pen out and counting the pen out were two different answers.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- says the factors could carry on for ever, the way the multiples on the track did. → both posts light, the stretch between them is shaded, and the whole numbers inside it are counted out along the run. A number past the right post is then dropped onto the board and the rows it would need are drawn, running off the board without ever reaching the number; it is turned back at the post with the overshoot left drawn. The track from the topic before opens under the pen at the same time, leaving the frame with no end mark.
- answers how many factors by reading the list out, and never says a number at all. → the question is read again with the words how many lit, and the cards in the pen are counted out one at a time with the count climbing in the corner as each is touched. The child's list stays exactly where it was written and the count is drawn underneath it, so the two answers stand together and neither replaces the other.
**Misconception to break:** Believing a bigger number could still turn up as a factor somewhere past the number itself.

## Delight
The second phase, when the first dial reaches the pair that comes back. The new card travels along the pen looking for its place and settles directly against the pair already pinned, and the two are drawn as mirrors of each other: the bar between the numbers is the same bar, and the numbers read the same two either way round. The mirror light fills at that instant, the dials come to a stop on their own, and the whole run of first numbers along the foot lights from one up to where the finger got to, with every failed place still marked. Drive the dial on and the pen simply keeps handing back mirrors, one after another, and the count never moves.

**General miss line:** Lay that pair out as rows and count them. What do they come to?

## Answer key
A factor pair is two numbers that multiply to give the number, and both of them are factors of it. Work the first number up from one and try every value in turn. When a pair repeats one already found, written the other way round, every factor has been found. One is a factor of every number and every number is a factor of itself, so those two are known before any work starts. No factor is ever greater than the number it belongs to, so the whole list lies between one and the number, which is why it ends and can be counted. We say the factors of a number are finite.

## Student controls
- **End posts**: puts up one at the left and the number itself at the right before any work starts
- **Pair dials**: chooses the two numbers of a pair, the first driven up from one
- **Lay it out**: draws the pair as rows on a ruled board so it can be counted
- **Pin the pair**: pins a pair whose rows come to the number into the pen
- **Nothing missed**: refuses a pair while a smaller first number is still untried
- **Mirror light**: comes on when a pair is one already pinned, the other way round
- **Count them**: shuts both ends and reads the pen out as a finished count of factors

## Textbook (this topic)
**Pages:** 64, 65
**Key terms:** factor pair, multiply, finite, greatest factor
Factors are found by pairing two numbers that multiply to give the number, and the list is shown to be finite. Both are factors of eighteen, and together they are a factor pair. Any two numbers that multiply to give eighteen are a pair of its factors. Finding the pairs finds the factors. No. Two times nine is already on your list, written the other way round. Once the pairs start repeating, every factor has been found. Work up from one and that moment tells you to stop. Yes. One is a factor of every number, because one divides everything exactly. And every number is a factor of itself. For most numbers those are the smallest and the biggest of its factors. No. Every factor is somewhere between one and the number itself, and there are only so many whole numbers in that stretch. So the list stops. We say the factors of a number are finite, which means you can count them.
