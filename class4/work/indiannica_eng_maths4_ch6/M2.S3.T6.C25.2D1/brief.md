## What it is
**Description:** Fill two rings and look at what falls in both, then settle a multiple with one division instead of two whole lists.
**Why:** Find what two numbers have in common, and learn the single division that settles a multiple without writing any list at all.
**Look:** Two rings of cord overlapping, filled with lists that stop or with tracks that do not, and a divide gate standing beside them.
**Context:** Grade 4, and two numbers are put side by side for the first time. An adult sees lists being compared. What is actually being carried is the difference from the topics before: comparing two lists that stop gives a shared list that stops, and comparing two lists that run on gives one that runs on, so common factors can be counted and common multiples cannot. The divide gate beside it is the shortcut the chapter offers once the lists have done their teaching, and it is the same single division that settles a factor question and a multiple question at once.
**For the child:** Put a number in each ring and fill both lists. Move anything in both into the middle. Switch to multiples and run the tracks on. Then use the gate: divide the bigger number by the smaller one and look at what is left over.
**Teaching notes:** Lay two string rings on the floor first, hand out number cards, and let the children put themselves in the right ring or in the overlap. Ask what the overlap holds before naming it, and ask whether the overlap could ever be empty; the answer, that one is always in it, is worth arriving at rather than being told. Then switch to multiples and ask the same question again, and let the class notice that this time the overlap keeps filling and there is no greatest number in it. Holding those two answers together is the whole topic. The divide gate comes last and comes as a shortcut, never as the first method, because a child who divides before ever comparing two lists has the answer and not the idea. When a division leaves something over, stop and get both statements said aloud, because they are one fact and children treat them as two. The closing board game is this tool as a race: a throw names a divisor, and one division decides whether the cell you want is legal.

## Phases (what each mount shows and does; the words are in tool.tsx)

### 1 · Numbers that divide both
**Screen:** two empty rings with both factor lists waiting to be filled
**Does:** fills each ring and moves whatever belongs to both into the overlap
**Win:** the overlap holds exactly the common factors and each one divides both numbers
**Feels:** the middle holds only what stood in both lists, and one is always standing in it
**Goal:** a common factor is a number standing in both factor lists
**Observe:** Something was standing in the overlap before either list had been fully read.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- sweeps every factor of both numbers into the middle, so the overlap holds the two lists joined together. → each card is picked up again and tested against both numbers as it crosses, the division for each drawn on the ruled board beside the rings with its leftover shelf showing. A card that divides only one of the two is set back into that ring rather than removed from the stage, and the middle is read out again once every card has been tested.
- leaves one out of the middle because it goes into everything and so does not feel like an answer. → one is dropped through the gate twice, twenty-four divided by one and thirty-two divided by one, each drawn on the board as a single row with the leftover shelf beside it empty. The card then travels into the middle and takes the shared colour like any other, and the middle is counted out with it in.
**Misconception to break:** Listing every factor of both numbers together, instead of keeping only the ones in both.

### 2 · Meeting points that never stop
**Screen:** the rings turned into two tracks leaving the frame with no end drawn
**Does:** runs both tracks on and watches the shared band catch new numbers
**Win:** the band keeps catching numbers as the tracks run and no last meeting is drawn
**Feels:** one overlap kept filling and the other one shut
**Goal:** two lists of multiples keep meeting, so the shared list has no end
**Observe:** The factor overlap filled and then stopped, and the multiple band never stopped.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- writes six, twelve and eighteen and reports that those are all the common multiples there are. → both tracks are run on past the end of the child's lists, one step at a time, and the band catches twenty-four, then thirty, then thirty-six, each meeting drawn as two flags sliding together. The three numbers the child wrote keep their own cards in the band, and the tracks and the band all leave the frame at the right with no end mark drawn on any of them.
- carries the answer back the other way and says the common factors must be infinite too, since the common multiples are. → the rings switch to factor mode on the very same two numbers. Two and three fill with their own factors, one and two in the first and one and three in the second, each penned between a wall at one and a wall at the number, and exactly one card travels into the overlap and the middle shuts. The multiple band is left running underneath it at the same time, so the shut middle and the open one are on the stage together.
**Misconception to break:** Thinking the meetings stop where the written lists stop, rather than where the lists stop being written.

### 3 · One division instead of two lists
**Screen:** the divide gate loaded with a bigger and a smaller number and the rings dimmed
**Does:** runs the single division and reads the answer with nothing left over
**Win:** the gate returns an exact answer and the rings stay unused beside it
**Feels:** one division, and no list at all
**Goal:** one division settles whether a bigger number is a multiple of a smaller one
**Observe:** The gate answered without either ring being filled at all.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- writes out the whole list of multiples of nine looking for a hundred and eight somewhere in it. → the gate runs its single division on the ruled board beside the list the child is building, twelve rows of nine with nothing on the leftover shelf, and it has finished by the time the fourth line of the list is written. The child's list is left standing and is carried on to the twelfth line afterwards, so that both routes reach the same place and only one of them is short.
- divides the smaller number by the bigger one, because the smaller number is the one being asked about. → the gate lays both ways round on the board one above the other. The smaller number divided by the bigger one puts no complete row on the board at all and sets the whole of it on the leftover shelf; the bigger divided by the smaller draws twelve equal rows with the shelf empty. Both boards stay drawn and nothing is marked.
**Misconception to break:** Writing out the whole list of multiples of 8 to look for 112 in it.

### 4 · What a remainder rules out
**Screen:** the gate holding a division that does not come out, with the leftover showing
**Does:** counts what is left over and writes both statements the division settled
**Win:** the leftover stands outside the rows and both statements are written under it
**Feels:** the leftover answered two questions at once
**Goal:** anything left over settles the factor question and the multiple question at once
**Observe:** What was left over on the shelf was written into two statements, not one.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- sees a small leftover and calls seven nearly a factor of fifty-five. → the six are drawn on the shelf outside the rows at full strength and counted one at a time. The seven rows are counted beside them and read as forty-nine, against a tray of fifty-five. The gate then asks what would have to be true of the tray for seven to be a factor of it, and the child's verdict stays in the slot throughout.
- writes the multiple statement and leaves the factor one unsaid, as though the division had settled one question and not two. → say both opens under the shelf and writes the two statements into one frame, with the same division lit behind each of them in turn, and with the leftover still standing outside the rows above them. The statement the child did write stays exactly where it was written and the second is drawn beside it rather than over it.
**Misconception to break:** Deciding that 9 is nearly a factor because the leftover is small.

## Delight
The second phase, once both tracks are running. A number that is caught by the band is not simply coloured in: the flag on the upper track and the flag on the lower track are drawn sliding towards each other until they meet in the band, where they become one card in the shared colour, and the two steps that brought them there stay drawn behind it. Run the tracks further and it happens again, and again, with the gap between the meetings always the same length. A child who holds keep going down long enough sees the whole stage scroll through meeting after meeting, and the shared band leaves the frame as open at its right hand end as the two tracks do.

**General miss line:** Fill both lists again and lift across only what is standing in each of them.

## Answer key
A common factor of two numbers divides both of them exactly, and it is found by writing both factor lists and taking what appears in each. One is a common factor of every pair of numbers, because one is a factor of everything. A common multiple appears in both lists of multiples, and because both of those lists run on for ever they keep meeting for ever, so there are infinitely many common multiples and no greatest one. To find out whether a bigger number is a multiple of a smaller one you do not need either list: divide the bigger by the smaller and look at what is left. Nothing over means it is a multiple, and the same division says the smaller number is a factor of the bigger.

## Student controls
- **Two rings**: gives each chosen number a ring of its own, overlapping in the middle
- **Third ring**: opens a ring for a third number when the work asks for one
- **Factor mode**: fills each ring with a factor list penned between walls at both ends
- **Multiple mode**: turns the rings into tracks running off the edge with no end drawn
- **Into the overlap**: moves anything belonging to both lists into the shared middle
- **Divide gate**: runs one division on a bigger and a smaller number and shows the remainder
- **Say both**: writes the factor statement and the multiple statement that one division settled

**Key terms:** common factor, common multiple, shared, remainder
Common factors and common multiples are found from two lists, and one division tests whether a number is a multiple. Numbers in both factor lists are common factors, and they run out. Numbers in both multiple lists are common multiples, and they never stop. One division tells you whether one number is a multiple of another, and anything left over says it is not. One, two, four and eight. Those are the common factors of the two numbers, and each one divides both of them exactly. Any number in only one list is not common.
