## What it is
**Description:** Decide what divides a number by looking at it, add every digit including the zeros, and count who is sharing before you test.
**Why:** Find out what divides a number by looking at it instead of dividing it, and find out what that answer does not cover.
**Look:** A number laid out one digit to a cell with four test lamps below it, a sum rail underneath and a sharing bay at the side.
**Context:** Grade 4, the closing topic. An adult sees four rules being memorised. What is actually being taught is what a test does and does not settle. A test answers about the divisor it was run for and about no other, and this chapter teaches four of them and stops, so a number that passes all four has not been fully examined. The printed sharing question turns on exactly that: it shows a number dividing by five and concludes that nothing will be left over when it is shared out, without counting the person doing the sharing, and that is two different mistakes stacked on one line.
**For the child:** Lay the number out, one digit to a cell. For two, five and ten, cover everything but the last digit. For three, bring every cell down onto the rail, zeros and all, and test the total. Before you test a sharing question, count everyone who is sharing.
**Teaching notes:** Sort real number cards into four labelled trays by hand before the tool is opened, and then check two or three of them by actually dividing, so the tests are seen to be true rather than taken on trust. Cover the number with a hand for the first few tests for two, five and ten, leaving only the last digit showing, and ask whether the answer is still available. It is, and that surprises them. For the test for three, insist that every digit comes down, zeros included, and say why out loud: a zero adds nothing to the total, but a child who skips a zero learns to skip, and the next number has something else in that place. The printed demonstration of this rule adds three of a four digit number's digits, and the very next exercise hands over a five digit number with two zeros in the middle, so the habit is tested immediately. The last phase is the one to leave time for. Count the sharers out loud, one person at a time, and always ask who is missing from the count. The answer is usually the person holding the tray. Then ask what the test actually proved, and accept only an answer that names the divisor it was run for.

## Phases (what each mount shows and does; the words are in tool.tsx)

### 1 · Reading the last digit
**Screen:** a long number with the shutter ready and three of the lamps lit
**Does:** drops the shutter, reads the one digit still showing and picks a lamp
**Win:** the answer lands with the rest of the number still covered by the shutter
**Feels:** the whole number covered up, and the answer still there to be read
**Goal:** the ones digit alone decides the tests for two, five and ten
**Observe:** For two, for five and for ten the whole number was covered and the answer was still available.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- adds the digits up to test a number for two, because adding the digits is the rule that took the most learning. → the shutter slides across and leaves only the ones cell showing, and the verdict is given from that one cell while the sum rail stays stowed and closed. The total the child built stays where it was written, and the lamp for three lights beside the lamp for two so that the rule and the test it belongs to are drawn together. Nothing is cleared.
- reads the first digit instead of the last, because a number is read from the left hand end. → the cells are named from the RIGHT hand end, ones first, with each name written under its own cell, and the shutter is drawn closing from the left so that the cell still showing is plainly the one the test was chosen for. The number is then divided out in full on the board beside it, and the child's verdict stays in the slot with the leading digit still lit where they were looking.
**Misconception to break:** Adding up all the digits for the test for two, which is the rule for three.

### 2 · Adding every digit for three
**Screen:** a number holding two zeros with the sum rail open and empty below it
**Does:** drags each cell down onto the rail in order, zeros and all, then tests the total
**Win:** the rail closes only when every cell has come down, and the total runs through the test
**Feels:** a zero adds nothing, and skipping it loses everything that comes after it
**Goal:** every digit goes into the total, zeros included
**Observe:** The rail refused to close while one cell was still up, even when that cell held a zero.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- leaves the zero out of the total, because a zero adds nothing to it. → the rail will not close and the skipped cell is handed back lit, with its zero drawn at full size where it stands and the gap on the rail left open at its full width. Once every cell has come down the total is written and sent back up through the lamp. The same run is then laid on a five digit number holding two zeros, where the cells either side of them are the ones a skipping finger takes with it.
- adds the digits, writes the total and stops there, reporting the total as the answer. → the total the rail built rises back up into the cells as a number in its own right and the lamp for three lights again above it, asking to be pressed a second time. The first total stays drawn where the rail wrote it, and the verdict that comes out of the second press is written under the number the child started from.
**Misconception to break:** Adding 4, 6 and 8 only, because the zeros look as though they can be left out.

### 3 · What the tests do not cover
**Screen:** a number that has passed all four lamps, with the empty slot beside them
**Does:** asks the empty slot a question and is shown it was never taught
**Win:** the empty slot stays dark and named, and the child says nothing is known about it
**Feels:** four lamps lit, and the slot beside them still dark
**Goal:** four tests are taught here and they settle nothing else
**Observe:** A number that passed all four lamps left the fifth slot exactly as dark as it began.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- says a number that passed the test for three must divide by six as well, because six is made out of two and three. → the fifth slot lights and stays dark beside the four lamps. The number is divided by six on the ruled board, its rows drawn and three set on the leftover shelf outside them at full strength, and the lamp for two is pressed on the same number and does not fill. The child's claim stays in the answer slot with the division standing under it.
- treats a number that has passed every lamp on the stage as a number that has been fully examined. → both numbers that passed all four are divided by four side by side on one board, one coming out with the shelf empty and the other leaving two standing outside the rows at full strength. The four lamps stay full above both of them the whole time, and the fifth slot stays dark and named. Nothing is marked either way.
**Misconception to break:** Treating a number that passes every test on the page as a number that has been fully examined.

### 4 · Testing for the right number of people
**Screen:** the sharing bay with the friends counted and the host box still unticked
**Does:** counts the people, ticks the one handing them out, then tests by that number
**Win:** the count changes, the test runs on the new number and any leftover stands outside the rows
**Feels:** the person holding the tray is sharing too
**Goal:** count everyone sharing, including the person handing the things out
**Observe:** The count in the sharing bay changed the moment the tick box was ticked, and the leftover changed with it.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- tests fifty by five, because five friends were invited, and concludes that none will be left. → the sharing bay counts the people out one at a time as drawn figures, and the tick box for the one handing them out is still empty and lights. Ticking it changes the count, the division runs again on the new count, and two are set on the shelf outside the rows at full strength. The first division stays drawn beside it with the number it was run for written under it.
- shows that fifty divides by five and takes that as settling whether any will be left, whoever ends up sharing. → both divisions are drawn on the same board, one under the other: fifty into five rows with the shelf beside it empty, and fifty into six rows with two standing outside at full strength. Each board is labelled with the number it was run for, and the question is read again above them with the words how many are sharing lit.
**Misconception to break:** Dividing by the number of friends and forgetting the person handing the food round.

## Delight
The second phase, at the moment a cell is skipped. The rail does not shut and nothing is marked: the cell that was passed by lifts a little where it stands, lights, and its digit is drawn larger for a beat, zero and all, while the cells that did come down stay lying on the rail in exactly the order they arrived. The gap on the rail where that cell belongs stays open at its full width, so the missing one is a hole in the line rather than a line that closed up neatly behind it. Bring it down and the rail shuts, the total is written, and the total goes straight back up through the lamp.

**General miss line:** Bring every cell down again, and leave none of them up there.

## Answer key
A number is divisible by 2 when its ones digit is 0, 2, 4, 6 or 8, by 5 when its ones digit is 5 or 0, and by 10 when its ones digit is 0. For those three, nothing but the last digit matters. A number is divisible by 3 when the total of its digits is divisible by 3, and every digit goes into that total, zeros included, because a zero adds nothing but skipping one loses whatever comes after it. This chapter teaches those four tests and no others, so nothing here settles 4, 6, 8 or 9. A test answers about the number you tested by and about no other, so before choosing a divisor, count everyone who is sharing, including the person handing the things out.

**Key terms:** divisible, divisibility test, ones digit, digit sum
Divisibility by two, three, five and ten is decided by looking at the number rather than dividing it. It ends in two, so yes. The ones digit decides it for two, for five and for ten. Zero, two, four, six or eight for two. Five or zero for five. Zero alone for ten. Nothing else in the number matters. Six and four and zero and five come to fifteen, and fifteen divides by three, so the number does. Count the zeros in as you go. A zero adds nothing, but skipping one is how a digit gets lost in a longer number. Nothing at all. This chapter teaches four tests and no more. A number that passes every test here may still divide by others, and nothing you have been shown settles that. No, and the five test cannot say. Six people leave two over. Before you test, count who is sharing, and remember to count the person handing them out.
