## What it is
**Description:** Step a number along a track that never reaches an end, and band a stretch of it to see what between leaves out.
**Why:** Build the multiples of a number by counting on in equal steps, and find out why the list has no last one.
**Look:** A track of equal steps running off the right hand edge with a flag on every step, a times table filling in above it and a shaded band below.
**Context:** Grade 4, and the chapter turns round here. An adult sees times tables being recited in another form. What is actually being taught is direction: factors were hunted by closing in on a number and multiples are built by running away from it, and this list has no last entry to find. The second thing being taught is smaller and catches more children, which is that a question asking how many wants a count and not only a list, and that between leaves both of its ends out.
**For the child:** Set the step to your number. Lay one step and see where the flag lands. Keep laying steps and watch the table fill in beside them. Try to find the last one. Then drop the band and count only the flags inside it.
**Teaching notes:** Say a times table aloud before the tool is opened and write the answers in a column, then open the track and let the class see that the column and the flags are the same list. That saves multiples from arriving as a new word for something nobody knew. Spend real time on the first step, because the first multiple of a number being the number itself sounds like a trick and is used later in two places, for the lowest multiple of a number and for the fact that a number stands at both ends of its own two lists. Then run the keep going control until somebody asks you to stop, and ask them why you cannot. Hold that answer against the pen from the topic before, where the list shut at both ends: one runs out and the other does not, and that is the only place where the two words genuinely differ. The between band deserves the last quarter of the session. Put both posts on flags on purpose, ask for the count before the list, and keep asking for the count, because the chapter's own worked example answers a how many question with three numbers and never says three.

## Opening (first frame of every mount)
Every mount comes up already furnished for its phase with nothing in front of it. On the first phase the step block is set, the track is empty and the table strip above it is ruled and blank. On the second the track is cleared back to the start post with one step waiting to be laid and nothing beyond it. On the third the track is already long, running out through the right hand edge of the frame with no end mark on it and no last step drawn. On the fourth the track is long and flagged and the between band lies under it with both of its posts standing on flags and nothing shaded yet. The running count at the top left holds the steps laid and the flags planted, and the answer slot and the pad stand empty down the right. One thing moves, once: the first blank line of the table strip, or the single step waiting at the start, or the edge of the frame where the track leaves it, or the left hand post of the band, gives one slow pulse while the voice asks where the next step is going to land. That pulse is the only invitation, and a finger on it is the whole way in.

## Phases (what each mount shows and does; the words are in tool.tsx)

### 1 · A times table read as a list
**Screen:** an empty track with the step block set and the table strip blank
**Does:** lays several steps and reads the filling table strip beside the flags
**Win:** every flag on the track matches a line of the table, which reads as one list
**Feels:** the table you already know by heart is the list the chapter is asking for
**Goal:** the answers of a times table are the multiples of the number
**Observe:** Every line of the times table matched a flag on the track, one for one.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- rings every number in the line, so that nine and one and eighty-one are all circled as multiples. → the line is read out as so many steps of nine, and the track lays exactly that many steps beside it. Only the place the steps land carries a flag; the step size and the number of steps are drawn on the step block and on the arcs instead, where neither of them is a flag. The child's rings stay exactly where they were put and the flag that the line names lights beside them.
- reads the column of answers as the factors of nine, because the two words turned up in the same week. → the pen from the topic before opens under the track holding the factors of nine, one and three and nine, penned between its two posts with both walls drawn. Above it the track runs its flags out through the edge of the frame with no end mark. The two are left on the stage together and read out one after the other, the short shut list and the one that leaves.
**Misconception to break:** Thinking multiples are a new thing to learn rather than the table already known by heart.

### 2 · The first multiple of a number
**Screen:** the track cleared with only the first step waiting to be laid
**Does:** lays the first step and reads where the flag lands
**Win:** the first flag lands on the number itself and the table strip shows one times it
**Feels:** the first step you lay lands on the number you started from
**Goal:** the first multiple of a number is the number itself
**Observe:** The very first step landed on the number itself.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- starts the list at twice the number, leaving the number itself off the front of it. → one step is laid, one flag is planted, and the flag lands on the number itself with the first line of the table strip writing itself above it, one step of it. The child's list stays exactly where it was written, and the missing first entry is drawn as an empty flag standing in front of it.
- answers lowest multiple with one, because one is the lowest factor of everything. → the pen and the track are drawn one above the other for the same number, with the one card lying inside the pen against its left post and no flag anywhere on the track standing on one. The first flag lights instead, and the two lists are read out in turn, the one that starts at one and the one that starts at the number.
**Misconception to break:** Starting the list at twice the number, because the number itself looks too easy to count.

### 3 · Multiples that never run out
**Screen:** a long track already scrolling with no end mark drawn
**Does:** asks for the last flag and is handed the next one instead, over and over
**Win:** the track scrolls on with no end mark and the child says out loud that there is no last one
**Feels:** every time you ask for the last one you are handed the next one
**Goal:** there is no last multiple, because the next step is always there
**Observe:** Asking for the last flag never produced one; it produced the next flag.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- writes down the last number on the page and calls it the last multiple. → the track runs on past where the child stopped, one step at a time and each exactly as long as the last, and the table strip writes one more line for each. The number the child wrote down keeps its flag and its place in the run, with the steps carrying on past it and out through the edge of the frame.
- says the multiples run out somewhere past a hundred, because that is where the tables stop. → keep going is left running and the track scrolls past a hundred, then past several hundred, then past a thousand, with the step block untouched in the corner and each arc drawn exactly the same length as the first one. The flag the child named keeps its own mark, far back down the track, and the edge of the frame stays as empty as it was.
**Misconception to break:** Treating the last multiple written down as the end of the list, because the page stopped there.

### 4 · Between leaves out both ends
**Screen:** the track with a band whose two posts both land on flags
**Does:** drops the posts, shades the middle and counts what is strictly inside
**Win:** the flags on the posts stay drawn outside the shading and the band reads out its count
**Feels:** the two flags on the posts are drawn as plainly as ever, and neither one is inside
**Goal:** a multiple sitting on either post is not inside the band
**Observe:** A flag standing on a post was drawn as plainly as any other and still sat outside the shading.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- counts twenty-eight and fifty-six in, because both of them are multiples of seven too. → the two posts are dropped exactly on those two flags and both flags stay fully drawn at full strength. Only the stretch strictly inside the posts is shaded, and the shading stops short of each post so the gap between the shading and the flag is visible. The band then counts what it holds, and the child's answer stays in the slot beside it.
- answers how many multiples by naming them and never gives a number. → the question is read again with the words how many lit, and the flags inside the band are counted out one at a time with the count standing in the corner and going up as each is touched. The list the child gave stays exactly where it was written and the count is drawn above it, so both answers are on the stage together.
**Misconception to break:** Counting 18 and 63 in because they are multiples too, which makes six instead of four.

## Delight
The third phase, when the last one is asked for. The track does not refuse and nothing is marked: it simply lays one more step of exactly the same length, plants one more numbered flag, writes one more line into the table strip, and scrolls so that the new flag is in the frame and the edge is empty again. Ask again and it happens again, at the same pace, with the step length never changing by a hair. A child can sit there asking for the last one as long as they like, and the only thing that ever comes back is the next one, which is the whole answer to the question and is never said out loud.

**General miss line:** Lay the steps again one at a time and read the table filling beside them.

## Answer key
A multiple of a number is that number multiplied by a whole number, so the answers in its times table are its multiples. The first multiple of any number is the number itself, because one times it is it, so every counting number is a multiple of itself and is its own lowest multiple. The list never runs out: whatever multiple you stop at, the next whole number gives another, so a number has infinite multiples. Between two numbers means strictly inside them, so a multiple sitting on either end is not counted. Answer a how many question with the count as well as the list.

## Student controls
- **Step block**: sets the size of the step, which is the number being counted on
- **Lay a step**: lays one more step of the same size and plants a flag where it lands
- **Table strip**: fills in the times table above the track as the flags go down
- **Keep going**: lays step after step and scrolls, offering no stopping post
- **Between band**: drops two posts and shades only what lies strictly inside them
- **Count the band**: reads out how many flags the band holds, as well as naming them
- **Name a flag**: says which numbered multiple a chosen flag is

## Textbook (this topic)
**Pages:** 65, 66
**Key terms:** multiple, times table, infinite, between
A multiple is named from the times tables, and multiples are shown to run on without end. The answers in a times table are the multiples of that number, and the first multiple is the number itself. Multiples never run out, so a number has infinite multiples. Between two numbers means strictly inside, so both ends are left out. The multiples of eight. Every answer in the table is eight multiplied by a whole number. The table you already know by heart is a list of multiples under another name. Seven itself, because one times seven is seven. That holds for every number. Its first multiple is always itself, so every counting number is a multiple of itself. There is no last one. Whatever multiple you stop at, multiplying by the next whole number gives another. We say a number has infinite multiples, and that is the opposite of its factors, which run out. Three of them, thirty-five, forty-two and forty-nine. Twenty-eight and fifty-six are multiples of seven too, but between means strictly inside, so neither end is counted. Give the count as well as the list.
