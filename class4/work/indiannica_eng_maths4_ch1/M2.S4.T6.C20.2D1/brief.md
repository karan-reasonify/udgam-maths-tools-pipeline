## What it is
**Description:** Flip a switch and the same seven digits regroup under a different set of commas.
**Why:** Find out how one number can be written two different ways, and why both of them are correct.
**Look:** A row of digits with a chart above it and another below, each with its own headings and its own commas.
**Context:** Grade 4, late in the chapter. An adult sees two charts being compared. What the page is actually doing is showing that a number and the way a number is written are two different things, and it does it by holding the digits still and moving only the commas.
**For the child:** Lay the commas into the row of digits, then throw the switch and watch them move. Check that no digit moved with them. Read the number out one chunk at a time, and say the name of each chunk.
**Teaching notes:** Write the digits once and put two sets of commas underneath the same row, on paper, before opening the tool. Then let the tool do it, because watching the commas slide while the digits hold still is the thing the page is claiming and paper has to redraw. Say one lakh and one hundred thousand in the same breath until they stop sounding like two different amounts. Go back to the opening page in the last phase; the question asked there was left open on purpose fifteen pages earlier and this is where it closes.
**Placement:** Explore

## Phases (what each mount shows and does; the words are in tool.tsx)

### 1 · The Indian chart groups two, two and three
**Screen:** a row of seven digits with the Indian chart lit above it
**Does:** lays the commas in from the right and names each period
**Win:** the commas sit at the Indian boundaries and the three bands are named
**Feels:** the commas are counted in, and the counting starts at the end you do not read from
**Goal:** the Indian system groups three places, then two, then two
**Observe:** The seven digit cards stood in the same seven places under both sets of commas.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- lays the second comma three gaps along from the first, carrying the grouping on in threes. → both comma tiles slide back to the right hand end of the rail and the counting runs again from the right, one card lifting per count: three cards lift and the first tile drops into the gap behind them, then two cards lift and the second tile drops behind those. The gap the child chose stays outlined and hollow on the rail, and their answer stays in the slot.
- counts in from the left, so the commas land after the first three digits and the next two. → a small ink figure appears under each card in turn from the right hand end, and the gap the count reaches lights before any tile moves; the count then runs again from the left and stops without lighting anything, because the chart is read from the other end. Nothing the child laid is taken away.
**Misconception to break:** Placing a comma after every three digits all the way along.

### 2 · The international chart groups three, three and three
**Screen:** the same row of digits with the switch thrown to international
**Does:** watches the commas slide and checks that no digit has moved
**Win:** the commas rest at every third place and the digit row is unchanged
**Feels:** everything about the number changes except the number
**Goal:** the digits do not move, only the commas do
**Observe:** Both charts wanted two commas in this number, and not one gap was shared.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- says the number has grown, because it now reads five million and a moment ago it read fifty-six lakh. → every card draws a thin ink line up into its column in the chart above and down into its column in the chart below, seven lines each way, and each line lands in a column standing for the same amount under either name, ten lakhs above and millions below. The two readings are then printed one above the other with the unchanged digit row between them.
- slides a digit card along to make the last group come out full. → the card lifts, is carried back to the place it came from and set down, its own column lighting as it lands, while under it the two comma tiles run freely along their rail. The rail is drawn under the row and never through it, so the commas are the thing that moves and the cards are the thing that does not.
**Misconception to break:** Believing the number itself has changed because it now looks different.

### 3 · Each period is read with its own name
**Screen:** a six digit number with its period bands showing
**Does:** reads each period aloud and adds its name, then checks against the printed line
**Win:** the spoken reading and the printed reading match
**Feels:** a long number stops being a string of digits and turns into three short numbers with names
**Goal:** a number is read one period at a time, not digit by digit
**Observe:** One lakh and one hundred thousand filled the conversion strip to exactly the same length.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- reads the number out one digit at a time, naming each digit in turn instead of each period. → the period bands come forward behind the digits and the cards of one band lift together as a single chunk; the reading strip fills a chunk at a time, the digits of the period first and then its name, and a chunk already read stays printed while the next one lifts. The child's reading stays in the slot.
- says the name of the ones period too, finishing the reading with the word ones. → the strip prints the whole reading with the word ones on the end, then that one word thins out of the strip while the three digits of the period stay lit; the ones heading on the chart stays exactly where it is, because the period keeps its name and only goes unsaid.
**Misconception to break:** Reading the number out digit by digit instead of period by period.

### 4 · One lakh, one million, and the numbers from the first page
**Screen:** the conversion strip beside the numbers from the chapter opening
**Does:** converts the library figure from one system to the other
**Win:** the opening question is answered and the strip agrees with the chart
**Feels:** a question left open on the very first page closes fifteen pages later
**Goal:** ten lakh and one million are two names for one amount
**Observe:** The library of nine million books came back as ninety lakh books.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- answers nine lakh for the library, taking one million for one lakh. → the conversion strip fills in, one lakh drawing out against one hundred thousand on the upper row and ten lakh against one million on the lower, each pair of plates sliding together until they are exactly the same length. Nine of the lower plates are then laid end to end and counted along in tens of lakh. The child's answer stays in the slot.
- says both numbers on the opening page are written in the same system. → the opening page line comes up with its figures and each is lit in turn over the charts: the students number carries its comma in a gap that both charts agree about, and the library number carries a period name that only the chart below has, and that chart's millions band lights as the word is read. Nothing the child wrote is removed.
**Misconception to break:** Treating one million as one lakh and answering nine lakh.

## Delight
The second phase, the first time the switch is thrown. The seven digit cards each take a thin ink ring and hold absolutely still, and everything else on the stage moves at once: the two comma tiles run along their rail to new gaps, the vermilion lakhs band behind the digits draws in by a column and turns rose as it becomes a millions band, the green thousands band widens by the column the lakhs band gave up, and the heading row above the digits hands its names over to the heading row below. When it settles the rings lift and the digits are exactly where they were. Nothing else moves and the voice says nothing while it happens.

**General miss line:** Slide the commas back and count in from the right again.

## Answer key
The Indian system groups the places three, then two, then two, so the periods are ones, thousands and lakhs. The international system groups them three, three and three, so the periods are ones, thousands and millions. The digits never move between the two. A number is read one period at a time, each followed by its name except the ones period. One lakh is one hundred thousands and ten lakh is one million.

## Textbook (this topic)
**Pages:** 11, 12
**Key terms:** Indian system, international system, lakh, million, period
The same seven digits are grouped two at a time in India and three at a time elsewhere, and so are read two different ways. Three digits make the ones period, then two make the thousands period and two make the lakhs period. So the number is written 56,87,709 and read as fifty-six lakh eighty-seven thousand seven hundred nine. The digits did not change at all, only the commas did. Grouped in threes the number is written 5,687,709 and read as five million six hundred eighty-seven thousand seven hundred nine. All the digits in one period are read together and then the name of that period is said. The ones period is the exception, because it has no name of its own. That is why the commas are worth putting in first. One lakh is one hundred thousands and ten lakh is one million, so nine million is ninety lakh. The first page mixed the two systems on purpose, and this is the page that answers the question it asked.
