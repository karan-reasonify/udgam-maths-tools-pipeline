## What it is
**Description:** Collect answers one at a time, set them out in a table under their names, turn a pictograph row into a number with its key, and find the most, the least, the same and the total.
**Why:** Collect facts that give information, set them out in a table under their names, read a pictograph row with its key, and find the most, the least, the same and the total.
**Look:** A desk with a drawn class along its top edge, a heap of answer slips, a ruled table in the middle, and a pictograph board that slides over it with a compare rail beside it.
**Context:** Grade 4, the first working topic of the data chapter. An adult sees a table being filled in. What is actually being settled is that data is facts collected to give information, and that a table puts each number under its own name, so it can be found, compared and added. The recap on the opening pages reads a pictograph of sixes with a key of three, and a child who skips the key writes the number of pictures as the number of sixes. The section headed Data Collection only sets out data that was already collected; this tool starts by collecting it.
**For the child:** Tap each child to hear their answer. Drag each answer to its column. Add the columns. Count the pictures in a row. Use the key to find the number. Find the most, the least and the total.
**Teaching notes:** Ask the class how they came to school before the tool is opened, and mark each answer on the board as it comes. Then set the answers out in a table, add it up, and check the total against the number of children asked. The section of the chapter headed Data Collection defines data and then only sets out data already collected, twenty-six children in four houses, and no task in the chapter asks a child to collect anything, so let the class collect first. The table in that section heads its row team where the text says house and lists the houses in another order; every value agrees, so read it from the table. The recap reads a pictograph of the sixes hit by five cricketers, one bat for three sixes, and asks for the most, the least, the two that are equal and the total. The questions ask for sixes, not bats, so every row is turned into sixes with the key before an answer is given, and the total adds every row, which is right there because the question asks for all five together. Name nobody; call each row by what it counts.

## Phases (what each mount shows and does; the words are in tool.tsx)

### 1 · What data is
**Screen:** a drawn class along the top of the desk, an empty heap and a question card asking how each child came to school
**Does:** taps each child, watches the answer drop as a slip, then says what the heap of slips can tell the school
**Win:** the last slip lands and the question card turns to show what the heap tells
**Feels:** each answer arrives with its name on it, one child at a time, and the heap grows by one
**Goal:** facts collected to give useful information are data, and each fact has a name that says what was counted
**Observe:** Every child in the class row gave one slip, and the heap's count matched the children asked.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- calls four numbers written with no names data about the class, and says what each one means. → the four numbers stay on the desk as the child left them. The question card asks which of them is the bus. One at a time, each slip's name is drawn back beside its number, and the card is answered only once the names are back. Nothing is cleared.
- calls a drawing of a rain cloud data, because rain is in the weather reports the section names. → the drawing is set beside the slip heap, and the question card asks how many. The heap answers with a count for each name. The drawing gives no count and no name, so nothing from it lands in the table, and it stays where the child put it.
- taps a child who has already answered, to add their answer a second time. → the child is drawn with the small tick at their feet and gives no second slip. The running count stays level with the children asked, and the heap stays as it was.
**Misconception to break:** Calling any list of numbers data even when nothing says what was counted, when a number gives information only with its name beside it.

### 2 · Setting data out in a table
**Screen:** the heap of slips beside a ruled table with a heading for each answer and every number cell empty
**Does:** drags each slip into the column for its name, then fills the total cell
**Win:** the heap empties into the columns and the total cell matches the number of slips collected
**Feels:** every slip slides under its own heading, and the columns add up to the heap they came from
**Goal:** a table puts each number under its own name, and the numbers add up to the whole group
**Observe:** The total cell matched the heap of slips every time, and the heap matched the class when each child gave one answer.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- counts the headings instead of adding the numbers, and says there are four children because there are four seasons. → the four heading cells are counted into the running count, and the child's four stays in the slot. The total cell then adds the numbers under them, six, nine, eight and five, one at a time, and twenty-eight lands. Each child chose one season, so the cell is labelled children, and the check strip stands the heap's twenty-eight slips beside it.
- drops a slip into the column beside its name, so one column is one too many and its neighbour one too few. → the column's slips fan out under their heading, each showing its name. The one whose name is not the heading's is lifted clear and turned so its name faces both headings. It stays where the child put it until the child moves it.
- adds a table in which each child named two games, and calls the sum the number of children. → the total cell matches the heap, twenty-four slips. The class row then shows its twelve children, each with two slips drawn from them, and the total cell's label reads answers, not children.
**Misconception to break:** Counting the four seasons and saying there are 4 children, instead of adding the numbers under them.

### 3 · Reading a pictograph with its key
**Screen:** the pictograph board over the table, with its title strip and key card, and one row lit
**Does:** counts the symbols in the lit row, reads the key, and writes the row's number with its unit
**Win:** the key's value is taken once for each symbol and the row's number lands at the end of the row
**Feels:** each picture holds the same amount, and the row is that amount taken once for every picture
**Goal:** a pictograph row is read by counting its symbols and taking the key that many times
**Observe:** The row reader counted five symbols into the corner, and thirty landed at the row's end.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- writes the number of pictures as the answer, five mangoes, without using the key. → the running count holds the five symbols. The row reader then takes the key's six once under each symbol in amber, climbing to thirty, which lands in the row's tag. The child's five stays in the slot beside it.
- reads the row above or below the one the question names. → the lit row keeps its sky blue band. The row the child read is outlined, and its name is set beside the name the question gives. The row reader then counts the lit row's symbols one at a time.
- writes the key's own number, six, as the row's number. → the row reader takes the six under the first symbol only and stops, with the tag reading six. It then carries on under each symbol in turn, and the tag climbs to thirty.
**Misconception to break:** Writing 5, the number of pictures, as the number of mangoes, without using the key.

### 4 · Most, least, equal and in all
**Screen:** the pictograph board with every row's number written, the compare rail empty and the total bar waiting
**Does:** stands the rows on the compare rail, names the most, the least and the two that are equal, then taps the total bar
**Win:** the rows stand in order on the rail, two equal numbers level with each other, and the total fills the bar
**Feels:** the rows stand in order on the rail, and a difference is the stretch by which one row passes another
**Goal:** the most and the least come from comparing the numbers, and the total from adding every row once
**Observe:** Two numbers shared one place on the rail, and the most and the least stood at its two ends.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- adds the stars, twenty-one, and gives that as the number of stickers in all. → the running count holds the twenty-one stars. The total bar then adds the rows' numbers, twenty, thirty-two, twelve and twenty, and eighty-four lands in the bar. Each sticker was collected by one class, so the bar is labelled stickers in all. The child's twenty-one stays in the slot.
- adds the two numbers when the question asks how many more, giving forty-four for thirty-two and twelve. → the two numbers are tapped on the rail and both rows light on the board. The sky blue band lies behind the five stars by which the longer row passes the shorter, and they are read with the key, twenty. The child's forty-four stays in the slot beside it.
- answers a question worded as how many children like one thing more than another with the first thing's whole count. → the question card turns to its other face, which asks how many more chose the first thing than the second. The two rows are lined up on the board, and the stretch by which the first passes the second is banded and counted.
**Misconception to break:** Adding the stars, 21, and giving that as the number of stickers, without using the key.

## Delight
The second phase, once the total cell matches the heap. The children in the class row give a small wave one at a time from the left. Each waves as their own slip is found in its column and lights there for a moment. A child who gave two answers waves twice, once for each slip. It runs as often as a finger likes on the total cell, nothing is marked either way, and the voice says nothing at all while it happens.

**General miss line:** Read the name at the top of the column, and count that row again.

## Answer key
Data is a collection of facts that gives useful information. Collected data is set out in a table with each number under its own name, and when each thing counted sits under one name only, the numbers under the names add up to the whole group. A pictograph row is read by counting its symbols and taking the key that many times. The most and the least are found by comparing the numbers, two rows with the same number are equal, and the total is every row added once.

## Student controls
- **Class row**: brings one child's answer down onto the desk as a slip when that child is tapped
- **Slip heap**: holds the answers collected so far
- **Table**: sets a heading cell for each answer with a number cell under it
- **Column drop**: takes a slip into the column for its name and adds one under that heading
- **Total cell**: adds the numbers under the headings and checks them against the heap
- **Pictograph board**: slides over the table with a title strip, a key card and rows of symbols
- **Row reader**: counts a row's symbols and takes the key that many times
- **Compare rail**: stands the rows' numbers side by side from the largest to the smallest
- **Total bar**: adds every row once, when the question asks for the total
