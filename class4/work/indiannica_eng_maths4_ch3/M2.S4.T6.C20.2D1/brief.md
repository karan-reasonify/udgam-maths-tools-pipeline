## What it is
**Description:** A question told in sentences, with every number liftable, and a mat that only takes the ones the question needs.
**Why:** Turn a question told in sentences into a subtraction, using only the numbers that answer what was actually asked.
**Look:** A short question across the top with every number liftable, a row of question cards under it, and an empty column mat waiting below.
**Context:** Grade 4, and the method is finished by now, so this topic is about getting from a sentence to a working. An adult sees word problems. What is actually being separated out is two decisions that usually happen silently and at the same time: what is being asked, and which of the printed numbers answer it. The chapter says outright that a question may print more than it needs, and it asks for two step questions where doing the right two steps in the wrong order gives a wrong answer that looks worked.
**For the child:** Read the question and pick the card that says what it is asking. Lift down only the numbers that answer it. Grey out any number with no job. If you cannot tell what to do, open the shrink strip and try it with tiny numbers. Write your answer back as a sentence.
**Teaching notes:** Make the question card a real step and do not let anybody past it. A child who has said out loud what is being asked has already half sorted the numbers, and a child who has not will work with whatever is printed. Keep a spare number dimmed rather than deleted. The point is not that it disappears but that it was looked at and turned down, and a child who can say why it was turned down is doing the actual work of the topic. The shrink strip is the chapter's own idea and it is worth using out loud: what must be added to four to make seven is three, and three is seven take four, so the same move works whatever the size of the numbers. Two step questions deserve the most time, because the two shapes pull in opposite directions. Two things bought together for less than their prices needs adding first and taking away after; one thing priced above another needs taking away first and adding after. Have the two steps written down in order before either is worked, and finish every question with the answer read back as a sentence, because a number with no sentence round it has not yet answered anything. The two houses on the opening page belong to this family as well.

## Phases (what each mount shows and does; the words are in tool.tsx)

### 1 · Find the two numbers the question is about
**Screen:** a two sentence question with two numbers in it and an empty mat
**Does:** picks the question card, lifts both numbers down and works the columns
**Win:** the mat holds the two numbers the right way up and the answer is written as a sentence
**Feels:** a paragraph turns into two numbers with a rule under them, and the sentences stop mattering
**Goal:** the two amounts the question compares come down onto the mat
**Observe:** Picking the question card sorted the numbers before a single one had been lifted.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- lifts both numbers down in the order the sentences print them and sets them out that way round on the mat. → the question card stays lit above the mat and the two cards are read out with what each one counts printed under it; the mat then shows both orders one under the other, and only the mat with the greater number on top has enough in its columns to pay. On the dairy's 3,929 kilograms of two kinds together, of which 1,560 kilograms is the one kind, the other comes to 2,369 kilograms, the tens fetching a ten from the hundreds on their left and those hundreds being left one less. Nothing is cleared.
- adds the two amounts because both of them count the same kind of thing. → the chosen question card is reprinted large across the mat and its words are matched one at a time to what the mat would do: the card that asks how many are left and the card that asks how much altogether are stood side by side with the same two numbers under each, and only one of the two leaves the greater amount standing unchanged at the top. Both workings are left on the stage and neither is crossed out.
**Misconception to break:** Adding the two amounts, because both numbers describe the same tank.

### 2 · Some numbers are printed to be left out
**Screen:** a three number question where one number answers nothing
**Does:** greys the spare number and lifts down only the two that are needed
**Win:** the spare number dims in its sentence, still readable, and the mat holds only two
**Feels:** a number can be perfectly true and have no work at all to do in this question
**Goal:** a number can be printed and still have no job in the question
**Observe:** A total with one part given, and two amounts compared, came down onto the same mat and were worked the same way: 3,929 less 1,560 gave 2,369, and 37,648 less 26,820 gave 10,828.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- lifts the number of carriages down onto the mat along with the two counts of people. → the question card stays lit and each number card is held up against it in turn with the sentence it came from printed underneath; the carriage card is the only one whose sentence says how the train is built rather than who is riding in it, and it settles back into its place in grey. The two that are left give 2,740 less 1,560, which is 1,180 people, the tens fetching a ten from the hundreds on their left. The child's answer stays in the slot beside the new one.
- rubs the spare number off the board so the question looks tidy. → the number comes back into its sentence in grey with its outline still drawn round it, and the whole question is read through once with it standing there; grey is the colour this chapter keeps for a place being held open and nothing being brought, which is exactly what that number is doing. On the pair of towns the distance between them goes grey and the two populations give 37,648 less 26,820, which is 10,828 people, the hundreds fetching a ten from the thousands on their left.
**Misconception to break:** Using all three printed numbers because they are all there on the page.

### 3 · Shrink the question until you can see it
**Screen:** a reach the target question with the shrink strip open beneath it
**Does:** works the tiny version, names the move, then does the same to the real numbers
**Win:** the tiny version answers itself and the same move lands on the real numbers
**Feels:** a question you could not begin becomes one you can answer out loud, and then it turns out to be the same question
**Goal:** the same shape in tiny numbers shows which move is wanted
**Observe:** The spare number stayed on the board in grey and the answer came out right without it.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- adds the two numbers because the question uses the word added. → the shrink strip opens under the sentence and reruns it with single digit numbers: what must be added to four to make seven. The answer is three, and the strip writes that three as seven take four, so the move is read off a sum the child never had to work. The real sentence then takes the same move: 75,000 less 45,698 gives 29,302, and 29,302 added back to 45,698 stands at 75,000. Both the strip and the mat stay on the stage.
- works the tiny version, gets it right, and then goes back to the real numbers and does something else. → the two sentences are stood one above the other with the same words underlined in both and only the numbers different, and the move the strip used is printed once between them with an arrow running down from the strip to the mat. The strip's working stays visible while the real one runs, so the two can be read against each other without anything being cleared.
**Misconception to break:** Adding 52,640 and 90,000 because the question uses the word added.

### 4 · Two steps need an order
**Screen:** a question needing an addition and a subtraction, with the step order bay open
**Does:** lays the two steps out in order, then works the first one
**Win:** the steps stand in order, the second unlocks only after the first and the answer lands
**Feels:** two steps that are both right can still come out wrong, and the only thing wrong with them was their order
**Goal:** the order of two steps is decided before either is worked
**Observe:** One question needed adding first and taking away second, and the next one needed exactly the opposite order.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- takes the smaller price off the greater one, which compares the two things instead of comparing the total with what was paid. → the step order bay draws both roads and labels what each one answers. Taking one price off the other answers how much dearer one thing is than the other; adding the two prices and taking the price actually paid off that total answers how much was saved. On the printer and the scanner the two prices come to 4,619 and the combined price of 4,358 leaves 261 rupees saved, the tens fetching a ten from the hundreds on their left. The other road keeps its answer too and neither is crossed out.
- adds the two printed numbers straight away when one thing is described as costing a set amount more than another. → the build bay opens beside the mat and the sentence that describes the second price underlines itself word by word: the amount printed is the dearer one, and the other is that amount less what it is dearer by. So 35,570 less 11,050 builds the cheaper machine at 24,520 with no column having to fetch anything, and only then do the two go onto the mat together and come to 60,090 rupees. The bay leaves its small working standing under the main one.
**Misconception to break:** Taking 3,260 off 7,480, which compares the two prices instead of comparing the total with what was paid.

## Delight
The third phase, the moment the shrink strip opens. The question standing on the board is about a number in the tens of thousands and a target in the tens of thousands, and the strip beneath it reruns the same sentence with a four where the one number was and a seven where the other was. It then answers itself while the child watches, because what must be added to four to make seven is three and everybody in the room can see that it is, and the three it writes down is written as seven take four. The move rides back up the arrow to the real sentence, which turns out to be the same question after all: the target less what is already there, so 75,000 less 45,698 is 29,302, and 45,698 and 29,302 put back together stand at 75,000. Nothing else moves and the voice says nothing while it happens.

**General miss line:** Read it again, one sentence at a time, and keep your question card showing.

## Answer key
Say what is being asked before touching a number. Then lift down only the numbers that answer it and grey the rest where they stand. A total and one part means taking the part off the total. Two amounts compared means taking the smaller off the greater. An amount needed to reach a target means taking the smaller off the target. If you cannot tell which, run the same question with tiny numbers and copy the move. Where two steps are needed, decide their order before working either: two things bought together for less than their prices means adding first and taking away after, while one thing priced above another means taking away first and adding after. Write the answer back into a sentence.

## Student controls
- **Question row**: states what is being asked before any number can move
- **Lift a number**: takes a number out of its sentence and down onto the mat
- **Push it back**: returns a number to its sentence, so nothing is committed
- **Dim bay**: greys a number the question does not need while leaving it readable
- **Shrink strip**: runs the same question again with single digit numbers
- **Build bay**: works out an amount that is described rather than printed
- **Step order**: holds two steps in order and locks the second until the first is done
- **Answer sentence**: writes the finished answer back into a sentence
