## What it is
**Description:** One of the three numbers is hidden, or one digit is, and the two that are left are enough to find it.
**Why:** Find the number, or the single digit, that somebody has hidden in a subtraction, using only the two that are still showing.
**Look:** A column mat with one row blank or a scatter of stars across it, a mode rail above and a build bay waiting underneath.
**Context:** Grade 4, and this is the one topic in the chapter where a child has to reason rather than follow a method. An adult sees three kinds of puzzle. What is actually being tested is whether the three names mean anything yet, because the move that finds a blank depends entirely on which of the three it is: the missing number on top is built by adding, and the missing number taken away is found by subtracting, and a child who has not named the blank first has a fifty fifty chance of doing the wrong one.
**For the child:** Say which of the three numbers is hiding before you touch anything. If it is the number on top, add the two you can see. If it is the number taken away, subtract instead. For a covered digit, start at the ones and read the working upwards. Then put what you found back in and check it.
**Teaching notes:** Make the naming a spoken step and never let it be skipped. A child who says out loud that it is the number on top that is missing has already chosen the right move; a child who starts working has a coin toss to lose. Hold the three puzzle shapes up together at least once, with a different row blank in each, because seen on their own they all look like the same question. For the covered digits, insist that the working is read upwards and say why it may be: the answer and the number taken away add back to the number on top, which is the checking rule from earlier used to find something rather than to test it. Start at the ones every time and do not let a middle column be attacked first, because a column with a ten coming into it cannot be read until the column on its right has been settled. This is the point in the chapter to come back to the building on the opening page: one house holds 888 seats and the other 384, so one has 504 more, and the question the chapter left open at the start can finally be closed. Finish every puzzle by putting what was found back into the subtraction and working it downwards.

On the second the same mat has its middle row empty instead, and the bay below carries a minus and a rule. On the third the mat is full and starred: single digits are covered across all three rows, the ones column is the only one lit, and the trade strip above the headings is drawn and empty. On the fourth three small mats stand side by side with a different row blank on each and the name it first gate closed in front of all three. One thing moves, once: the blank row on the mat, or the rightmost star card, or the closed gate, gives one slow pulse while the voice says that one of the three numbers is hiding. That pulse is the only invitation, and a finger on it is the whole way in.

## Phases (what each mount shows and does; the words are in tool.tsx)

### 1 · A missing greater number is built by adding
**Screen:** the mat with the upper row blank and both other rows showing
**Does:** drops the two showing numbers into the build bay and adds them
**Win:** the total rises into the blank row and the subtraction reads straight through
**Feels:** the puzzle says take away and the thing that solves it turns out to be adding
**Goal:** the difference and the subtrahend rebuild the number on top
**Observe:** The two numbers that were showing added back to exactly the number that had been covered: 4,694 and 5,286 gave 9,980, and taking 5,286 off 9,980 gave 4,694 again.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- takes the smaller of the two showing numbers off the greater one, because the puzzle is a subtraction. → the build bay fills with the two showing numbers and works them both ways at once in its two halves: 4,694 and 5,286 added give 9,980, and the greater less the smaller gives 592. Each answer is lifted into the blank row in turn and the whole subtraction is read downwards from it, and only the number built by adding leaves 5,286 taken away with 4,694 standing under the rule. Nothing is cleared and the child's answer stays in the slot.
- lines a three digit number taken away under a four digit answer by their left ends in the build bay. → the heading over every digit of the shorter card is named under it as the card slides, and the card carries on sliding right until its last digit stands in the ones column, leaving its left hand column ruled and empty rather than closing the gap up. The rule and the total row stay empty the whole time it moves, so nothing is added until the places agree; with 986 under 4,694 by their right ends the total is 5,680, and 5,680 less 986 leaves 4,694 standing under the rule.
**Misconception to break:** Taking 3,571 from 4,238, because the question mentions taking away.

### 2 · A missing smaller number is found by taking away
**Screen:** the mat with the middle row blank and the top and answer rows showing
**Does:** sets out the number on top less the difference and works it
**Win:** the answer drops into the middle row and adding all three back agrees
**Feels:** the same mat, the same two numbers showing, and the move flips over
**Goal:** the difference comes off the number on top to give the subtrahend
**Observe:** One puzzle was solved by adding and the next one, which looked the same on the mat, needed taking away: 9,625 with an answer of 3,289 gave 6,336.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- adds the two showing numbers, because adding is what the last phase wanted. → the bay builds both and leaves them side by side. Adding 9,625 and 3,289 gives 12,914, which will not go into a middle row under a number smaller than itself, and the mat is shown with no room for it; taking away gives 6,336, and putting 6,336 into the middle row leaves 3,289 standing under the rule where it was printed. Neither working is crossed out.
- puts the answer into the middle row and the number taken away into the answer row, swapping the two lower rows over. → the mat reads itself back with its row names printed down the left margin and the subtraction worked downwards, each row named aloud as it is used. With 3,289 in the middle the answer comes to 6,336, and with 6,336 in the middle the answer comes to 3,289, so both fill the mat and only one of them answers the question that was asked. The question card stays lit above the mat the whole time.
**Misconception to break:** Adding the two that are showing, which is the move for a missing number on top.

### 3 · A missing digit is found by rebuilding its column
**Screen:** a subtraction with stars in two rows and every column tappable
**Does:** starts at the ones, rebuilds the column upwards and types the digit in
**Win:** the star lifts, the column reads both ways and any ten appears on its left
**Feels:** an answer that adds back perfectly turns out not to fit, and the cells printed on the page are the thing that says so
**Goal:** a covered digit is read out of its own column, starting at the ones
**Observe:** An answer of 60,607 added back to 27,117 perfectly and the puzzle still would not take it.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- tackles a starred column in the middle of the number before the columns on its right have been settled. → the column that was tapped dims and the ones column lights instead, with the column lock naming it aloud. On 7,242 less 3,417 the ones are settled first, the answer's five and the number taken away's seven making twelve, so the digit on top is two and a ten rides up into the tens on its left. Only then will the tens column open, and the middle column the child reached for is still sitting there untouched. The whole puzzle rebuilds to 7,242 less 3,417, which leaves 3,825, and its ones and its hundreds are the two columns that had to fetch a ten.
- hands in an answer that adds back perfectly and does not fit the cells the page prints. → the two candidates are stood one above the other with the printed row of cells drawn over each of them. On the puzzle whose cells read eight, blank, seven, two, blank on top and five, six, six, blank, seven underneath, 87,724 less 27,117 comes to 60,607 and adds back exactly, and the cell drawn over its first digit is printed as a five while a six is standing in it. The other candidate, 83,724 less 27,117, comes to 56,607, and every one of its digits drops into a cell that is either blank or already says that digit. Neither working is crossed out and the cells stay drawn over both.
**Misconception to break:** Guessing a digit from the size of the numbers instead of rebuilding the column it sits in.

### 4 · Say which number is missing before you start
**Screen:** three puzzles side by side with different rows blank and the gate closed
**Does:** names the blank on each one, then works the move that blank needs
**Win:** each gate opens on a correct naming and each puzzle takes a different move
**Feels:** the puzzles stop being three kinds of thing and turn into one question asked about three different rows
**Goal:** naming the blank decides which of the three moves is needed
**Observe:** On one puzzle four of the five columns had to fetch a ten, and the answer row had five cells with only four of them filled.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- works the first puzzle correctly and then uses that same move on the next two without looking at what is blank in them. → the three mats lift their blank rows together and each one is named in the margin beside it: the number on top on the first, the number taken away on the second, a single digit on the third. The bay under each mat then draws the sign that mat needs, a plus under the first, a minus under the second and a column strip under the third, so all three moves stand on the stage at the same time. Nothing already worked is cleared.
- names the blank correctly and then works the other move anyway, because two numbers showing are in the habit of being added. → the naming the child gave is printed on a card above the bay and stays there while the working runs, and the sign in the bay is drawn from that card rather than chosen: a blank on top draws a plus, a blank in the middle draws a minus, and a blank under the rule draws a minus too, because that is an ordinary subtraction. With 888 and 384 showing and the row under the rule blank, the bay draws a minus and the seat gap comes to 504, which is the question the opening page asked and then left standing. The card and the working are left side by side.
**Misconception to break:** Starting to work before naming the blank, and taking one of the two showing numbers off the other.

## Delight
The third phase, the moment the ones column is settled on a puzzle where it passes nine. On 7,242 less 3,417 the answer's five and the number taken away's seven are added inside their own column, the total runs past nine and will not sit in a cell that holds one digit, and the ten it cannot keep rides up out of the column and comes down small and vermilion above the tens on its left. Nothing has been typed into the tens yet and it is already easier than it was, because a column that had two unknowns in it now has one. Then the same trade is redrawn the other way, as the finished subtraction will show it: the tens lending a ten to the ones and being left one less, four written down as three. One trade, two readings, and the star in the ones lifts under a finger. Nothing else moves and the voice says nothing while it happens.

**General miss line:** Name what is blank again, then settle the ones column before any other.

## Answer key
Name the blank before you touch anything. If the number on top is missing, add the difference and the subtrahend, because those two rebuild it. If the number taken away is missing, take the difference off the number on top, and what is left is the subtrahend. If a single digit is covered, read the working upwards as an addition: the difference and the subtrahend add back to the number on top, column by column from the ones, carrying a ten into the column on the left wherever a column passes nine. Whatever you find, put it back into the original subtraction and check it.

## Student controls
- **Mode rail**: covers the number on top, the number taken away, or single digits
- **Name it first**: will not let anything move until the blank has been named
- **Build bay**: stacks the two numbers that are showing and works the move the blank needs
- **Star cards**: covers single digits across the three rows
- **Column lock**: names the column that has to be settled before this one
- **Carry watch**: drops the ten into the column on the left once a column passes nine
- **Check it back**: works the whole subtraction downwards against the difference that was printed

## Textbook (this topic)
**Pages:** 33, 34
**Key terms:** missing minuend, missing subtrahend, missing digit, column
A missing greater number is found by adding, a missing smaller number by subtracting, and a missing digit by rebuilding its column. Add the two you have. The difference and the subtrahend together rebuild the minuend. It is the check from earlier, used to find a number rather than to test one. Take the difference away from the number on top. What is left is the subtrahend. Whatever you took away and whatever remained must add back to the number you started with. Read the working upwards. The difference and the number taken away should add up to the number on top, column by column. Start at the ones and carry into the column on the left where a column passes nine. Each of the three needs a different move. Naming the blank first saves you from adding where you should subtract. This is where the three names earn their keep.
