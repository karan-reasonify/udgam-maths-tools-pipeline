## What it is
**Description:** Round both numbers first, take one from the other the fast way, and measure how far that answer sits from the worked one.
**Why:** Get a quick answer by rounding both numbers first, then use it to decide whether the answer you worked out can be right.
**Look:** A stage split down the middle, the exact working in columns on one side and the rounded numbers on the other, with a bar drawn between the two answers.
**Context:** Grade 4, the closing topic of the chapter. An adult sees rounding done twice and then a subtraction. What is actually being taught is what an estimate is for. It is not a worse answer and it is not a guess: it is a fast answer whose job is to say whether the worked answer is anywhere near believable, and it earns its keep exactly when a trade has gone wrong and the worked answer is out by a whole thousand while looking perfectly neat on the line.
**For the child:** Set the picker to the place the question asks for. Round both numbers first, then take one rounded number off the other. Work the exact answer on the other side. Run the gap bar between the two. If they are far apart, work the exact side again.
**Teaching notes:** Round first and take away second, every time, and say it in that order out loud, because working first and rounding the answer is a different question that gives a different number. The closing exercise never says which place to round to, and the worked case just above it rounds to the nearest thousand, so read the whole exercise that way and say so, otherwise half the class will round to hundreds and be right about the method and wrong about every answer. Use the number line strips for the first few, because rounding read as a rule about a digit falls over the moment a number sits near the middle, and rounding read as a choice of the nearer multiple does not. Spend time on the pairs where the estimate lands badly. Where one number rounds up and the other rounds down, the estimate can sit a long way off, and a child who has only ever seen close estimates will start treating them as answers. Finish on the judge bay. This is where the whole chapter comes back round: a worked answer out by a thousand is almost always a lending column that was never reduced, and the estimate is what catches it in seconds.

## Phases (what each mount shows and does; the words are in tool.tsx)

### 1 · Round first, then take away
**Screen:** two numbers with the estimate side empty and the place already set
**Does:** rounds both numbers and takes one rounded number off the other
**Win:** the estimate lands without a single column being written
**Feels:** the taking away turns out to be the easy half, and all of the work is in the rounding that comes first
**Goal:** the rounding happens before anything is taken away
**Observe:** The estimate landed with no column drawn on that side of the stage at all.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- works the exact subtraction first and rounds the answer afterwards, handing that in as the estimate. → the two roads are drawn side by side on the estimate side with the order of their steps numbered beside them. On 5,623 and 2,365, rounding first gives 6,000 and 2,000 for an estimate of 4,000, while working first gives 3,258 and rounding that afterwards gives 3,000. Both are printed, neither is crossed out, and the question's own wording is lit above them.
- rounds the greater number, leaves the smaller one standing exactly as it is, and takes that off the rounded one. → the number line strip opens under the number that was left alone, stretched so that the two nearest thousands sit on plates at its ends with the number pinned between them; the estimate side takes rounded numbers only, so that number waits out on its strip until it is sent to one end or the other. On 6,987 and 5,698 the pin on the second strip stands nearer the upper end, so it goes to 6,000 and the estimate is 1,000 against a worked answer of 1,289. Nothing already written is cleared.
**Misconception to break:** Working the subtraction first and rounding the answer, which is a different question.

### 2 · Close, not equal
**Screen:** both sides finished with the gap bar waiting between them
**Does:** runs the gap bar and reads how far apart the two answers are
**Win:** the gap bar is drawn short and labelled, and both answers stay standing
**Feels:** near is what was asked for, and then a pair turns up where near is not very near at all
**Goal:** the estimate sits near the exact difference rather than on it
**Observe:** Rounding 7,899 up by 101 and 4,766 up by 234 left the estimate 133 under the worked answer, so 3,000 stood beside 3,133.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- calls the estimate wrong because it does not equal the worked answer. → the two answers drop onto one number line together, the worked 3,133 and the estimated 3,000 that 7,899 and 4,766 give, and the space between them is drawn and labelled 133; the line runs the whole length of the worked answer, so the gap is seen against what was being measured rather than on its own. The two rounding moves are printed beside it, 7,899 going up by 101 and 4,766 going up by 234, and 234 less 101 is the 133 that was left over. Both answers stay standing.
- decides from one close pair that an estimate always lands near, and stops looking at the gap at all. → the pair 10,900 and 10,172 is loaded beside the one just finished and both gap bars are run at once. The first is 133 long against a worked answer of 3,133. The second is 272 long against a worked answer of 728, because the greater number was pushed up by 100 and the smaller was pulled down by 172, and 100 and 172 together are the 272. Neither bar is marked right or wrong and both stay drawn.
**Misconception to break:** Calling the estimate wrong because it does not come to 4,416.

### 3 · Both numbers, and the same place
**Screen:** the place picker with one number already rounded and the other waiting
**Does:** tries to round the second to a different place, then sets them both together
**Win:** the picker moves both strips together and the two rounded numbers land at the same place
**Feels:** one pair of numbers and two different estimates, and both of them survive
**Goal:** rounding to different places makes the estimate useless
**Observe:** On 10,900 and 10,172 the estimate came to 1,000 while the worked answer was only 728.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- sends the greater number to the nearest thousand and the smaller one to the nearest hundred, taking each to whichever place looks tidiest. → both strips open together under the two numbers with the same place marked on each, and the picker is shown moving the two as one piece. On 7,480 and 2,360 the mixed rounding gives 7,000 less 2,400, which is 4,600 and answers no question that was asked, while sending both to the nearest thousand gives 7,000 less 2,000, which is 5,000 against a worked answer of 5,120. All three numbers are left standing and none is crossed out.
- says the estimate made at one place must be wrong because it does not match the estimate made at another. → two strips open together under the same number, one stretched to the hundreds either side of it and one to the thousands either side, with the same pin standing on both; the nearer end is a different number on each strip, and on each strip it really is the nearer end. The exercise this chapter closes on never names a place at all, and the worked case printed directly above it goes to the nearest thousand, so the nearest thousand is the reading printed across the top of the stage and kept there for every pair in it. Nothing is marked.
**Misconception to break:** Rounding each number to whichever place makes it easiest, one at a time.

### 4 · An estimate tells you when an answer cannot be right
**Screen:** the judge bay holding a written answer with the estimate building beside it
**Does:** builds the estimate and decides whether the written answer can stand
**Win:** the gap bar stretches long and the written answer is sent back to be worked again
**Feels:** a fast answer that is roughly right catches a worked answer that is badly wrong
**Goal:** an answer far from its estimate is the one to work again
**Observe:** A worked answer sitting a whole thousand from its estimate turned out to have a lending column that was never reduced.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- trusts a written answer because its columns look neat, and never builds the estimate beside it. → the estimate builds in a moment on the right, 9,215 going to 9,000 and 3,684 going to 4,000 for an estimate of 5,000, and the gap bar is run from there to the written answer of 6,531, with the 1,531 between them printed on it. The written working is left standing exactly as it was with all its columns showing, and when it is worked again the answer is 5,531, the tens and the hundreds each fetching a ten from the column on their left.
- sees the gap stretch long and rubs the whole working out to begin it again. → the columns of the written subtraction light one at a time from the ones, and the first column whose digit does not agree with the rebuilt one is the only one left lit; on that working it is the thousands, which lent a ten to the hundreds on its right and was never left one less, so a nine went into the working where an eight belonged. The rest of the columns stay standing, and the gap bar shortens to nothing as that single column is put right. Nothing is cleared and nothing is marked.
**Misconception to break:** Trusting a worked answer because the columns look neat, instead of standing an estimate beside it.

## Delight
The second phase, the moment the gap bar is run twice. The first pair is 25,896 and 12,036: the worked answer is 13,860, the estimate is 14,000, and the two come down onto one line drawn from nothing up to the worked answer, where the 140 between them is a step so short it has to be labelled before it can be seen. Then 10,900 and 10,172 are loaded and the same bar is run again: the worked answer is 728, the estimate is 1,000, and the 272 between them is more than a third of the whole answer, so the bar runs off the end of a line the first pair had barely marked. The label roughly doubled and the bar changed out of all recognition, so the number that measures a gap and the size of that gap are both on the stage at once, and they are plainly not the same thing. Nothing else moves and the voice says nothing while it happens.

**General miss line:** Look at the strip under each number again, and at which end it is nearer.

## Answer key
To estimate a difference, round both numbers to the same place and then take the smaller rounded number off the greater one. Round first and subtract second, because working first and rounding the answer is a different question. Both numbers go to the same place, or the two are changed by different amounts and the gap between them shifts. The greater number still goes first after rounding. An estimate is meant to land near the exact difference, never to equal it, and a worked answer sitting far from its estimate is the one to go back to.

## Student controls
- **Place picker**: sets the place both numbers round to and moves the two together
- **Round first**: rounds both numbers before anything is taken away
- **Exact side**: works the two numbers in columns with the trades drawn
- **Estimate side**: takes one rounded number off the other, with no columns at all
- **Number line strip**: shows a number between the two nearest multiples of the chosen place
- **Gap bar**: draws and labels the distance between the estimate and the exact difference
- **Judge bay**: takes an answer somebody wrote and asks whether the estimate allows it

## Textbook (this topic)

**Key terms:** estimate, rounding, difference, nearest thousand
Both numbers are rounded to the same place and then subtracted, giving a quick answer close to the worked one. Round them. Numbers ending in zeros are easy to subtract in your head. Rounding both to the nearest thousand turns a long subtraction into a short one. The estimate is 3000 and the true difference is 3133. They sit close together, which is all an estimate ever promises. An estimate is for speed, and the worked answer is for being right. No. The two would be changed by different amounts and the gap between them would shift. Round both to the same place, and remember the greater number still goes first. Start with the worked one, because the estimate takes seconds to repeat. An estimate will not hand you the answer. It will tell you when an answer is far too big or far too small.
