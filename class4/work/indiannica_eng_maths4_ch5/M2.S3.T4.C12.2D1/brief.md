## What it is
**Description:** Divide place by place from the biggest place down, say what is left at each step, and write a zero only when the number is smaller than the divisor.
**Why:** Divide a number place by place from the biggest place down, and find out exactly when a zero belongs in the answer.
**Look:** A long division frame under place headings, a working window above it naming the place, a multiple strip beside it and a check bar underneath.
**Context:** Grade 4, and the method the rest of the chapter is built on. An adult sees a familiar written layout. What is actually at stake is one test, the test for a zero in the quotient. The summary box that follows the worked examples says a zero appears when a digit is not divisible by the divisor. That is the wrong test: the worked examples themselves divide thirty-five and fifty-six by six, neither divisible, and write five and nine. The test the first example states is that the number being divided is smaller than the divisor. A child who follows the box writes a zero at every step that leaves a remainder.
**For the child:** Start with the biggest place. Divide, write the digit on top, and say what is left. Bring the next digit down. If the number is smaller than the divisor, write a zero. Multiply back to check.
**Teaching notes:** Share play money into envelopes before the tool is opened, hundreds first, changing any hundred that will not go round into ten tens, then the tens, then the ones. Ask when an envelope got none of a note, and why a zero goes in that place. Then use the tool for its two refusals. It will not bring a digit down until what is left has been said, because the leftover is where the next number comes from. And it will not take a zero at a step where the divisor fits. The summary box after the worked examples says a zero appears when a digit is not divisible by the divisor. Do not teach that sentence. The worked examples divide thirty-five hundreds and fifty-six tens by six, neither of them divisible, and write five and nine. The first example states the real test itself: three is smaller than five, so put a zero in the quotient. A child who follows the box writes a zero at every step that leaves a remainder, and ends with an answer full of zeros. Say the test the same way every time: smaller than the divisor, a zero; not smaller, at least one fits. The check at the end is the other half of the box, and it is right: divisor times quotient, add the remainder, gives the dividend. The blocks bay is practice in adding the answers of several one-digit divisions, and its label says that a block's total is the sum of its quotients; the seating circle on the closing skills page, which needs that same reading, is served by the two-digit tool, because most of its divisions are by ten or by a number with two digits.

## Phases (what each mount shows and does; the words are in tool.tsx)

### 1 · Start with the biggest place
**Screen:** a frame with the dividend under its place headings and the working window on the biggest place
**Does:** divides the digit in the biggest place, writes the quotient digit above it and says what is left
**Win:** the first quotient digit lands above its place and what is left is carried to the next place
**Feels:** the biggest things go round first, and whatever is left is changed down into the next place rather than thrown away
**Goal:** long division starts with the digit in the biggest place, and what is left is carried on
**Observe:** The first quotient digit landed above the biggest place, and what was left went on to the next place rather than away.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- starts with the ones, as in adding and taking away, and builds the quotient from the right. → the working window is set on the ones as the child chose: six ones shared by three gives two, and four tens shared by three gives one ten with one ten left, and that ten has nowhere to go because the ones are already done. The frame then replays the division from the hundreds beside it, the two hundreds left over lit as they are changed into twenty tens and carried on, and both runs stay standing.
- writes a zero for the two thousands of two thousand seven hundred and thirty-six, then divides the seven hundreds on their own as if the two thousands had gone. → the working window holds the two thousands with their place name and then does not move on alone: the two thousands are changed into twenty hundreds and slide right to join the seven, and the window reads twenty-seven hundreds. The child's seven hundreds stays written above it, and the check bar later rebuilds the child's answer as seven hundred and thirty-six, two thousand short of the dividend.
**Misconception to break:** Starting with the ones, as in adding and taking away, and building the quotient backwards.

### 2 · Bring down and divide again
**Screen:** what is left after the first step, with the next digit waiting and the bring down arrow unlit
**Does:** fills the left slot, brings the next digit down, and divides the new number in the working window
**Win:** the working window shows the new number with its place name and the next quotient digit settles
**Feels:** what is left is not finished with, it is the front half of the next number
**Goal:** each step brings down the next digit and divides everything that has built up
**Observe:** The bring down arrow would not light until the left slot held a number.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- divides the five tens on their own and forgets the hundred that was left. → the child's step is run in the working window as five tens shared by four, one ten each, while the hundred left from the first step stays lit in the left slot, still waiting. The window is then rebuilt with that hundred changed into ten tens and slid beside the five, and it reads fifteen tens; both working numbers stay written, one above the other.
- takes two fours out of fifteen tens and carries seven tens on, when a third four still fits. → the seven is written in the left slot as the child gave it and lit against the four at the bracket, seven beside four. The multiple strip lights the rung for three fours, twelve, which still fits under fifteen, and the rung for four fours, sixteen, which does not. The child's two stays beside the quotient cell and the step is offered again, nothing marked.
**Misconception to break:** Dividing the 5 tens on their own and forgetting the hundred that was left.

### 3 · Smaller than the divisor means a zero
**Screen:** a step whose working number is smaller than the divisor, with an earlier step that left a remainder still lit
**Does:** compares the working number with the divisor, writes the zero, and carries the whole number on
**Win:** the zero lands above its place and the working number joins the next digit whole
**Feels:** a step with something left over can still fit the divisor several times, and only a number smaller than the divisor fits it none
**Goal:** a zero goes in the quotient only when the number being divided is smaller than the divisor
**Observe:** The gate took a zero at one step and handed it back at another step that left something over.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- writes a zero in the quotient at every step that leaves a remainder, because that step does not divide exactly. → the gate hands back the zero offered for nine thousands and the one offered for thirty-six ones, and at each the strip lights the rungs that fit: one seven under nine, five sevens under thirty-six. Only at three tens does the whole strip stay dark, because three is smaller than seven, and there the gate opens and takes the grey zero. The child's zeros stay written beside the cells they were offered for, and the check bar rebuilds the four hundred they would make as two thousand eight hundred, beside the nine thousand eight hundred and thirty-six.
- leaves out a zero that belongs, writing one hundred and forty-five remainder one for nine thousand eight hundred and thirty-six divided by seven. → the check bar multiplies seven by the child's one hundred and forty-five, adds the remainder, and the one thousand and sixteen it rebuilds slides under the dividend a whole place short. The frame is replayed with the gate over the tens cell, the strip stays dark against three tens because three is smaller than seven, the grey zero is taken, and the answer reads one thousand four hundred and five with every place filled.
**Misconception to break:** Writing a zero in the quotient at every step that leaves a remainder, because that step does not divide exactly.

### 4 · Multiply back to check
**Screen:** a finished division with the check bar empty under it
**Does:** multiplies the divisor by the quotient, adds the remainder, and lays the result beside the dividend
**Win:** the rebuilt number slides under the dividend and the two match digit for digit
**Feels:** the division is undone, and the dividend comes back only when the leftover is put back too
**Goal:** divisor times quotient, add the remainder, gives back the dividend
**Observe:** The rebuilt number slid under the dividend and matched it digit for digit.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- multiplies the divisor by the quotient and forgets to add the remainder. → the check bar builds eight times six hundred and forty-seven, five thousand one hundred and seventy-six, and slides it under the dividend, where the ones do not match. The remainder is then lifted out of its slot and added on, the rebuilt number becomes five thousand one hundred and seventy-eight, and it matches digit for digit. Both rebuilt numbers stay drawn.
- accepts eighty-four remainder two for seven thousand five hundred and sixty-two divided by nine, because every step shown was right. → the check bar builds nine times eighty-four, adds two, and the seven hundred and fifty-eight it makes slides under the dividend a whole place short. The frame is replayed step by step, and at the ones the two left is smaller than nine, so the gate takes the grey zero in the ones place. The check runs again on eight hundred and forty remainder two and matches digit for digit.
**Misconception to break:** Checking by multiplying the divisor by the quotient and forgetting to add the remainder.

## Delight
The third phase, the moment the working number is smaller than the divisor. The gate lifts, the whole strip of multiples lights and then goes dark rung by rung from the top down, because not even the first rung fits, and the grey zero drops into the quotient cell at full size. Then the working number does not fall away: it slides right, whole, and the next digit comes down beside it, so three tens and six ones become thirty-six ones in front of the child. It can be replayed as often as a finger likes, nothing is marked, and the voice says nothing at all while it happens.

**General miss line:** Read the working window with its place name, then lay the multiples beside it.

## Answer key
Start with the digit in the biggest place. Divide what is there by the divisor, write the quotient digit above that place, take the multiple away and say what is left. What is left joins the next digit brought down, and that is the next number to divide. When the number being divided at a step is smaller than the divisor, no divisor fits, so a zero goes in the quotient and the whole number is carried on. That is the only test. A step that leaves something over but is not smaller than the divisor still gets a digit of one or more. A zero before the first digit may be written or left off; a zero anywhere after it must be written. To check, multiply the divisor by the quotient and add the remainder; the answer is the dividend.

## Student controls
- **Working window**: shows the number being divided at each step, with its place name
- **Multiple strip**: lists the divisor times one up to times nine beside the window
- **Quotient row**: takes each quotient digit above its own place
- **Left slot**: takes what is left after each step, and must be filled before the next digit comes down
- **Bring down**: brings the next digit down beside what is left, unlit until the left slot is filled
- **Zero gate**: takes a zero only when the working number is smaller than the divisor
- **Check bar**: multiplies the divisor by the quotient, adds the remainder and stands it beside the dividend
- **Blocks bay**: holds three divisions to a block and adds their quotients

## Textbook (this topic)
**Pages:** 55, 56
**Key terms:** long division, bring down, quotient, remainder
Long division works from the biggest place down, bringing the next digit down each time, writing a zero when the number is smaller than the divisor, and checking by multiplying back. With the hundreds. Six hundreds shared by five gives one hundred each, and one hundred is left over to carry into the tens. Fifteen tens, and fifteen tens divided by five is three tens. Each step brings down the next digit and divides what has built up. A zero, because three is smaller than five. The test is whether the number is smaller than the divisor, not whether it divides exactly. Multiply six by five hundred and ninety-three, then add three. It gives back three thousand five hundred and sixty-one, so the division is right.
