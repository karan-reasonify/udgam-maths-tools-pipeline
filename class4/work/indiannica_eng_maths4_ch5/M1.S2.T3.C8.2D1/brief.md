## What it is
**Description:** Grow a division pattern a zero at a time, then cut a number's digits into a quotient and a remainder for ten, a hundred or a thousand.
**Why:** Watch a division pattern grow a zero at a time, then cut a number into a quotient and a remainder by where its digits fall.
**Look:** A number in digit cards under place headings, a splitter bar above it, two trays below it, and a pattern ladder at the left.
**Context:** Grade 4, and the first place in the chapter where a division is read rather than worked. An adult sees digits being cut off the end of a number. What is actually being taught is why the cut falls where it does: ten goes exactly into every ten, so only the ones can be left over, and a hundred goes into every hundred, so only the last two digits can be. A child who has learnt the cut as a trick puts it in the wrong place the first time the divisor changes, and copies a remainder with a zero in front of it, which the chapter's own table does once.
**For the child:** Count the zeros in the divisor. Cut off that many digits from the right. The digits in front are the quotient. The digits cut off are the remainder. Write the remainder as a number.
**Teaching notes:** Tie some sticks into bundles of ten and bundles of a hundred before the tool is opened, and ask how many whole tens a number makes and how many loose sticks are left. That is dividing by ten, and the written number already shows the answer: the loose sticks are the ones digit. The tool keeps the bundles and the digits on the stage together while the cut is made, so the rule is seen to be the bundles rather than a trick with the end of a number. Say the count of zeros aloud every time: one zero in ten, one digit cut off; two in a hundred, two digits; three in a thousand, three. The patterns at the start of this section work the same way from the other side, since a zero added to the dividend puts a zero on the quotient only while the divisor stays the same and the division leaves nothing over, so let a child add a zero to the divisor as well and see the quotient stay where it was. One row of the chapter's table for a thousand gives its remainder with a zero in front of sixteen. Copying the last three digits produces that, but a remainder is a number, and sixteen is written as sixteen. The box that divides one number four ways is worth doing on the tool with the trays left standing, so the four answers can be read in a row.

## Phases (what each mount shows and does; the words are in tool.tsx)

### 1 · A zero on the dividend, a zero on the quotient
**Screen:** the pattern ladder with one division fact on its bottom rung and the rungs above it empty
**Does:** adds a zero to the dividend, keeps the divisor, and writes the new quotient on each rung
**Win:** each rung fills with a quotient one zero longer than the rung below it
**Feels:** the divisor stayed still, and every zero the dividend gained turned up on the answer
**Goal:** ten times the dividend gives ten times the quotient when the divisor stays the same and nothing is left over
**Observe:** Each rung of the ladder held a quotient one zero longer than the rung below, while the divisor stayed put.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- adds a zero to the divisor as well as the dividend and expects the quotient to grow. → the rung is written as the child set it, with a zero on both numbers, and its quotient comes out equal to the rung below it, twelve beside twelve. The rung above is then written with the divisor left alone, its quotient one zero longer, and both rungs stand together with nothing marked.
- adds one zero to the quotient when the dividend has gained two, writing a hundred and twenty for eight thousand four hundred divided by seven. → the rungs are walked from the bottom one at a time, each adding one zero to the dividend and one to the quotient, with the count of zeros written beside each number. The child's answer stays on its rung beside the one the ladder builds, and seven times it is written under it, eight hundred and forty, beside the eight thousand four hundred it should have made.
**Misconception to break:** Adding a zero to the divisor as well, which leaves the quotient at 12.

### 2 · Ten splits off the ones digit
**Screen:** a three-digit number in cards with the splitter set to ten and the bundle view open
**Does:** drops the bar, then matches the two trays to the bundles of ten and the loose sticks
**Win:** the loose sticks and the ones digit light together in the remainder tray
**Feels:** the loose sticks are the ones digit, and every bundle of ten is already in the digits in front of it
**Goal:** divided by ten, the ones digit is the remainder and the digits in front are the quotient
**Observe:** The loose sticks and the ones digit lit together in the remainder tray.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- takes the first digit as the remainder because it is the one read first. → the bar drops at the ten setting, one card in from the right, and the bundle view lights the whole bundles of ten with the quotient tray and the loose sticks with the remainder tray. The bundles the child's digit stands for, seven bundles of a hundred, are lit beside the eight loose sticks, and the child's digit stays in the slot with nothing marked.
- writes the quotient as the single digit next to the bar, three, instead of the whole number in front of it. → the quotient tray writes its digits under itself as one number, seventy-three, and the bundle view counts the tens out: seven hundreds are seventy tens, and three more tens make seventy-three. The child's three stays beside the tray, and ten threes add eight is written under it, thirty-eight, beside the seven hundred and thirty-eight it should have rebuilt.
**Misconception to break:** Taking the first digit as the remainder because it is the one read first.

### 3 · A hundred takes two digits, a thousand takes three
**Screen:** a four-digit number with the splitter at a hundred and its thousand setting waiting
**Does:** cuts at a hundred, then moves the bar one place left for a thousand and cuts again
**Win:** both cuts stand one above the other, the bar one place further left for the thousand
**Feels:** one more zero in the divisor, one more digit that cannot make a whole one of it
**Goal:** divided by a hundred the last two digits are the remainder, and by a thousand the last three
**Observe:** The bar moved one card further left each time the divisor gained a zero.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- cuts off one digit whatever the divisor, instead of one digit for each zero in it. → the bar drops where the child placed it, one card in, and the trays read eight hundred and twenty-five and four. The check under the trays multiplies a hundred by eight hundred and twenty-five, adds four, and stands the eighty-two thousand five hundred and four it builds beside the eight thousand two hundred and fifty-four. Both stay drawn, and the zeros on the setting key are then counted one at a time.
- writes the two trays the wrong way round, giving the last two digits as the quotient. → the trays are read back with their names lit, and the check multiplies a hundred by the child's quotient, fifty-four, and adds eighty-two: five thousand four hundred and eighty-two stands beside the eight thousand two hundred and fifty-four, plainly different. The bundle view then fills the trays in its own order, whole hundreds first and loose sticks last, with the child's answer still written.
**Misconception to break:** Cutting off the same number of digits whatever the divisor, instead of one digit for each zero in it.

### 4 · A remainder is a number
**Screen:** a five-digit number cut by a thousand, with a zero standing first in the remainder tray
**Does:** reads the remainder tray, lifts the front zero off, and writes the remainder as a number
**Win:** the front zero is set aside and the remainder settles, written the way any number is
**Feels:** the zeros in front were holding no place at all, and the number reads the same without them
**Goal:** a zero in front of a remainder's other digits changes nothing and is not written
**Observe:** The zero lifted off the front of the remainder, and the tray wrote the same number without it.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- writes the remainder with its zeros in front, copying the three digits the bar cut off. → the front zeros lift off one at a time into the saucer at full size, and the number written under the tray does not change as each one goes. The child's remainder stays written beside it exactly as it was entered, and the tray's reading is then written into the answer slot under it, seven, the way any number is written.
- lifts every zero out of the remainder, the one in the middle as well, and writes fifty-six for five hundred and six. → the lift takes only a zero standing in front of the other digits, and the zero with a five on its left stays in the tray, lit and at full size. The bundle view counts what the remainder holds, five bundles of a hundred and six loose sticks, and the child's fifty-six is laid out beside it as five tens and six, plainly fewer. Both stay drawn.
**Misconception to break:** Writing the remainder as 007 because those were the three digits cut off.

## Delight
The fourth phase, the moment the front zero is lifted. The zero rises off the remainder tray and walks to the saucer at full size, the digits left behind do not move a hair, and the number written under the tray does not change by so much as a letter. Put the zero back and it changes nothing again. Lift a zero that has a digit on its left and the lift will not take it; that zero stays lit in the tray, and the bundle view under it shows the hundred it is holding open. The lifting can be done as often as a finger likes, nothing is marked, and the voice says nothing at all while it happens.

**General miss line:** Lay the bundles out under the cards again, and count the whole bundles first.

## Answer key
When the divisor stays the same and the division leaves nothing over, ten times the dividend gives ten times the quotient, so each zero added to the dividend adds a zero to the quotient. Divided by ten, the ones digit is the remainder and the number formed by the digits in front of it is the quotient. Divided by a hundred, the last two digits form the remainder and the rest form the quotient. Divided by a thousand, the last three digits form the remainder. One digit is cut off for every zero in the divisor. A remainder is a number, so any zero standing in front of its other digits is not written: the last three digits zero, zero and seven make the remainder seven. Divided by one, a number is its own quotient with nothing left over.

## Student controls
- **Splitter bar**: drops in from the right hand end, one digit for ten, two for a hundred, three for a thousand
- **Quotient tray**: takes the digits to the left of the bar and reads them as the quotient
- **Remainder tray**: takes the digits to the right of the bar and reads them as one number
- **Front zero lift**: lifts a zero standing in front of the remainder's other digits and shows it changes nothing
- **Bundle view**: draws the number as bundles and loose sticks under the digit cards
- **Pattern ladder**: adds a zero to the dividend a rung at a time with the divisor fixed
- **One control**: runs the same number divided by one, so four answers stand side by side
