## What it is
**Description:** Deal a heap onto plates one round at a time, name the four numbers, and try one plate, as many plates as things, and no plates at all.
**Why:** Share a heap equally onto plates, name the four numbers that come out of it, and find out which sharings cannot be done at all.
**Look:** A tray of counters at the left, a row of plates along the middle with a leftover dish beside them, and four name tags waiting at the side.
**Context:** Grade 4, the first working topic of the division chapter, and the one every later topic leans on. An adult sees counters being shared out, which a class can do with real counters. What is actually being settled is the language and the limits: which of the four numbers is which, why a leftover can never be as big as the divisor, and which of the chapter's rules hold for every number. Two of those rules are printed with the word always and no condition, and zero divided by zero would then get three answers: one, zero and not possible. Only the last is right, so this tool states the other two for a divisor other than zero.
**For the child:** Deal one counter to every plate, a round at a time. Count the rounds. Put what cannot go round in the dish. Drop each name tag on what it names. Then try one plate, and no plates at all.
**Teaching notes:** Share a real heap onto paper plates before the tool is opened, one counter to each plate a round at a time, and count the rounds out loud. Each round takes away as many counters as there are plates, which is the repeated taking away the recap asks for, and the number of rounds is the share on each plate. Then use the tool for the two things a desk cannot do. It will not let a sharing stop while the leftover could still go round once more, which is why the remainder is always smaller than the divisor. And it will not start a deal onto no plates at all. The chapter states its rule for a number divided by itself, and its rule for zero divided by a number, with the word always and no condition. Zero divided by zero meets all three of its rules and would get three answers: one, zero and not possible. Only the last is right, so say each rule for a divisor other than zero, and if a child asks about zero divided by zero, the answer is that it is not possible, like every division by zero. The recap's worked model turns six fifteens into ninety divided by fifteen, and three of its items divide by the other number, so show both division facts from every multiplication rather than letting the model look like the only way. The summary box calls a division that comes out whole a perfect division. Every division in the chapter has a whole number quotient, so say what is meant: nothing left over. The six highlighted words make a set of flashcards, and four of them are the name tags on this bench.

## Phases (what each mount shows and does; the words are in tool.tsx)

### 1 · One multiplication, two divisions
**Screen:** counters laid out in equal rows, with a fact card reading the multiplication above them
**Does:** reads the rows one way and writes a division, then turns the card and writes the other
**Win:** both division facts settle under the fact card, one for each number that was multiplied
**Feels:** one set of rows, read from two sides, and each side divides by a different number
**Goal:** a multiplication fact with no zero in it gives two division facts, one for each number that was multiplied
**Observe:** The fact card turned round and gave a second division without a single counter moving.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- writes one division from the fact and stops, as though a multiplication could be undone only one way. → the fact card turns round on its own with the counters left exactly where they stand, and the rows are read the other way, thirteen rows of nine where there were nine rows of thirteen. A second blank division line opens under the child's first one, which stays written, and the new rows light one at a time while their count climbs at the top left.
- answers twelve when asked how many times twelve could be taken away from eighty-four. → the takes are replayed from the tray one at a time, each take of twelve lifting off as one group and landing in a row along the bench, and the running count climbs by one for each take, not by twelve, until the tray is empty at seven. The child's twelve stays in the slot and the row of seven takes lies beside it, counted.
**Misconception to break:** Writing only one of the two facts, as if a multiplication could be undone in only one way.

### 2 · Naming the four numbers
**Screen:** a heap in the tray, a row of plates, an empty leftover dish and four name tags at the side
**Does:** deals the heap a round at a time, then drops each name tag on the thing it names
**Win:** the four tags settle on the heap, the plate dial, one plate's share and the dish
**Feels:** four names, four different things on the bench, and each tag has only one place to land
**Goal:** the dividend is shared by the divisor, the quotient is each share and the remainder is what is left
**Observe:** Every name tag settled on only one thing on the bench.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- calls six the quotient in seventy-nine shared among six, because it is the smaller of the two numbers in the story. → the quotient tag is left lying where the child dropped it, on the plate dial, and the bench deals the seventy-nine a round at a time onto six plates: the round count at the top left climbs to thirteen and one plate's share reads thirteen underneath it. The words quotient and divisor are lit side by side on the tag rail, and one plate is lit with its count.
- drops the remainder tag on one of the plates, because the leftover counter looks like one more share. → the leftover counter is carried to that plate, which now holds fourteen while the other five hold thirteen, and the six plates are drawn plainly unequal side by side. The empty dish beside them lights, the counter walks back into it, and the plates settle level again with the child's tag still lying where it fell.
**Misconception to break:** Calling 6 the quotient because it is the smaller of the two numbers in the story.

### 3 · Why the remainder stays small
**Screen:** a deal stopped early, with as many counters in the leftover dish as there are plates
**Does:** tries to close the dish, deals one more round instead, then runs the check bar
**Win:** one more round leaves fewer in the dish than there are plates, and the check bar rebuilds the heap exactly
**Feels:** a leftover as big as the plates is not a leftover yet, it is one more round waiting
**Goal:** the remainder is always smaller than the divisor, and divisor times quotient add remainder gives the dividend
**Observe:** The dish would not close while it held as many counters as there were plates.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- stops dealing and writes the nine in the dish as the remainder, though seven of them could still go round. → the dish lid stays open and all seven plates light, with seven of the nine counters in the dish lit to match them. The deal control lights, and one more round is dealt from the dish: each plate takes one, the dish is left holding two, and the lid closes by itself. The child's nine stays in the slot beside the new count, nothing marked.
- checks by multiplying the plates by the share and stops, without adding what is in the dish. → the check bar builds the heap from the plates alone, seven rows of eight, and it stands beside the tray two counters short of the heap the tray began with. The two in the dish are then carried up and added, and the rebuilt heap matches the tray counter for counter. Both rebuilt heaps stay drawn.
**Misconception to break:** Stopping the sharing while the leftover pile is still as big as the divisor.

### 4 · Dividing by one, by itself and by zero
**Screen:** the same heap in the tray with the rule bay open beside the plate dial
**Does:** deals onto one plate, then onto as many plates as counters, then empties the tray, then tries no plates
**Win:** three deals settle, and with no plates the deal never starts and the stage says there is nowhere to put a share
**Feels:** no plates is not a small number of plates, it is nowhere at all to put a share
**Goal:** dividing by one leaves a number alone, a number other than zero divided by itself is one, and dividing by zero is not possible
**Observe:** With no plates the deal never started, and no number appeared anywhere on the stage.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- says thirty-four divided by zero is zero, because with no plates nothing seems to be handed out. → the dial is turned to none and the deal control stays unlit, the thirty-four counters staying in the tray exactly as they were. The caption says there is nowhere to put a share, no number is drawn anywhere on the stage for it, and the child's zero stays in the slot beside the pad's key reading not possible.
- answers one for zero divided by zero, which has no answer, by reading it as a number divided by itself. → the tray is emptied and the dial turned to none together, and the deal control stays unlit exactly as it did for thirty-four, because there are no plates and so nowhere to put a share, whatever the tray holds. The caption says this one is not possible too, the rule for a number and itself is lit with the words other than zero, and the child's one stays in the slot.
**Misconception to break:** Saying a number divided by zero is zero, or is the number itself, because nothing seems to have been done to it.

## Delight
The third phase, the moment one more round is dealt from a dish that holds as many counters as there are plates. Every plate lights at once, one counter leaps from the dish to each plate in the same instant, and the dish is left holding fewer than the plates, so its lid swings shut by itself. The check bar then runs without being asked: the plates' shares stack into rows of ten beside the tray, the dish tips its last few in after them, and the rebuilt heap stands level with the tray, counter for counter. The round can be undone and dealt again as often as a finger likes, nothing is marked, and the voice says nothing at all while it happens.

**General miss line:** Deal one more round in your head. Could every plate still take one?

## Answer key
Division is equal sharing or equal grouping. The number being shared is the dividend, the number of equal parts is the divisor, the size of each part is the quotient, and anything left over is the remainder. The remainder is always smaller than the divisor, because as many as the divisor would give every part one more. Divisor times quotient, add the remainder, gives back the dividend. A division that leaves nothing over is sometimes called a perfect division, and that is all the phrase means: nothing left over. A multiplication fact with no zero in it gives two division facts, one for each number that was multiplied. Dividing by one leaves a number as it is. A number other than zero divided by itself is one. Zero divided by a number other than zero is zero. Dividing by zero is not possible, for every number, zero included.

## Student controls
- **Plate dial**: sets how many plates the heap is shared onto, from none upwards
- **Deal a round**: puts one counter on every plate and lifts that many out of the tray
- **Round counter**: keeps the number of rounds dealt, which is the share on each plate
- **Leftover dish**: holds what cannot go round, and will not close while every plate could take one more
- **Name tags**: dividend, divisor, quotient and remainder, each dropped on the thing it names
- **Fact card**: turns a picture of equal rows round, so one multiplication reads as two divisions
- **Check bar**: multiplies the plates by the share and adds the dish to rebuild the heap
- **Rule bay**: sets one plate, as many plates as counters, an empty tray, or no plates at all
