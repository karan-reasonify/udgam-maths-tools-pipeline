## What it is
**Description:** Divide down to the value of one, multiply up to the value of many, answer in the units the question asks, and find the value of one either way round.
**Why:** Find the value of one thing by dividing, the value of many by multiplying, and answer in exactly the units the question asks for.
**Look:** Two rails across the stage, things on top and their value underneath, tied at every stop, with an answer slot and its unit at the right.
**Context:** Grade 4, the chapter's recap, and the one place where a division and a multiplication are used together on purpose. An adult sees a shopping sum. What is actually being taught is where to stop: at the value of one, which is found by dividing, then at the number the question wants, which is found by multiplying, and then at the unit the question counts in. One exercise gives rice for a number of people, and the rice one person needs is a part of a kilogram, which this chapter cannot write. The number of people one kilogram feeds is whole, and the chapter's own self-assessment says the value of one is found and vice versa, so the tool finds it either way round.
**For the child:** Find what one costs by dividing. Find what many cost by multiplying. If one way gives less than one, turn it round. Answer in the units the question asks for.
**Teaching notes:** Price a pack from a list before the tool is opened: what one item costs, then what a different number costs. Then use the tool for the two things a written line cannot show. It holds both ways of finding the value of one at once, and it keeps the unit the question asks for in front of the child until the answer is in it. The worked example for this topic buys eight scarves for twelve hundred rupees and finds that ten cost one thousand five hundred rupees, and it is numbered ten; there is no example nine anywhere in the chapter, so nobody needs to look for it. Three of the exercise questions divide cleanly the taught way. The rice question does not: the rice one person needs is a part of a kilogram, which this chapter has no way to write. One kilogram feeds a whole number of people, so find that instead. The chapter's own self-assessment says the value of one is found and vice versa, and either way round is exactly that. Watch the hostel question, which asks how much more a longer stay costs, so its answer is the cost of the extra days and not of the whole stay, and the saving question, which asks for weeks, so the days the division gives are not the answer. The self-assessment has six lines. One of them says the child can estimate a quotient, which the chapter never teaches, so a child cannot honestly tick it from this chapter; say so rather than letting it pass.

## Phases (what each mount shows and does; the words are in tool.tsx)

### 1 · Find the value of one first
**Screen:** the two rails at the first stop, holding a number of things and their value
**Does:** walks both rails back to one thing and writes its value
**Win:** both rails stop at one and the value of one settles on the lower rail
**Feels:** the value of many is shared out equally among the things, and one thing carries one share
**Goal:** the value of one is the value of many divided by how many there are
**Observe:** Both rails walked back to one in a single movement, and the value of one landed at that stop.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- multiplies two hundred and seventy by six instead of dividing, which gives the cost of thirty-six notebooks. → the rails are walked the child's way, out from six notebooks to thirty-six, and the value rail reads one thousand six hundred and twenty rupees there, the child's number at a stop with thirty-six things above it. The rails are then walked back from six to one, and forty-five rupees settles under one notebook. Both stops stay drawn along the rails.
- divides six by two hundred and seventy, which asks how many notebooks one rupee buys, and gets stuck. → the turn control shows the child's way round, with the notebooks on the lower rail, and walking back to one rupee stops short of the tick for one notebook, a part drawn and labelled as a part. The lamp lights over the other way, where one notebook is forty-five rupees, and both ways stay drawn.
**Misconception to break:** Multiplying 270 by 6 instead of dividing, which gives 1620 rupees, the cost of 36 notebooks.

### 2 · Then multiply up to many
**Screen:** the rails standing at one thing, with the number the question wants lit further along
**Does:** walks the rails out from one to the number wanted by multiplying
**Win:** the rails stop at the number wanted and its value lands in the answer slot
**Feels:** once one thing is priced, any number of them is only that price again and again
**Goal:** the value of many is the value of one times how many are needed
**Observe:** From one, the rails walked out to the number wanted, and the value grew by the value of one at every stop.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- divides again at the second step, because the first step was a division. → the child's division is walked on the rails and lands at a stop where fourteen notebooks would cost less than one notebook did, which the rails draw and leave standing. Multiply to many then walks out from one to fourteen, the value rail climbing by forty-five rupees at every stop, and six hundred and thirty rupees settles under fourteen notebooks.
- multiplies the price of all nine tickets by four, as if four were a number of lots of nine. → the rails stop at thirty-six tickets for the child's four thousand three hundred and twenty rupees, and that stop stays drawn. The rails are then walked back from nine to one ticket, one hundred and twenty rupees, and out again to four, four hundred and eighty, with both walks left standing.
**Misconception to break:** Dividing again at the second step, because the first step was a division.

### 3 · Answer the question that was asked
**Screen:** a finished working whose result is in days, with the answer slot asking for weeks
**Does:** reads the unit in the answer slot and changes the result into it
**Win:** the converter turns the days into weeks and the answer settles in the unit asked
**Feels:** the working can be finished and the question still not answered, until the number is in the units the question counts
**Goal:** the answer is given in the units the question asks for, and how much more is the extra part only
**Observe:** The slot would not close on a count of days when the question counted in weeks, and the bracket measured only the extra stay.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- answers fifty-six because it came out of the division, when the question asked for weeks. → the answer slot shows weeks written after its box and hands the fifty-six back, still written beside it, with the unit strip lighting days against the child's number. The converter then lays the days out in rows of seven, eight rows, and eight weeks settles in the slot.
- answers five thousand four hundred rupees, the cost of all nine days, when the question asked how much more two extra days cost. → the more bracket opens over only the two stops beyond the week the story already paid for, and measures one thousand two hundred rupees there. The child's total stays written beside the rails, the week's four thousand two hundred is lit as already paid, and the two numbers are shown to differ by exactly that week.
**Misconception to break:** Answering 56 because it came out of the division, when the question asked for weeks.

### 4 · Choosing which one to find
**Screen:** a story where one way round gives less than one of the unit, with the turn control waiting
**Does:** tries the first way, sees a part, turns the rails, and finds the whole number value
**Win:** the lamp lights over the whole number way and the answer comes out whole
**Feels:** the value of one does not have to be the value of one person, it can be what one kilogram does
**Goal:** the value of one can be found either way round, and the way that comes out whole is the one to use
**Observe:** One way round gave a part of a kilogram, and the same story turned round gave a whole number of people.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- insists on the rice for one person, finds it is a part of a kilogram, and says the question cannot be done. → the rails walk back to one person and stop short of the tick for one kilogram, the part drawn and labelled as a part not yet writable, and left standing. The turn control then swaps the rails, walks the kilograms back to one, and fifteen people settles under one kilogram with the lamp lit over it. Seven hundred and twenty people are then walked out to forty-eight kilograms.
- turns the rails round and then multiplies seven hundred and twenty by fifteen, because the second step of the method was a multiplication. → the child's ten thousand eight hundred kilograms is drawn at its stop on the kilogram rail, far past the thirty that fed four hundred and fifty people, with the people rail above it reading only seven hundred and twenty. The rails are then walked from one kilogram feeding fifteen people out to seven hundred and twenty people, fifteen at a stop, and forty-eight kilograms settles there.
**Misconception to break:** Stopping because 30 kilograms cannot be shared among 450 people in whole kilograms, instead of turning the question round.

## Delight
The fourth phase, when the turn control is pressed on a way that gave a part. The two rails lift and cross over each other in one smooth movement, the ties staying taut between them the whole time, so the kilograms land on top and the people underneath. Divide to one then walks the turned rails back to one kilogram, the lamp lights over them, and a whole number of people settles at that stop, while the part from the first way stays drawn short of one on the record strip below. Turn it back and forth as often as a finger likes; the lamp follows the whole number way each time, nothing is marked, and the voice says nothing at all while it happens.

**General miss line:** Walk both rails back a stop, and read the things and the value together.

## Answer key
The unitary method finds the value of one unit first and then the value of the number needed. The value of one is found by dividing the value of many by how many there are, and the value of many by multiplying the value of one by how many are needed. The value of one can be found either way round: the value of one thing, or how many things one unit of value covers. Use the way that comes out whole. Give the answer in the units the question asks for, changing days to weeks or months to a year where the question counts in those. When a question asks how much more, the answer is the value of the extra part only, not the total.

- **Unit converter**: changes days to weeks and months to a year beside the answer slot
