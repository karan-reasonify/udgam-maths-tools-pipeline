## What it is
**Description:** Do the steps of long division in your head, write each leftover small beside the next digit, and still write every zero.
**Why:** Divide by one digit in your head, writing only the answer and the small leftovers, and find out that nothing of the long method has gone missing.
**Look:** One strip across the stage with the divisor behind a bar, the quotient row under it, and the long lane below that.
**Context:** Grade 4, and the quick way of doing what the earlier topics did slowly. An adult sees a shortcut. What is actually being tested is whether the long steps are still being done when they are no longer written, and the step most likely to be lost is the zero. The summary box two topics back gave the wrong test for a zero, saying that a digit which is not divisible makes one. In short division there is no written step to catch that. The test is whether the number is smaller than the divisor, and the strip makes it the only way a zero can be written.
**For the child:** Divide the first digit, or the first two. Write the answer underneath. Write what is left small beside the next digit. If the number is smaller than the divisor, write a zero. Check it the long way.
**Teaching notes:** Race it before the tool is opened: one partner divides the long way on paper, the other the short way, and they compare. The short way wins on time and loses when a step is skipped, and the step most often skipped is the zero. The summary box two topics back says a zero appears when a digit is not divisible by the divisor. In short division there is nothing written to catch that mistake, so a child following the box writes a zero under every digit that leaves something over. The test is smaller than the divisor, and the strip will take a zero only then. The worked examples for this topic print their working without units, writing eight where they mean eight tens, so read each step aloud with its place name. The first exercise sets eight quick frames, and two of them leave a remainder the instruction does not ask for; ask for it anyway, because the remainder is part of the answer. One of those frames divides by ten, which is not a one-digit divisor, and is easiest done the way the topic on ten, a hundred and a thousand taught.

## Opening (first frame of every mount)
Every mount comes up already furnished for its phase with nothing in front of it. On the first phase a two-digit dividend stands in the strip with the divisor behind its bar, the quotient row empty and the long lane drawn empty beneath it. On the second a three-digit dividend stands with its first quotient digit already written and the space beside the next digit empty, waiting for what was left. On the third a dividend stands with one digit smaller than the divisor, the guard closed over the quotient cell under that digit, and a later step that will leave something over. On the fourth one division is loaded in the strip and in the long lane together, both unworked. The running count at the top left holds the quotient digits written against the digits of the dividend, and the answer slot and the pad stand empty down the right. One thing moves, once: the first digit cell, or the empty space beside the next digit, or the guard, or the long lane's first row, gives one slow pulse while the voice asks which digit comes first. That pulse is the only invitation, and a finger on it is the whole way in.

## Phases (what each mount shows and does; the words are in tool.tsx)

### 1 · The same steps, kept in your head
**Screen:** a two-digit dividend on the strip with the divisor behind its bar and the quotient row empty
**Does:** divides each digit in turn and writes only the quotient digit under it
**Win:** the quotient row fills and the long lane below shows the same digits it would have written
**Feels:** nothing of the long way has gone, it has only stopped being written down
**Goal:** short division does the steps of long division in the head and writes only the quotient
**Observe:** The quotient row filled while the long lane underneath wrote out every step the strip had kept in the head.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- thinks the short way is a separate trick that could give a different answer from the long way. → the long lane is worked from the same digits while the child fills the strip, and each quotient digit it writes lights the matching cell above at the same moment: eight tens by four is two tens in both lanes, and four ones by four is one in both. The two rows of digits are drawn level, with nothing marked.
- writes two hundred and two for eight hundred and forty-eight divided by four, working the hundreds and the ones and losing the tens in the middle. → the guard over the tens cell hands the zero back, because four tens is not smaller than four, and the long lane works that step in full beneath it: four tens by four, one ten. The strip's tens cell takes the one, and the child's two hundred and two stays written beside the row.
**Misconception to break:** Thinking short division is a different method that could give a different answer.

### 2 · Carry the leftover to the next digit
**Screen:** a three-digit dividend where the first step leaves something over
**Does:** writes the leftover small beside the next digit and divides the number the two make
**Win:** the small leftover and the digit light together as one number and the next quotient digit lands
**Feels:** the leftover tens have not gone anywhere, they have been changed into ones and put beside the ones already there
**Goal:** each leftover is written small beside the next digit and joins it
**Observe:** The small leftover and the next digit lit together and were read as one number.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- divides the six on its own and drops the three that was carried. → the child's step is run in the long lane as six ones by seven, which gives nothing, while the three tens left from the step before stay lit in that lane, still waiting. The check under the lanes rebuilds the child's answer as five hundred and sixty-six, thirty short of the dividend. The small three is then walked into place beside the six, the two light together as thirty-six ones, and the step runs again.
- adds the carried three to the six and divides nine, as a carried number was used in adding. → the long lane changes the three tens into thirty ones and lays them beside the six ones, and the tag row reads thirty-six ones, not nine. The child's nine stays written above the tag, and the check under the lanes rebuilds the child's answer, eighty-one remainder two, as five hundred and sixty-nine, twenty-seven short of the dividend.
**Misconception to break:** Dividing the 6 on its own and dropping the 3 that was carried.

### 3 · A zero still has to be written
**Screen:** a dividend with one digit smaller than the divisor and another step that leaves a remainder
**Does:** compares each number with the divisor, writes the zero where it is smaller, and carries the whole digit on
**Win:** the zero settles under its digit and the digit slides on to join the next one
**Feels:** a step with something left over can still fit the divisor, and the only step that gets a zero is one where the divisor fits no times at all
**Goal:** a zero goes in the quotient whenever the number is smaller than the divisor, and only then
**Observe:** The guard took a zero under one digit and handed one back under another digit whose step left something over.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- writes a zero under every digit whose step leaves a remainder, because it does not divide exactly. → the guard hands back each zero offered at a step that is not smaller than the divisor, and lights on the times table card the facts that fit: twenty-seven tens takes four sixes and thirty-five ones takes five. Only under the two hundreds, where two is smaller than six, does the guard open and take the grey zero. The child's zeros stay written beside their cells, and the long lane works each step in full underneath.
- writes sixty-three for four thousand eight hundred and twenty-four divided by eight, leaving out the zero where two tens are smaller than eight. → the tens cell of the quotient row is lit empty between the six and the three. The guard opens over it, because two tens is smaller than eight, and takes the grey zero, and the two tens are carried small beside the four. The check under the lanes rebuilds the child's sixty-three as five hundred and four, beside the four thousand eight hundred and twenty-four.
**Misconception to break:** Writing a zero under every digit whose step leaves a remainder, because it does not divide exactly.

### 4 · Long or short, the same answer
**Screen:** one division loaded in the short strip and in the long lane, both unworked
**Does:** works the short strip, then the long lane, and compares the two answers digit by digit
**Win:** each quotient digit lights in both lanes together and the two remainders stand level
**Feels:** two ways of writing the same steps, and a disagreement points to one step rather than a vote between two answers
**Goal:** long and short division give the same quotient and remainder
**Observe:** When the two lanes disagreed, only the step that slipped was lit in the long lane.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- trusts one way over the other when the two disagree, instead of looking for the step that slipped. → the two lanes are compared cell by cell from the left, each matching digit lighting in both, and the first cell that does not match stops the comparison and lights the step behind it in the long lane. Both answers stay written, and that one step is worked again in both lanes at once.
- gives sixty-eight remainder six for five thousand four hundred and seventy-eight divided by nine, the short way losing the zero the long way writes. → the long lane writes the step in full, seven tens by nine, no nines, and puts a grey zero in the tens place, and the strip's tens cell lights empty against it. The check under the lanes rebuilds the child's answer as six hundred and eighteen, beside the five thousand four hundred and seventy-eight.
**Misconception to break:** Trusting one way over the other when the two disagree, instead of looking for the step that slipped.

## Delight
The fourth phase, when the long lane is worked under a finished strip. Each quotient digit the long lane writes travels up and lights the same digit in the strip's quotient row, and each leftover the long lane writes in full slides up and settles onto the small raised numeral the strip already carries, fitting it exactly. The two remainders arrive at the right hand end on the same tick and stand level. It can be run as often as a finger likes, nothing is marked, and the voice says nothing at all while it happens.

**General miss line:** Say the step out loud with its place name, and look at the long lane under it.

## Answer key
Short division does the steps of long division in the head. Divide the first digit, or the first two if the first is smaller than the divisor, write the quotient digit under it, and write what is left small beside the next digit. The small leftover and that digit make the next number to divide. When that number is smaller than the divisor, a zero goes in the quotient and the whole number is carried on to the next digit. Long and short division always give the same quotient and remainder. Short division is quickest when the times table of the divisor is known, which is why it is used mostly for divisors of one digit.

## Student controls
- **Digit cells**: hold the dividend's digits under their place headings
- **Quotient row**: takes one quotient digit under each digit of the dividend
- **Small leftover**: writes what is left small beside the next digit and joins the two into one number
- **Zero guard**: takes a zero only when the number is smaller than the divisor
- **Long lane**: runs the same division in full under the strip, step by step
- **Times table card**: shows the divisor's times table at the side
- **Compare**: lights each quotient digit in both lanes when the two ways agree
