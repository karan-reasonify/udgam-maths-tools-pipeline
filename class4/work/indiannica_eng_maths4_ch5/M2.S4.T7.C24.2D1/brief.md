## What it is
**Description:** Pull the total and the number of equal parts out of a story, find a missing factor, catch the zero the working hides, and keep the remainder when the question asks for it.
**Why:** Read a story, decide what is shared and into how many, and give every part of the answer the question asks for.
**Look:** A story card at the top with its numbers lit, two slots and a share bar under it, a division frame beside them and an answer builder at the right.
**Context:** Grade 4, and the topic where division stops being given as a division. An adult sees word problems. What is actually being taught is deciding: which number is the total being shared, which is the number of equal parts, and which part of the answer each question wants. The worked example that finds a missing factor brings two digits down together in its figure and never shows the step that puts the zero in two hundred and three, and without that step the answer reads twenty-three. The exercise that follows ends on two questions that each ask what is left, and there the remainder is an answer and not a leftover to be dropped.
**For the child:** Find the number being shared. Find how many equal parts. Divide one digit at a time. Answer every question that is asked. Give each answer its unit.
**Teaching notes:** Plan a trip on paper before the tool is opened: how many children, how many seats in a van, how many full vans, and how many children are left over. Then ask whether the answer to how many vans are needed is the quotient or one more than it. That question is the whole of this topic, because it makes the class decide what each part of a division means in the story. The opening page of the chapter asked how far a car goes on one litre, from the whole trip and the petrol used, and a box on this topic sends the child back to it to answer it now: the distance is shared among the litres, so it is the dividend. The worked example that finds a missing factor divides five thousand and seventy-five by twenty-five, and its figure brings the seven and the five down together. Seven tens is smaller than twenty-five, so a zero goes in the tens place; skip that step and the answer reads twenty-three instead of two hundred and three. The car park question assumes every level holds the same number of vehicles, which it does not say, so say it. The last two exercise questions each ask what is left, and there the remainder is an answer. The Critical Thinking box asks for the average time spent on each question and never says what an average is; here it means the time each question would take if every one took the same time. It also needs the time between two clock readings, which this chapter does not teach, so work that part out together first.

## Phases (what each mount shows and does; the words are in tool.tsx)

### 1 · What is shared, and into how many
**Screen:** a story card with two numbers lit and the total and parts slots empty
**Does:** drags each number from the story into the slot for the job it does, then reads the share bar
**Win:** the bar splits into equal parts and one part's size lands in the answer builder
**Feels:** the story decides which number is the whole, and the words that decide it can be pointed to
**Goal:** the total being shared is the dividend and the number of equal parts is the divisor
**Observe:** A number dropped in the parts slot sprang back while the story's words for the parts lit up.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- divides the petrol by the distance, which asks how much petrol one kilometre takes, a different question from the one set. → the child's twenty-four springs back out of the total slot while the words for the whole trip are lit, and the child's order is drawn as a second bar beside the story's: the petrol cut into four hundred and fifty-six parts, each far less than a litre, beside the distance cut into twenty-four equal parts of nineteen kilometres. Both bars stay drawn, nothing marked.
- finds nineteen and writes it as litres, when the question asked how far the van goes. → the answer builder's row carries the unit the question asks for, kilometres, written after the box, and the child's litres is laid beside it. The share bar is then read part by part, each part a stretch of road nineteen kilometres long with the one litre it used drawn under it.
**Misconception to break:** Trying to divide 24 by 456, which asks how much petrol one kilometre takes, a different question.

### 2 · Finding a missing factor
**Screen:** the missing factor bay holding a product and one of its two numbers
**Does:** divides the product by the known number, then multiplies back to check
**Win:** the missing number settles and the two numbers multiply back to the product
**Feels:** a product and one of its numbers are a division waiting to be done, and multiplying back says whether it was done right
**Goal:** a missing factor is the product divided by the factor that is known
**Observe:** The missing number was found by dividing, and the two numbers multiplied back to the product.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- multiplies the two numbers given, as if the product were one of the factors. → the bay writes the child's number, ninety-four thousand two hundred and eight, in the empty box and multiplies it back by thirty-two, which lands nowhere near two thousand nine hundred and forty-four. The bay then divides the product by the number that is known, writes ninety-two, and multiplies the two back to the product exactly, with the child's number still standing above.
- writes ninety-one after a slip at the last step, and never multiplies back to see it. → the bay multiplies thirty-two by ninety-one and writes two thousand nine hundred and twelve under the product, exactly one thirty-two short. The child's ninety-one stays in its box, and the last step is replayed in the frame, sixty-four ones holding two thirty-twos.
**Misconception to break:** Multiplying the two numbers given, as if the product were one of the factors.

### 3 · The zero the working hides
**Screen:** a frame where the next number to divide will be smaller than the divisor, with two digits waiting
**Does:** brings down one digit only, sees it is smaller than the divisor, writes the zero, then brings the next
**Win:** the zero lands in its place and the answer reads with every digit it needs
**Feels:** the step that gives nothing is still a step, and it has to be written down or the answer loses a place
**Goal:** a zero goes in the quotient when the number brought down is smaller than the divisor, and one digit at a time shows it
**Observe:** Bringing down one digit at a time showed a step that bringing two together had hidden.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- brings two digits down together and loses the zero, so two hundred and seven is read as twenty-seven. → the frame sends the second digit back to its place and brings down the six alone: sixteen tens, laid against twenty-four, falls short, because sixteen is smaller than twenty-four, and the grey zero drops into the tens place. The eight then comes down. The check multiplies twenty-four by the child's twenty-seven, six hundred and forty-eight, far short of the dividend, while two hundred and seven rebuilds it exactly.
- writes the zero for sixteen tens and then divides the eight on its own, as if the sixteen tens had gone. → what is left under the zero step, sixteen tens, stays lit in the frame, still there, and the bring down arrow lays the eight beside it, so the working number reads one hundred and sixty-eight ones. The child's eight ones stay written above it, and seven twenty-fours are laid against the larger number and fit it exactly.
**Misconception to break:** Bringing two digits down at once and losing the zero, so 207 is read as 27.

### 4 · When the leftover is part of the answer
**Screen:** a story that asks two things, with two slots open in the answer builder
**Does:** works the division, puts the quotient in one slot and the remainder in the other, each with its unit
**Win:** both slots fill with their units and the answer closes
**Feels:** the leftover is not waste this time, it is the answer to half the question
**Goal:** when a question asks what is left, the remainder is part of the answer
**Observe:** The answer would not close while the row for what was left stood empty.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- gives only three hundred and thirteen and drops the ten millilitres, which answers the second question. → the answer builder stays open with its second row lit and empty, its unit, millilitres, already written after it, and the running count reads one ask answered of two. The remainder is lifted from the foot of the frame, where it has stood the whole time, and carried across to that row, with the child's first row left exactly as it was filled.
- gives the remainder the unit of the parts, ten bowls left over instead of ten millilitres. → the unit strip lights millilitres against the row, and the share bar is read again: fifteen bowls each holding three hundred and thirteen millilitres, and ten millilitres of soup drawn apart from them in a dish of its own, soup and not bowls. The child's row keeps its number and its unit until the child changes it.
**Misconception to break:** Giving only 313 and dropping the 10 mL, which is the answer to the second question.

## Delight
The third phase, when one digit comes down at a time. The seven tens drop into the working number alone, twenty-five is laid against them and they fall short, and a grey zero drops into the tens place of the quotient at full size. Then the five comes down to join the seven, making seventy-five ones, and the last digit settles. Beside it a copy of the frame brings the two digits down together at the same moment, ends one digit short, and reads twenty-three against two hundred and three, both left standing. It runs as often as a finger likes, nothing is marked, and the voice says nothing at all while it happens.

**General miss line:** Read the story again, and point to the words that say how many parts.

## Answer key
In a word problem, the total being shared is the dividend and the number of equal parts is the divisor. The answer to how many in each is the quotient. When two numbers multiply to make a product and one of them is known, the other is the product divided by the one that is known. Bring down one digit at a time, because a zero goes in the quotient whenever the number being divided is smaller than the divisor, and bringing two digits down together hides that step. When a question asks how many are left, the remainder is part of the answer and must be given. Answers carry the unit the question uses. Where a question shares a time or an amount out and asks for each part, each part is the same time or the same amount, and that is all an average means here.

## Student controls
- **Story card**: shows the story with its numbers lit, and lights the wording for each number's job
- **Total slot**: takes the number being shared
- **Parts slot**: takes the number of equal parts
- **Share bar**: draws the total as a bar split into that many equal parts
- **Missing factor bay**: holds a product and one of its numbers and divides to find the other
- **One digit at a time**: brings digits down singly and refuses two brought down together
- **Answer builder**: lists each ask of the question with its own slot and its own unit

## Textbook (this topic)
**Pages:** 59
**Key terms:** dividend, divisor, product, remainder
A word problem is solved by deciding which number is shared and into how many parts, and by using the remainder whenever the question asks about what is left. One hundred and eighty, the total being shared. Thirty is the number of equal parts. So each row has one hundred and eighty divided by thirty, six seats. Divide the product by the number you know. Five thousand and seventy-five divided by twenty-five is two hundred and three. None, so a zero goes in the tens place. Without it, the answer would wrongly read twenty-three. The quotient says how many are in each group. The remainder says how many are left out. Both are answers, so neither can be dropped.
