## What it is
**Description:** Try the divisor's multiples until one is too big, say what is left at every step, and take the dividend apart in chunks you can work out.
**Why:** Divide by a two-digit number by trying its multiples, and then by taking away chunks you can work out, and find out why every choice of chunks ends on the same answer.
**Look:** A ladder of multiples beside a long division frame on the left, and a bar with a column of chunk cards on the right.
**Context:** Grade 4, the hardest topic in the chapter. An adult sees a two-digit divisor, which looks like only a harder long division. What is actually new is that the times table for the divisor is not known by heart, so each step is found by trying multiples until one is too big, and a step that is not stated clearly loses the leftover that makes the next number. The worked example for this topic states its remainder at the first and last steps and skips it at the middle one, where nine tens are left and become the ninety-four ones divided next. The partial quotient method then lets a child use chunks they can work out, and the chunks always add up to the same quotient.
**For the child:** Write the divisor's multiples until one is too big. Take the one before it. Say what is left every time. Or take away chunks you can work out. Add the chunks to get the quotient.
**Teaching notes:** Pack beans into cups before the tool is opened, a chunk at a time, and let different groups choose different chunks. Every group ends with the same number in each cup, and that surprise is the lesson of the second half of this topic. For the first half, the divisor's times table is not known by heart, so write its multiples on the board, times one, times two and upwards, and stop at the first one that is too big. The worked example for this topic does exactly that, and it states what is left at its first step and at its last, but not at the middle one. Eighteen goes into one hundred and seventeen six times, and nine tens are left. Those nine tens and the four ones make the ninety-four ones divided next, and a child who never hears the nine tens does not know where ninety-four came from. Make stating the leftover a habit at every step; the tool will not bring a digit down until it has been written. The chapter's self-assessment asks a child to estimate a quotient, which the chapter never teaches. Trying multiples until one is too big is trial and check, and it is taught here as that, not as estimating, which is taught nowhere in these pages. The two Critical Thinking questions for this topic have divisors of three hundred and of a hundred, bigger than any divisor the chapter teaches; the missing number bay will build them, but say so. The closing skills page seats a cricket ground in six blocks, three divisions to a block, all of them exact. Eleven of its eighteen divisions are by ten or by a number with two digits, so it belongs here, and the other seven are by one digit, which the long division topic already covers. The page never says that a block's people are the sum of its three quotients, though that is the only reading that answers it; say so before the class starts adding, and the six blocks then hold six thousand seven hundred and sixty-nine people. The lab that closes the chapter uses digit cards, with a card drawn from a pack as the divisor and a jack counting as eleven.

## Phases (what each mount shows and does; the words are in tool.tsx)

### 1 · Try multiples until one is too big
**Screen:** a two-digit divisor at the foot of an empty ladder, with the number to divide in the working window
**Does:** climbs the ladder a rung at a time until one rung is bigger than the number
**Win:** the rung that passes the number lights as too big and the rung below it gives the quotient digit
**Feels:** the rung that is too big is not a mistake, it is the rung that proves the one below it is the largest that fits
**Goal:** write multiples of the divisor until one passes the number, and take the one before it
**Observe:** The climb stopped at the first rung that passed the number, and the rung under it gave the digit.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- stops at sixteen times three, forty-eight, because it looks near enough to seventy-five, and writes three. → the leftover under the child's rung, twenty-seven, is offered to the left slot and springs back, because twenty-seven is bigger than sixteen. The climb then carries on from that rung: sixty-four fits, and eighty passes seventy-five and lights as too big. The child's three stays written beside the four the ladder settles on.
- takes the rung that is too big, because it is the last one written. → the too-big rung is dragged toward the frame and will not land: eighty is laid against seventy-five as two bars side by side, and the longer runs past the end of the shorter. The child's five stays in the quotient cell, and the rung below it lights as the last one that fits, with nothing marked.
**Misconception to break:** Stopping at the first multiple that looks close, without checking that the next one really is too big.

### 2 · Say what is left at every step
**Screen:** a division part way through, with the left slot empty at a middle step
**Does:** works out what is left at that step and writes it before bringing the next digit down
**Win:** the leftover slides down beside the next digit and makes the number the next step divides
**Feels:** the leftover is the piece of the next number that was already there before the digit came down
**Goal:** what is left at each step joins the next digit to make the next number
**Observe:** The left slot had to be filled before the next digit could come down.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- skips the leftover at a middle step, then does not know where the next number came from. → the bring down arrow stays unlit and the left slot at the middle step is lit empty, with the rung taken and the working number above it. The subtraction is run in the frame, one hundred and seventeen take away one hundred and eight, and the nine tens drop into the slot and slide down beside the four, so the ninety-four the next step divides is seen being made.
- takes fourteen once out of thirty-four tens and tries to carry twenty tens on. → the left slot springs the twenty back, because twenty is bigger than the divisor, and lights the rung for two fourteens, twenty-eight, which still fits under thirty-four. The child's one stays written beside the quotient cell, and the step runs again with six tens dropping into the slot.
**Misconception to break:** Skipping the leftover at a middle step, then not knowing where the next number came from.

### 3 · Taking away chunks you know
**Screen:** a bar for the dividend with the chunk cards beside it and an empty chunk column
**Does:** takes away chunks one after another, writing each chunk size, until too little is left to go round
**Win:** the bar shrinks below the divisor and the chunk column adds up to the quotient
**Feels:** any handful you can work out will do, and the handfuls you write down are the answer
**Goal:** chunks of the divisor that are easy to work out are taken away until less than the divisor is left
**Observe:** The chunk column added up to the quotient, and the amounts taken away added up to the dividend.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- adds up the amounts taken away, six hundred, three hundred and sixty and forty-eight, instead of the chunk sizes. → the child's sum is written under the column, one thousand and eight, and it matches the dividend exactly: the amounts taken away rebuild all the eggs, not one shop's share. The chunk sizes are then lit one at a time, fifty, thirty and four, and added under their own rule to eighty-four. Both sums stay drawn, with what each one counts written beside it.
- stops after the fifty and the thirty and writes eighty, with forty-eight eggs still left for twelve shops. → the bar refuses to close while forty-eight stands on it, because forty-eight is bigger than twelve, and every shop's cup lights as able to take more. The typed chunk card opens beside the bar, and the child's eighty stays in the column's total line until the last chunk is taken.
**Misconception to break:** Adding up the amounts taken away, 600, 360 and 48, instead of the chunk sizes 50, 30 and 4.

### 4 · Different chunks, the same quotient
**Screen:** the same dividend loaded into both lanes, with the first lane already finished
**Does:** takes the second bar apart with different chunks and adds its column
**Win:** the two chunk columns end on one number and the two quotients stand level
**Feels:** the lists of chunks disagree and the share in every part does not, because the share was never in any one chunk
**Goal:** different chunks add up to the same quotient
**Observe:** Two lanes with different chunks ended on the same quotient.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- thinks a different set of chunks must give a different answer, or that only the set in the worked example is allowed. → the second lane runs the child's own chunks beside the first lane's, the two columns plainly different, and both are added on the same tick and land on eighty-four. One shop's eggs from each lane are then drawn side by side, the same count, with both columns left standing.
- takes twenty more for each shop after the seventy, two hundred and forty, when only one hundred and sixty-eight are left. → the card is dragged to the bar and will not land: two hundred and forty is laid against the hundred and sixty-eight still standing, and runs past its end. The card goes back to the stack, the child's twenty stays written at the foot of the column with nothing marked, and ten for each shop is offered beside it, a hundred and twenty, which fits.
**Misconception to break:** Thinking a different set of chunks must give a different answer, or that only one set is allowed.

## Delight
The fourth phase, the moment the second lane's column is added. The two columns stand side by side with different chunk sizes in them, and their sums are written under their rules on the same tick. They land on one number, and the two quotients slide together into a single line across the panel while both emptied bars light at once, each holding the same small leftover. A third set of chunks can be run as often as a finger likes, and it lands on the same number every time. Nothing is marked, and the voice says nothing at all while it happens.

**General miss line:** Go back to the last rung that fitted, and say what is left under it.

## Answer key
To divide by a two-digit number, write its multiples, times one, times two and on, until one is bigger than the number being divided; the one before it gives the quotient digit. Say what is left at every step, because what is left joins the next digit to make the next number to divide. In the partial quotient method, take away any multiples of the divisor you can work out, such as ten or fifty times it, until what is left is zero or smaller than the divisor. The chunk sizes add up to the quotient and what is left is the remainder. Different chunks give the same quotient. A missing dividend is divisor times quotient, add the remainder, and a missing divisor is the dividend take away the remainder, divided by the quotient.

## Student controls
- **Ladder of multiples**: writes the divisor times one, times two and upwards, and lights the first rung that is too big
- **Left slot**: takes what is left at every step and refuses a number as big as the divisor
- **Bring down**: brings the next digit down beside what is left once the slot is filled
- **Chunk cards**: take away ten, twenty, fifty or a hundred times the divisor, or a typed chunk
- **Chunk column**: writes each chunk size taken away and adds them up at the end
- **Second lane**: runs the same dividend with different chunks beside the first
- **Missing number bay**: builds the missing one of dividend, divisor, quotient and remainder from the other three
