## What it is
**Description:** Sort the months by their days, lay a year out in weeks, and run any year through the whole leap year rule. Count days with both ends decided first, and write short dates.
**Why:** Know the days in each month and in a year, and decide whether a year is a leap year. Count the days between two dates with each end decided first, and write a date in short form.
**Look:** A wall of twelve month cards under a year dial, a rack of month bars and a weeks ruler, a day counter with two end switches, and a date writer.
**Context:** Grade 4, and the recap topic, where the chapter moves from the clock to the calendar. An adult sees a calendar being read, which every classroom wall allows. What is actually being settled is three rules the page states badly or not at all. A year is fifty-two weeks and one day, three hundred and sixty-five days in all. A year divisible by four is a leap year, except a century year not divisible by four hundred, so 1900 was not a leap year and 2000 was. And a count of the days between two dates has no answer until it is decided whether the first day and the last day count. The page changes that from one example to the next without saying so.
**For the child:** Sort the months by their days. Add the year and lay out the weeks. Run the year through the leap year gate. Before counting days, decide both ends. Write the day, then the month, then the year.
**Teaching notes:** Use a real calendar for this year before the tool is opened. Have the class find the third Saturday of a month and count the days to a class event, saying first whether today and the event day count. The opening page asks for the date of the third Saturday in July this year and names no year. On the chapter's own printed calendar, for 2026, July begins on a Wednesday and its Saturdays fall on the fourth, eleventh, eighteenth and twenty-fifth. So the answer for 2026 is the eighteenth of July; say which year you are using. The page says a year is twelve months or fifty-two weeks; say fifty-two weeks and one day. Its leap year rules are a flat rule and an exception that breaks it, and they never say that a century year divisible by four hundred is a leap year. State the rule whole every time: a year divisible by four is a leap year, except a century year not divisible by four hundred. The worked day counts change which ends they count from one example to the next, so ask about both ends before every count. The exercise and the On Your Own box need holiday dates the chapter never gives. Republic Day is 26 January, Independence Day 15 August, Teacher's Day 5 September, Children's Day 14 November and Christmas Day 25 December. One exercise does not say whether the day of leaving is one of the fifty-four days of a visit. It is 2 July if it is, and 3 July, the book's answer, if it is not. Accept any day-first short form of a date, with dots, slashes or dashes. The closing page asks for finishing dates and daily durations. Its four find-outs about the pool, the races and a venue need facts no page gives, so this plan does not answer them. The six self-assessment statements say nothing about counting days or writing dates, so add both when the class ticks the list, and make flashcards for the eleven vocabulary words.

## Phases (what each mount shows and does; the words are in tool.tsx)

### 1 · Days in the months and the year
**Screen:** the wall of twelve month cards for 2026 and the empty month rack
**Does:** sorts the months onto their bars, adds the year on the year bar, lays the weeks ruler along it, then finds a chosen weekday in a month
**Win:** the year bar reads three hundred and sixty-five, and the weeks ruler shows full rows of seven with one day over
**Feels:** the months do not share one length, and the year does not fill its weeks exactly
**Goal:** four months have thirty days, seven have thirty-one and February twenty-eight, three hundred and sixty-five days in all, which is fifty-two weeks and one day
**Observe:** The year bar read three hundred and sixty-five, and the weeks ruler filled fifty-two rows of seven with one day over.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- calls a year of three hundred and sixty-five days exactly fifty-two weeks, one day short of its length. → the weeks ruler lays the year bar's three hundred and sixty-five days out in rows of seven, and fifty-two full rows fill, three hundred and sixty-four days, with one yellow day standing alone after them. The child's answer stays in the slot beside the ruler, nothing marked.
- gives August thirty days, taking the months to go thirty-one, thirty, thirty-one, thirty all through the year. → August's card slides onto the thirty-one bar, and its dates on the wall run to the thirty-first. July and August stand side by side on that bar, two months in a row with thirty-one days each, and the child's thirty stays in the slot.
**Misconception to break:** Saying a year is exactly fifty-two weeks, which would be only 364 days.

### 2 · Leap years
**Screen:** the year dial and the leap year gate, with February on the wall showing twenty-eight days
**Does:** dials a year, runs it through the three questions of the gate in order, and watches February
**Win:** February grows a twenty-ninth day for each leap year, the year bar reads three hundred and sixty-six, and a century year that fails the last question stays at twenty-eight
**Feels:** four gives the rule, a century year breaks it, and four hundred mends it again
**Goal:** a year divisible by four is a leap year, except a century year not divisible by four hundred; a leap year has twenty-nine days in February
**Observe:** February grew a twenty-ninth day for the years that passed the gate, and a century year that failed the last question kept twenty-eight.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- calls 1700 a leap year because it is divisible by four, stopping at the first question. → the gate takes 1700 through all three questions: yes at divisible by four, yes at century year, and no at divisible by four hundred, and the no path lights. February on the wall stays at twenty-eight days and the year bar at three hundred and sixty-five, and the child's answer stays in the slot.
- calls 1600 not a leap year because it is a century year, as if every century year were left out. → the gate takes 1600 on past the century question to the last one, divisible by four hundred, where it answers yes, and the yes path lights. February on the wall grows a twenty-ninth day in yellow, the year bar reads three hundred and sixty-six, and the child's answer stays in the slot.
**Misconception to break:** Calling 1700 a leap year because it is divisible by four, and leaving out the century rule.

### 3 · Counting days between dates
**Screen:** two dates flagged on the wall, with both end switches unset and the day counter waiting
**Does:** sets whether the first day counts and whether the last day counts, then counts the days left in the first month, any whole months, and the days in the last month
**Win:** the counted days light on the wall, the parts are written month by month, and they add to the total
**Feels:** each end is a decision, and the count moves by one each time an end is turned in or out
**Goal:** before counting the days between two dates, decide whether the first day and the last day count, then count month by month
**Observe:** The counter would not light until both switches were set, and the answer changed by one when a switch was turned.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- takes eighteen from thirty for September and adds seven for October, which counts 7 October, and answers nineteen days between 18 September and 7 October, not counting either date. → the counter stays dark until both end switches are set to leave the ends out. It then fills in green the twelve days from 19 to 30 September and the six days from 1 to 6 October, and 7 October stands outlined only, not counted. The parts are written twelve and six, eighteen in all, and the child's nineteen stays in the slot beside them.
- takes twelve from thirty for November, which leaves out 12 November, and answers twenty-one days from 12 November to 3 December, counting both dates. → the first end switch reads counted, and 12 November fills in green on the wall with the eighteen days after it, nineteen in November. The three days of December fill after them, and the parts are written nineteen and three, twenty-two in all, beside the child's twenty-one in the slot.
- adds sixteen to 22 March and writes 7 April as the last day of a sixteen-day tournament that counts 22 March as its first day. → the first end switch reads counted, 22 March fills in green as day one, and the days fill on, ten in March and six in April, the sixteenth landing on 6 April. 7 April stands outlined only, the day after, and the child's answer stays in the slot.
**Misconception to break:** Taking 18 from 30 and then adding 7, which counts 7 October when it should be left out, and answering 19.

### 4 · Writing a date in short form
**Screen:** a date written in words, and the date writer's three empty slots
**Does:** fills the day and the month with two digits each and the year with four, then switches between dots, slashes and dashes
**Win:** the date settles in short form, and the same date shows with dots, with slashes and with dashes
**Feels:** the short form keeps one order, day, then month, then year, and fills every place
**Goal:** a date in short form is the day, the month and the year in figures, day first, with two digits for the day and the month and four for the year
**Observe:** The date settled day first, with two digits for the day and the month, and the switch showed it with dots, slashes and dashes.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- writes the month first, 01.03.2027 for 3 January 2027, which reads as 1 March. → the date writer's slots are labelled day, month and year in order, and the child's digits are read back through them: day one, month three, 1 March 2027, and that date lights on the wall two months on from 3 January. The digits are then set day first, 03.01.2027, and the child's answer stays in the slot.
- drops the noughts and writes 3.1.2027 for 3 January 2027. → the day and month slots of the date writer each show both places drawn, and their empty first places light until a nought fills each. The same date then shows with dots, slashes and dashes, 03.01.2027, 03/01/2027 and 03-01-2027, and the child's answer stays in the slot beside it.
**Misconception to break:** Writing the month first, 01.03.2027, which reads as 1 March, or dropping the noughts and writing 3.1.2027.

## Delight
The first phase, when the year dial is turned on by one. The day left over on the weeks ruler lifts off in yellow and slides across the wall, and each month card's dates move one column to the right, so the first of January moves from a Thursday in 2026 to a Friday in 2027. Turned on from 2028, a leap year, two days lift off, and the first of January moves two columns, from a Saturday in 2028 to a Monday in 2029. It can be turned as often as a finger likes, nothing is marked, and the voice says nothing at all while it happens.

## Answer key
April, June, September and November have thirty days; January, March, May, July, August, October and December have thirty-one; February has twenty-eight, or twenty-nine in a leap year. A year that is not a leap year has three hundred and sixty-five days, which is fifty-two weeks and one day, and a leap year has three hundred and sixty-six. The earth takes about three hundred and sixty-five and a quarter days to go round the sun. A year divisible by four is a leap year, except a century year not divisible by four hundred: 1900 was not a leap year, and 2000 was. Before counting the days between two dates, decide whether each end counts, then count month by month. A date in short form is the day, the month and the year in figures, day first, as in 03.01.2027, and dots, slashes or dashes may separate them.
