## What it is
**Description:** Pour loose parts into whole ones until no more will fill, and read the two ways of writing what you hold.
**Why:** Gather loose parts into whole ones, and find the two different ways of writing what you are holding.
**Look:** A tray of empty whole circles on the left, a heap of equal loose parts on the right, and both readings written under them at once.
**Context:** Grade 4, the topic that reaches past one whole. An adult sees two notations for the same quantity. What is being settled is that they are different kinds of thing: one is a single fraction whose upper number is not the smaller one, and the other is a whole number standing beside a proper fraction. The printed page runs the two definitions together in a single sentence, and a child who takes that sentence at face value has no way to read a fraction whose two numbers are equal, which is improper and is exactly one whole.
**For the child:** Set how many parts make one whole. Pour the loose parts in and fill each whole before the next. Read the tray both ways as you go. The number below the line never changes. To go back, break every whole one into parts and count them all.
**Teaching notes:** Gather real paper quarters into whole plates first and count them aloud both ways, nine quarters and two plates and a quarter, before anything is written. Then open the tool and keep both readings on from the first pour, because seeing them tick along together is what stops the pair being learnt as two unrelated procedures. Spend a minute on a fraction whose two numbers are equal. Pour it, let the wholes fill exactly, and say out loud that this is an improper fraction with no whole number and no part beside it, only one whole. The printed definition cannot describe it, so the class needs to have watched it happen. Pin the lower number across the top and keep pointing at it: it is the same before and after every conversion, in both directions, and it is the one handhold a child has while the other two numbers move. When the division is written out, make the quotient and the remainder point at the tray, the quotient at the full wholes and the remainder at the loose parts, rather than standing as numbers on their own. Two printed items arrive with fraction parts that are not in lowest terms. Work them as they are printed, then reduce, and say plainly that a mixed number is usually written with its fraction part reduced and that both writings mean the same amount.

## Phases (what each mount shows and does; the words are in tool.tsx)

### 1 · When the top is not the smaller one
**Screen:** the sorter holding several written fractions with the tray empty behind it
**Does:** compares the two numbers of each fraction and pours the improper ones
**Win:** the improper cards fill at least one whole, and the one whose numbers match fills exactly with nothing over
**Feels:** a card with two matching numbers fills its circle exactly and has nothing left over to stand beside it
**Goal:** an improper fraction is one whose upper number is not the smaller one
**Observe:** A card whose two numbers were equal poured until the wholes were full and left nothing beside them.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- leaves 7/7 out of the improper cards, because it does not look like more than one whole. → the test is printed above the sorter in the words it is written in, numerator greater than or equal to denominator, with greater and equal lit one after the other as two separate ways of passing it. The card is then poured: seven parts go into a circle ruled into seven, it fills right round, the heap is empty and nothing stands beside the full circle. The tray says in words that this one is improper and is one whole, and the child's sorting is left exactly as it was.
- calls 5/12 improper because twelve is the bigger of its two numbers. → the sorter lights the two numbers in their places rather than by size, the upper one above the line and the lower one below it, and reads the test out with each one named as it is reached. The card is poured anyway: five parts go into a circle ruled into twelve and stop well short of filling it, with seven parts of that circle left amber and the green bracket spanning the whole drawn above them. The child's card stays where it was put.
**Misconception to break:** Leaving 7/7 out because it does not look like more than one whole.

### 2 · A whole number with a part beside it
**Screen:** two full wholes on the tray with some loose parts beside them
**Does:** writes the whole count and the leftover parts into the two places of a mixed number
**Win:** the mixed number is written with the whole part and the proper fraction in their places
**Feels:** two numbers written side by side, and each of them counts a different thing on the tray
**Goal:** a mixed number is a whole number standing beside a proper fraction
**Observe:** The quotient always pointed at the filled circles and the remainder always pointed at the loose parts.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- treats 8/8 as a mixed number and tries to write a whole one with a fraction part standing beside it. → the card is poured and the circle fills right round, with the heap empty and the loose row beside the tray drawn and bare. The mixed number's second place is then read out for what it holds, a proper fraction, and there is no piece anywhere on the tray to put in it; the tray prints that a mixed number is a whole number with a proper fraction beside it and that this card has a whole number and nothing beside it. The single fraction reading stays up throughout and the child's writing is left standing.
- writes the loose parts as the whole part and the full plates as the fraction part, swapping the two places over. → the tray reads itself back with a leader drawn from each place of the mixed number to the thing it counts, the first place to the full circles and the second place to the loose pieces, and the part size pinned above is lit as the second place's lower number. The child's writing stays exactly as it was and the tray's reading is written underneath it, so two whole ones and three fifths and the swapped version stand one above the other and are poured out in turn.
**Misconception to break:** Calling it improper because the whole amount is more than one.

### 3 · Dividing to find the whole part
**Screen:** a heap of loose parts with the division written empty under the tray
**Does:** pours, then fills in the quotient and the remainder from what the tray shows
**Win:** the quotient points at the filled wholes, the remainder points at the loose parts, and the lower number has not moved
**Feels:** the division is the pouring written down, and each of its two answers is pointing at something on the tray
**Goal:** dividing the upper number by the lower one gives the whole part and the remainder
**Observe:** The number below the line was the same before and after, in both directions, every time.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- writes the remainder as the denominator and the divisor as the numerator, turning 23/6 into three whole ones and six fifths. → the two leaders are drawn from the division to the tray and stay drawn: the quotient to the three full circles and the remainder to the five pieces still loose. The fraction part the child wrote is then built on the tray as it stands, six pieces where a whole one takes five, and it fills a circle and leaves one over, so a fraction part that is not a proper fraction is standing beside a whole number. The pinned part size is lit at the top throughout and the child's writing is left where it was.
- converts correctly and leaves the fraction part as it comes out, writing fifty-two sixths as eight whole ones and four sixths. → nothing is marked, because nothing here is wrong: the tray pours the heap and eight circles fill with four pieces over, exactly as the child wrote. The four loose pieces are then laid onto one more empty circle ruled into six, where they cover four of its parts, and the same four are laid onto a circle ruled into three, where they cover two of its parts exactly. Both writings are left standing side by side with a rose tie between them, because the second is a second writing of the first and not a correction of it.
**Misconception to break:** Writing the remainder as the whole part and the quotient as the fraction part.

### 4 · Going back the other way
**Screen:** a mixed number on the tray with the build it back control waiting
**Does:** breaks the whole ones into parts, adds the loose ones and writes the single fraction
**Win:** the single fraction is written over the same lower number and the two readings agree
**Feels:** every whole one breaks into exactly the number of pieces pinned at the top, and the counting does the rest
**Goal:** a mixed number becomes one fraction by multiplying and adding
**Observe:** One conversion left a fraction part that would still divide, and the tray accepted it.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- adds the whole number to the upper number of the fraction part, writing 4 3/7 as seven sevenths. → the seven over seven the child wrote is poured onto the tray and fills one circle exactly, with the heap empty and eleven of the twelve circles on the tray untouched. Beside it the four whole ones are broken along their own lines and counted into the heap, seven, fourteen, twenty-one, twenty-eight, and the three loose pieces are counted in after them to thirty-one. Both writings stay on the stage and neither is crossed through.
- multiplies the whole number by the upper number of the fraction part instead of by the lower one, reaching fifteen sevenths. → the breaking is run at walking pace with each circle counted out as it opens, and what comes out of one circle is the pinned number of parts rather than the numerator of anything; the pinned seven is lit at the top of the stage each time a circle breaks. The child's fifteen over seven is poured on a second tray and fills two circles with one over, while the real heap fills four with three over, and both trays are left standing.
**Misconception to break:** Adding the whole number to the upper number and writing seven over seven as the answer.

## Delight
The fourth phase, the moment build it back is released on four whole ones and three loose sevenths. The whole circles do not vanish and reappear as a number. Each of them cracks along the ruled lines it has carried since it was drawn, seven pieces at a time, and the pieces fan out and slide across into the loose heap, where they are counted in one by one with the running count climbing seven, fourteen, twenty-one, twenty-eight. The three that were already loose join at the end, twenty-nine, thirty, thirty-one, and the single fraction writes itself as thirty-one over seven with the seven arriving from the pinned number at the top of the stage rather than from anywhere in the working. Press it again and every part flies home into a circle and closes up. Nothing else on the tray moves and the voice says nothing at all while it happens.

**General miss line:** Pour it again slowly and watch both readings climb together underneath the tray.

## Answer key
A fraction is improper when its upper number is greater than or equal to its lower number. That is a test on the two numbers and nothing else, so a fraction whose two numbers are equal is improper and is exactly one whole. A mixed number is a different kind of thing: a whole number written beside a proper fraction. To turn an improper fraction into a whole number or a mixed number, divide the upper number by the lower one; the quotient is the whole part, the remainder is the new upper number, and the lower number stays exactly as it was. When the division leaves no remainder the answer is a whole number and there is no fraction part at all. To go the other way, multiply the whole number by the lower number, add the upper number of the fraction part, and write the total over the same lower number. The lower number never changes in either direction.

## Student controls
- **Part size**: names how many parts make one whole and pins that number across the top
- **Pour**: drops loose parts into the whole ones in order, filling each before the next
- **Both readings**: keeps the single fraction and the mixed number written side by side
- **Sorter**: lights which of the two numbers of a written fraction is the larger
- **Exactly full**: says in words when the wholes have filled with nothing left over
- **Build it back**: breaks a mixed number into parts and writes the single fraction
- **Show the division**: writes the division under the tray with its quotient and remainder labelled

## Textbook (this topic)
**Pages:** 78, 79, 80
**Key terms:** improper fraction, mixed number, quotient, remainder
Improper fractions and mixed numbers are defined apart and converted each way by division and multiplication. A fraction whose numerator is not smaller than its denominator is an improper fraction. The same amount can be written as a mixed number, a whole number with a proper fraction beside it. Divide to go one way, multiply and add to go back, and the denominator never changes. In both, the numerator is not smaller than the denominator, so both are improper fractions. Seven thirds is more than one whole. Eight eighths is exactly one whole. No, it is a mixed number, which is a whole number and a proper fraction written side by side. An improper fraction is a single fraction with a large top. They are two ways of writing one amount. Divide nine by four. It goes twice with one left over, so there are two wholes and one quarter more. The quotient becomes the whole number and the remainder becomes the new numerator. Multiply the two by the six to get twelve, add the one to get thirteen, and write it over six. The denominator never changes, whichever direction you are converting in.
