## What it is
**Description:** Build other names for one amount by moving both numbers together, and test any pair by crossing them.
**Why:** Find out that one amount can be written many ways, build those writings yourself, and learn a test that works when there is no picture to look at.
**Look:** Two strips of the same length laid one under the other from a common left edge, a builder above them and a cross bench along the foot.
**Context:** Grade 4, the topic that gives one amount several names. An adult sees the reducing move run backwards, which it is. What is being taught is that a pair of fractions can look nothing alike and be the same amount, and that there is a test for it that needs no picture at all. A child who has only the picture cannot check a pair of large fractions, and a child who has only the cross products cannot say why the test works.
**For the child:** Lay both strips from the same left edge. Move the upper and lower numbers together, never one alone. Watch where the colour stops, not how many cuts there are. If a number is given to you, find what it was multiplied by first. On the cross bench, write both products before you decide.
**Teaching notes:** Fold and cut real strips first, all of one length, and lay them one under another from a single left edge against a ruler. That common edge is the whole argument: two strips that do not start together prove nothing, and a class that has laid them carelessly once will remember why. Then use the tool for what strips cannot do at speed. Set the builder to double and have the class watch the cuts multiply while the colour stops in the same place, and ask what would happen to the colour if only the top number were doubled. Teach the target bay as a question rather than a procedure: what was this number multiplied by, and what does that leave the other one to do? A child who cannot answer the first question is guessing at the second. Save the cross bench for last and open it with the strips hidden, because its whole value is that it works when no picture is available. Write both products every time, in the same order every time, and read the verdict off them rather than off the fractions. Then put the strips back and check one pair the slow way, so the test is seen to agree with the pictures rather than replace them.
**Placement:** Explore

## Phases (what each mount shows and does; the words are in tool.tsx)

### 1 · Same amount, different writing
**Screen:** two equal strips, one showing a half and one showing two quarters
**Does:** fills both strips from their own fractions and reads where the filled parts end
**Win:** the filled ends line up exactly and the guide line runs through both
**Feels:** three glasses filled three different ways, and the level is the same on all three
**Goal:** two fractions that look different can be the same amount
**Observe:** One strip carried three cuts and the other carried nine, and the colour stopped in the same place on both.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- picks the fraction with the largest numbers, because nine parts with three taken sounds like more than one part out of three. → the three strips are laid from the common edge with their cuts drawn and the guide line dropped through all three fills, and the colour stops in one place on every strip. The cuts are then counted out along each, three and six and nine, and the parts taken counted under them, one and two and three, with the child's choice still lit where it was made and nothing cleared.
- lays the strips from wherever each happens to fall, or compares strips that were never the same length. → the bench slides every strip back to the common left edge one at a time, with the edge drawn through all of them, and redraws any strip that was not the full length at the full length with its cuts rescaled. The child's arrangement is drawn once more above the corrected one so the two can be read against each other, and the two verdicts, the one the loose arrangement gave and the one the common edge gives, are left standing together.
**Misconception to break:** Picking the one with the largest numbers because nine parts sounds like more.

### 2 · Building one by multiplying
**Screen:** a filled top strip with an empty lower strip and the builder set to two
**Does:** multiplies both numbers, watches the cuts double and the filled length hold
**Win:** the lower strip fills to the same end with twice as many cuts in it
**Feels:** the cuts double while a hand watches and the colour stops in the very same place
**Goal:** multiplying both numbers by the same number keeps the amount
**Observe:** The row of equivalents went on as long as the multiples did, and never ran out.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- multiplies the upper number and leaves the lower number as it is, turning 2/7 into 4/7. → the lower strip is redrawn as the child built it, still in sevenths and now with four of them filled, and its colour runs plainly past the guide line dropped from the strip above. Both strips stay on the bench with their fractions written under them, and the builder then runs the same doubling on both numbers on a third strip, which cuts into fourteen and stops exactly on the line.
- multiplies the upper number by two and the lower number by three, because both numbers were multiplied. → the bench draws what the child built, four over twenty-one, cut into twenty-one with four filled, and its colour falls short of the guide line. Beside it the same starting fraction is written in twenty-firsts by multiplying both numbers by three, giving six over twenty-one, and that one lands on the line with two more parts filled than the child's. Both strips stay drawn and neither is marked.
**Misconception to break:** Multiplying only the upper number, which makes a larger amount rather than another name for the same one.

### 3 · Hitting a numerator or a denominator you are given
**Screen:** the target bay holding a fraction with one number of its partner already printed
**Does:** works out what that number was multiplied or divided by, then uses it on the other
**Win:** the factor is named, the other number fills in by itself and the strips are drawn to match
**Feels:** the number that was handed to you turns out to be the instruction for the one that was not
**Goal:** the factor is found from the number that was given
**Observe:** The target bay would not fill the blank until the factor had been named.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- sees that five became twenty by going up fifteen and adds fifteen to the six as well. → the factor slot rejects nothing and prints nothing; instead the bench draws both readings as strips from the common edge. Twenty over twenty-one is cut into twenty-one with twenty filled and its colour runs almost to the end of the strip, while five over six stops well short, and the guide line shows the gap. Multiplying both by four gives twenty over twenty-four on a third strip, which lands on the line, and the child's answer stays in the slot beside it.
- works out the factor correctly and then fills in only the number that was asked for, writing thirty-six over five. → the factor the child named, twelve, is printed between the two fractions and stays there. The bench then draws an arrow from the sixty to the five labelled with that factor and a second arrow from the thirty-six to its blank, still empty, so the missing half of the move is the thing on the stage. The two strips are laid from the common edge and thirty-six over five will not cut at all, because a strip cannot be divided into five parts and have thirty-six of them filled; the bench says so in words and leaves the working standing.
**Misconception to break:** Adding the difference to the other number instead of multiplying it by the same factor.

### 4 · Testing a pair without building anything
**Screen:** two written fractions on the cross bench with the strips hidden
**Does:** drags a line each way across the pair and reads the two products
**Win:** both products are written up, and the verdict follows from whether they match
**Feels:** two numbers written in a row, and the verdict is read off them rather than off the fractions
**Goal:** equal crossing products mean equivalent fractions
**Observe:** With every picture hidden, the two crossing products still settled the pair.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- multiplies upper number by upper number and lower number by lower number, and compares those two. → the two straight lines the child drew are left on the bench with their products, eighty-four and a hundred and eighty-nine, and they are not crossed out. The bench then draws the two crossing lines over them in a different weight, from the upper number of each fraction to the lower number of the other, and writes a hundred and twenty-six at the middle of each; the verdict panel fills only from the crossed pair, and the four products stay on the stage together.
- draws one crossing line, writes its product and gives a verdict on that alone. → the verdict panel stays blank with the reason drawn rather than said: the second crossing line is drawn empty, from the upper number of the second fraction to the lower number of the first, with its product space ruled and waiting. The child's first product stays written at the middle of its own line, and the panel only fills once both spaces hold a number, whichever way the two then compare.
**Misconception to break:** Multiplying upper by upper and lower by lower, which tests nothing.

## Delight
The second phase, at the moment the builder is set to double and released. The lower strip does not redraw itself all at once. Every score line on it divides, one after another from the left, so the seven parts become fourteen while a hand watches, and each filled part splits into two filled parts of half the size. The colour spreads into the new cut at the very instant the cut appears, so nothing is ever briefly wrong, and the far edge of the fill never moves off the guide line by so much as a hair. When it settles, the two written fractions under the strips read two over seven and four over fourteen, and the guide line runs down through both fills as though it had been drawn for one of them. Push the builder back to one and the whole thing runs in reverse, the cuts pairing off again. Nothing else on the bench moves and the voice says nothing at all while it happens.

**General miss line:** Lay them from the same edge again and watch where the colour stops, not how many cuts there are.

## Answer key
Two fractions are equivalent when they stand for the same amount, however different they look. To build one, multiply the upper number and the lower number by the same number, or divide them both by a common factor. Running through the multiples of both numbers together makes several at once. When one of the two numbers is given, work out what the matching number was multiplied or divided by, and do the same to the other. To test a pair without building anything, cross multiply: upper number of the first by lower number of the second, and upper number of the second by lower number of the first. Equal products mean the fractions are equivalent, and unequal products mean they are not.

## Student controls
- **Lay a strip**: cuts a strip into its parts and fills as many as the upper number says
- **Multiply both**: multiplies both numbers by the chosen number and redraws the lower strip
- **Divide both**: divides both numbers by a common factor and redraws the lower strip
- **Run the multiples**: steps through several equivalents at once and writes them in a row
- **Target bay**: takes a given upper or lower number and asks for the factor before filling the other half
- **Cross bench**: lays two fractions side by side and draws each crossing product as it is made
- **Hide the strips**: clears the pictures so a pair has to be judged by crossing alone
