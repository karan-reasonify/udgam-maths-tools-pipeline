## What it is
**Description:** Cut a whole into equal parts, colour some of them, and write the fraction that names what you took.
**Why:** Cut one whole into equal parts, take some of them, and find out which of the two numbers of a fraction does which job.
**Look:** One whole thing in the middle of the stage with a cutter above it, an empty fraction being filled in underneath, and five labelled trays along the foot.
**Context:** Grade 4, the first working topic of the fractions chapter. An adult sees a shape being shaded in, which a child could do with a pencil. What is actually being settled is which of the two written numbers does which job, and that equal is part of the meaning rather than a tidiness rule. A child who reads the pair as two ordinary numbers will go on to say that three eighths beats three quarters, and will say it with confidence, because eight is the larger number.
**For the child:** Cut the whole first, then colour the parts you are taking. Count the cuts for the number below the line. Count the coloured parts for the number above it. If the pieces are not equal, no name will fit. On the shelf, a card may belong in more than one tray.
**Teaching notes:** Fold a paper square into equal parts and colour some of them by hand before the tool is opened, and write the fraction underneath the folded square rather than beside it, so the picture and the writing are read together from the start. Then use the tool for the thing paper cannot do. Go to the free cut early and make the pieces plainly unequal on purpose, and ask the class to name one of them. The refusal is the teaching, and it is worth waiting for somebody to argue about. Insist on reading every fraction from the bottom up, out loud, in two halves: the whole was cut into this many equal parts, and this many were taken. A class that reads the lower number first has already stopped comparing the two numbers as though they were separate. Use the collection view once the cut whole is secure, because a child who has only ever seen a shaded circle will not recognise the same fraction in a row of counters. The sorting shelf is where the recap work belongs: let a card sit in two trays at once, and do not rush the card that is improper because its two numbers are equal, which is the one the class will leave out. The thirteen vocabulary words of the chapter can be made into cards from this shelf, one word to a tray, and quizzed in pairs.

## Phases (what each mount shows and does; the words are in tool.tsx)

### 1 · What the two numbers count
**Screen:** one uncut bar, the cutter dial above it and two empty fraction slots below
**Does:** sets the number of cuts, colours some of the parts, then fills the two slots
**Win:** both slots fill, and the cuts and the coloured parts are counted back against the picture
**Feels:** the bar is cut before anything is coloured, so the lower number is already there and waiting
**Goal:** the lower number counts the equal parts and the upper number counts the parts taken
**Observe:** The cuts went in before any colour did, and the lower number could be counted on a bar with nothing filled.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- writes the seven on top and the four underneath, because the seven is the number the sentence gives first. → the counting pass runs on the slots exactly as the child filled them. The lower slot asks for four and only four of the seven cut lines are ticked before it is satisfied, leaving three pieces of the bar outside the count altogether, and the upper slot then asks for seven coloured pieces and runs out after four. The child's two tiles stay exactly where they were dropped, a second bar cut into seven is drawn beneath the first with four of its parts filled, and the two bars are left standing together.
- counts the pieces left over instead of the pieces the bar was cut into, and writes four over three. → the counting pass runs twice over the same bar with nothing cleared. The first pass counts only the plain pieces and stops at three; the second runs along the whole bar and counts seven, the four filled ones included, with the bracket spanning the whole bar drawn green above it. The lower slot is read out as the count under that bracket, and the three the child wrote is left standing beside it so the two counts can be held against each other.
**Misconception to break:** Writing 7/4 because seven was the number mentioned first.

### 2 · Equal parts, or no fraction at all
**Screen:** a bar with one free cut line resting a little off its middle
**Does:** slides the cut until the two pieces plainly differ, then tries to name one
**Win:** the naming slot refuses the label, an even cut is drawn beside the uneven one, and the name lands only once the pieces match
**Feels:** four pieces, and not one of them has a name until all four of them match
**Goal:** pieces that are not equal have no fraction name
**Observe:** The pieces nobody took stayed on the mat, full size, for the whole of every phase.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- calls one of four unequal pieces a quarter, because there are four pieces lying on the mat. → the slot refuses the tile and prints what is wrong with the picture rather than what the answer is. An evenly cut copy of the same strip is drawn directly beneath from the same left edge, and the long piece is slid down onto the small ones, where it covers two of them exactly. The strip is then read out as five pieces of that smaller size, so each small piece is one fifth of it, and the child's own cut stays exactly where it was slid to.
- nudges the cut until the two pieces look about right and names them halves without measuring anything. → the two pieces are lifted off the strip and laid one on top of the other, where the longer one overhangs by the amount it overhangs by. A measuring bar runs along each and prints its length, and the naming slot stays shut while the two lengths differ. The moment the cut is slid until the two measure the same the slot opens by itself and the word half goes in, and no tile is ever moved for the child.
**Misconception to break:** Calling any one of four pieces a quarter because there happen to be four of them.

### 3 · The five kinds, sorted by their two numbers
**Screen:** the shelf of five labelled trays with a stack of written fractions beside it
**Does:** drops each written fraction into every tray its two numbers earn
**Win:** every card settles in each tray it earns, and the cards sharing a lower number are drawn together
**Feels:** one card, three trays open at once, and not one of the three is wrong
**Goal:** a fraction is sorted by comparing its two numbers
**Observe:** With the cut slid off centre, no label would go into the slot at all.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- leaves 8/8 out of the improper tray, because its two numbers are the same rather than one of them being bigger. → the test is printed above the tray in the words it is actually written in, numerator greater than or equal to denominator, with greater and equal lit one after the other as separate ways of passing. The card is then drawn out on the mat behind the shelf: a whole cut into eight parts with eight of them filled leaves nothing amber anywhere and the green bracket spanning the whole sits exactly over the filled run, so the card is one whole. The child's sorting is left exactly as it was and no card is moved for them.
- drops each card into one tray only, as though a fraction had to pick a single kind and stay in it. → each card is lifted in turn and every one of the five tests is run on it with the trays it passes lighting together rather than one at a time. On 1/9 three trays light at once, because its upper number is the smaller one, its upper number is one, and it shares a lower number with 5/9 standing in the stack beside it. Every card the child has already placed stays where it was put and the further trays it earns are simply drawn open beside it.
**Misconception to break:** Leaving 8/8 out of the improper tray because the two numbers are the same rather than one being bigger.

### 4 · Halves and quarters in one field
**Screen:** one whole already cut in half, with one of the halves waiting
**Does:** cuts one half in two, names the new pieces, and slides everything back into a whole
**Win:** the pieces close back into one whole and the three fractions are written under them
**Feels:** a half and two quarters, three pieces of two different sizes, and they still close back into one whole
**Goal:** two quarters make a half and four quarters make a whole
**Observe:** One card landed in three trays at once and nothing on the shelf objected.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- calls the two smaller pieces halves, because they were cut out of a half. → the two small pieces are laid onto the untouched half and two of them cover it exactly, so the half is redrawn as two of that size standing side by side. The whole circle is then rebuilt and the same piece is laid onto it, where four of that size are needed to fill it and the count is drawn out one piece at a time. The names the child wrote stay printed under their pieces and the new naming is written underneath rather than over them.
- counts the three pieces lying on the mat and calls each of them a third. → a circle of exactly the same size is drawn beside the child's and ruled into three genuinely equal parts. The half is laid over one of those thirds and hangs past it, and each of the two quarters is laid over one and falls short of it. The refusal from the second phase is printed again underneath, that pieces which are not all the same size have no single name, and the child's three labels stay on the mat throughout.
**Misconception to break:** Calling the two smaller pieces halves because they came out of a half.

## Delight
The fourth phase, the moment cut again lands on one of the two halves. The piece opens along a single drawn line into two, and every label on the mat rewrites itself in the same movement: the untouched piece still reads as a half, and the two new ones come up as quarters while the bracket spanning the whole circle stays green and stays exactly the size it was. Then the three pieces slide back together and close into one circle with no seam showing, and the three fractions are written in a row underneath, a half and a quarter and a quarter, with the whole drawn whole above them. Push a finger against the closed circle and it opens again into the same three pieces, so the cutting and the closing can be run back and forth as often as a hand likes. Nothing else on the mat moves and the voice says nothing at all while it happens.

**General miss line:** Count the cuts first and the coloured pieces second. The mat is still showing both.

## Answer key
The lower number says how many equal parts the whole was cut into, and the upper number says how many of those parts are being counted. Read a fraction from the bottom up, because a piece has to have a size before there is anything to count. The parts must be equal or there is no fraction to name them at all. The same written fraction serves one thing cut up and a group of separate things. A fraction is proper when its upper number is the smaller one, improper when its upper number is greater than or equal to its lower one, and a unit fraction when its upper number is one. Two fractions are like when they share a lower number and unlike when they do not. Two quarters make a half, two halves make a whole, and four quarters make a whole.

## Student controls
- **Cutter dial**: cuts the whole into that many equal parts and draws every cut
- **Take a part**: colours a part and adds it to the count above the line
- **Free cut**: replaces the even cutter with a line the child can slide off centre
- **Name it**: opens the two fraction slots and reads them back against the picture
- **Collection view**: swaps the cut whole for a row of separate things of one kind
- **Sorting shelf**: opens the five labelled trays and the stack of written fractions
- **Cut again**: cuts one chosen part in two and renames every piece
