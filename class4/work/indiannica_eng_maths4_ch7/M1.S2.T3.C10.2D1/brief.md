## What it is
**Description:** Split a pile into equal bowls, collect as many bowls as the fraction asks for, and keep the rest in sight.
**Why:** Take a fraction of a pile by splitting it into equal bowls and collecting some of them, and keep the bowls you did not take where you can see them.
**Look:** A pile on the left, a row of bowls on the right, a tray above them and the words of the question running across the top.
**Context:** Grade 4, the topic that turns a fraction into an amount. An adult sees a division followed by a multiplication, which is two things already known. What is actually being taught is the order, and that the answer to the arithmetic is often not the answer to the question. A child who divides last is working with numbers three or four times larger than needed, and a child who stops at the tray has answered half of a two part question while getting every step right.
**For the child:** Read the fraction above the bench. Open one bowl for each part the lower number names. Deal the pile out until every bowl holds the same. Lift as many bowls as the upper number says. Before you answer, read the question again.
**Teaching notes:** Deal real counters into real bowls for the first two questions, because a child who has watched sixty counters go four at a time into fifteen bowls has seen a division happen and will not later ask which number goes into which. Say the method in the printed order every time, divide then multiply, and say why rather than only how: the other order gives the same answer and hands a nine year old a three digit number to divide. Run the both ways control once, early, so that the class sees the two lines agreeing and stops suspecting that the order is a rule with no reason behind it. The part worth slowing down on is the bench. Leave the untaken bowls standing while the answer is written, and ask every time what that second number would be, whether or not the question wants it, until asking becomes automatic. Two of the printed word problems want the share and one wants what is left, and nothing in the arithmetic tells them apart. Finish on the question strip with the underline hidden, and have the class underline what is being asked for before a single counter moves.

## Phases (what each mount shows and does; the words are in tool.tsx)

### 1 · What of means here
**Screen:** a pile of counters with a fraction written above the empty bowls
**Does:** reads the fraction, opens the bowls it asks for and deals the pile out
**Win:** the bowls fill evenly with nothing left over and the pile is empty
**Feels:** the word of turns out to be an instruction, and the number underneath the line is the one that carries it out
**Goal:** a fraction of a number means splitting it and keeping some of the parts
**Observe:** The lower number opened the bowls and the upper number lifted them, and the two jobs never swapped over.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- reads the word of as a signal to take away and works out forty-two take away seven. → the word of is underlined on the question strip and the bench draws what each reading would do, side by side and neither marked. Taking seven away leaves thirty-five tokens in one heap with no bowl opened at all and no way for the two of the fraction to do anything; opening seven bowls and dealing fills each with six, and the two lifts two of them onto the tray for twelve. Both readings stay on the stage and the child's answer stays in the slot.
- opens two bowls rather than seven, because the two is the number read first. → the bench deals the pile into the two bowls the child opened and each one fills with twenty-one, printed underneath. The fraction above the bench is then read from the bottom up with the lower number lit, it asks for seven bowls, and the rail plainly has two; the lift is refused with nothing marked and the two full bowls are left standing exactly as the child filled them while seven empty bowls are drawn open beside them.
**Misconception to break:** Reading of as a signal to subtract, and working out forty-two take away seven.

### 2 · Divide first, then multiply
**Screen:** the same pile with both working lines open, one above the other
**Does:** works it by dividing first, then runs the other order and compares
**Win:** both lines land on the same answer and the larger middle number on the other line is ringed
**Feels:** both roads arrive at the same place and only one of them can be walked in your head
**Goal:** dividing before multiplying keeps the numbers small
**Observe:** Dividing first and multiplying first landed on the same answer, and one line carried a three digit number through its middle.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- multiplies by the upper number first and then loses the thread inside the larger division. → both working lines run together and neither is marked. The multiply-first line is followed through to the end and lands on twenty-four, exactly where the divide-first line lands, so the order is never called wrong; the ring closes on the hundred and ninety-two it had to carry through its middle, and the eight the other line carried is ringed beside it. Both lines stay written out in full.
- divides the pile by the lower number and hands in what one bowl holds, without lifting anything onto the tray. → the fraction above the bench is read from the bottom up with each number lit as its job is named, the eight opening the bowls and the three lifting them. The eight the child wrote stays in the slot and the bench then lifts three bowls onto the tray one at a time with the running tray count climbing eight, sixteen, twenty-four, and the five bowls not lifted stay full on the bench behind it.
**Misconception to break:** Multiplying by the upper number first and then getting lost in the larger division.

### 3 · One share found, the other still waiting
**Screen:** the tray loaded and the bench still holding the bowls nobody took
**Does:** counts the tray, then counts what is still on the bench
**Win:** both numbers are written up and add back to the pile the bench started with
**Feels:** the bowls nobody lifted were standing there the whole time, already holding the other answer
**Goal:** the bowls that were not lifted are an answer too
**Observe:** The bowls nobody lifted held forty into thirty-two on one question and were the answer on the next.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- writes forty and stops, when the question asked how many there were of each colour. → the question strip stays lit above the bench with both of its asks underlined, and the tray and the bench are counted separately with each count written up under the thing it counted, forty on the tray and thirty-two on the bench. The child's forty stays in the slot and the leftover check adds the two counts against the seventy-two the bench began with. Nothing is cleared.
- counts the bowls left rather than what is in them, and answers four. → the four bowls left are lifted clear of the rail and emptied out onto the bench, where the tokens in them are counted one at a time up to thirty-two, with the count under each bowl still reading eight. The child's four is left in the slot and the two numbers are printed one above the other with what each of them counts written beside it, four bowls and thirty-two pencils.
**Misconception to break:** Writing forty and stopping, when the question asked for both colours.

### 4 · Checking the answer against the question
**Screen:** the question strip with the asked-for part underlined and the answer bay empty
**Does:** reads the strip again and sends either the tray or the bench to the bay
**Win:** the bay accepts the pile the question asked for and the other pile stays drawn beside it
**Feels:** the arithmetic was right the whole way through and the answer was still the wrong one of the two piles
**Goal:** the answer written down is the one the question asked for
**Observe:** One answer was wrong with every step of its arithmetic right.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- writes eighteen, the number the working produced, when the question asked how many kites were left. → the answer bay returns the tray with nothing marked and re-reads the underlined words of the question without saying any number. The eighteen stays exactly where the child put it, the three bowls nobody lifted are counted out on the bench to twenty-seven, and the two counts stand side by side under the question strip with the underline still drawn above them.
- hands in both numbers together and leaves whoever reads it to pick the one that was wanted. → the underline is drawn again under the words of the question, one word at a time, and the bay accepts one pile only. The pile the underline is pointing at lights on the bench and the other stays drawn beside it, unmarked and uncleared, so both are still countable while the single answer is written. The two counts are then read back as a pair, the eighteen that went and the twenty-seven that stayed.
**Misconception to break:** Writing eighteen, which is the number worked out rather than the number asked for.

## Delight
The second phase, the moment both ways is pressed. The two working lines run at once and in step, not one after the other: the upper line divides sixty-four by eight, writes the eight, multiplies by three and writes twenty-four, while the lower line multiplies three by sixty-four, writes a hundred and ninety-two in the middle of itself, and then has to take that back down by eight. The ring closes around the hundred and ninety-two as it is written, and the two answers land on their lines on the very same tick, both reading twenty-four. Neither line is marked and neither is called the wrong way round; the only difference on the stage is the size of the number each of them had to carry. Nothing else moves and the voice says nothing at all while it happens.

**General miss line:** Deal the pile out again, one round at a time, and count what a single bowl is holding.

## Answer key
Between a fraction and a number, the word of means take that fraction of the number. Divide the whole number by the lower number of the fraction first, which splits it into that many equal groups, then multiply the answer by the upper number, which collects that many groups. Dividing first keeps the numbers small enough to work in the head; multiplying first gives the same answer through larger ones. What is left is the whole number take away the share that was collected. Read the question again before writing the answer down, because it may want the share, or what is left, or both.

## Student controls
- **Open bowls**: opens as many bowls as the lower number of the fraction
- **Deal out**: shares the pile evenly into the open bowls, one round at a time
- **Lift to tray**: moves as many full bowls onto the tray as the upper number says
- **Both ways**: runs the multiply first order beside the divide first one and shows both lines
- **Question strip**: holds the words of the problem with the thing asked for underlined
- **Answer bay**: takes either the tray or the bench as the answer to the question
- **Leftover check**: adds the tray and the bench back together against the starting pile

## Textbook (this topic)
**Pages:** 74, 75
**Key terms:** of, divide, multiply, whole number
A fraction of a quantity is found by dividing by the denominator and then multiplying by the numerator. Divide the whole number by the denominator to make equal groups, then multiply by the numerator to keep some of the groups. Take that share away from the total to find the rest. Read the question again to see which one it asks for. To take three fifths of the sixty, which means splitting sixty into five equal parts and keeping three of them. Between a fraction and a number, of means multiply. Divide first. Sixty divided by five is twelve, and three twelves are thirty-six. The other way makes a hundred and eighty and then divides it. Same answer, harder numbers. Fifteen go to the guest, so forty are left for the principal. Finding the fraction answered only part of it. Take the share away from the total to get the rest. Read the question again. It may ask for the part you found, or for what is left, or for both. The arithmetic can be right while the answer is incomplete.
