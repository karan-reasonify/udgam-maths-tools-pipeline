## What it is
**Description:** Push pieces of one size together or take some away, then tidy the answer before you write it down.
**Why:** Put pieces of one size together, or take some away, and then find out what still has to be done to the answer before it can be written down.
**Look:** Two sets of equal pieces on a bench with their size written above it, a running count at the top left and a tidy rail along the foot.
**Context:** Grade 4, the closing topic, and the shortest rule in the chapter. An adult sees numerators being added. What is actually being taught is everything after that: an answer is not finished until it has been looked at again. This is where the earlier topics are paid for, because tidying an answer needs lowest terms and mixed numbers, and a child who has only the adding rule will write a top-heavy fraction and think the question is done.
**For the child:** Check that both fractions are made of pieces of one size. Add or take away only the numbers above the line. The number below the line stays as it is. Now look at your answer again. Gather any whole ones, and reduce if the two numbers share a factor.
**Teaching notes:** Push two handfuls of identical paper pieces together by hand before anything is written, and ask what has changed and what has not. The answer that the pieces are still the same size is the whole rule, and a class that has said it out loud will not add the lower numbers. Keep the piece size written above the bench and point at it every time, including during subtraction, because subtraction is where children start taking the lower numbers apart. Then give the tidying its own share of the lesson, because it is longer than the rule it follows. Walk answers along the rail that need one step, both steps and neither, in that order, and say each time which stations were skipped and why, so tidying is a decision rather than a reflex. Work one sum whose answer turns out to be a whole number, and let somebody point out that there is nothing to reduce. The closing crossword is nothing but like-fraction subtraction, so work every clue on the bench and drop it into its cell. One cell comes out with nothing in it at all. Leave that cell empty, say that this chapter has not given nothing a fraction name, and sort only the cells that carry sixteenths when the grid asks which of the answers are like fractions. Finish by reading the seven statements of what a child can now do, one at a time, against the workings still on the bench.

## Phases (what each mount shows and does; the words are in tool.tsx)

### 1 · Adding the tops and nothing else
**Screen:** two sets of pieces of one size with the size written above the bench
**Does:** pushes the two sets together and reads the new count against the fixed size
**Win:** the heap count lands on the sum, the size above the bench has not moved, and the answer is written
**Feels:** the count changes and the size written above the bench does not move at all
**Goal:** adding like fractions adds the upper numbers and keeps the lower one
**Observe:** The count above the bench climbed while the size written beside it never moved.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- adds the lower numbers as well as the upper ones, writing 3/10 and 4/10 as seven twentieths. → the heaps are pushed together again at walking pace with the pinned ten lit above the bench, and the count climbs to seven while the size beside it holds. The seven over twenty the child wrote is then laid out on a second bench, where a whole one takes twenty pieces and seven of them fill barely a third of it, against a whole one in tenths with seven filled. Both benches stay drawn, side by side, and neither is marked.
- tidies one of the two fractions before adding, reducing 2/12 to 1/6 and then trying to push it into a heap of twelfths. → the push is refused with nothing marked, and the reason is drawn rather than stated: one piece from each heap is lifted out and laid side by side, and the sixth is plainly twice the size of the twelfth. The pinned size above the bench is lit and read out as the thing both heaps have to agree on. The child's reduced fraction stays on the ledge, the original heap of twelfths is laid back beside the other, and the push then goes through to ten twelfths.
**Misconception to break:** Adding the lower numbers as well and writing seven over twenty.

### 2 · Taking away works the same way
**Screen:** one heap of pieces with a second fraction marked out inside it
**Does:** lifts the marked pieces away and counts what is left
**Win:** the remaining pieces are counted, the size is unchanged and the difference is written
**Feels:** the pieces that left and the pieces that stayed are exactly the same size as each other
**Goal:** subtracting like fractions subtracts the upper numbers and keeps the lower one
**Observe:** Taking pieces away behaved exactly as putting them together had.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- subtracts the lower numbers too, and cannot say what the answer is written over. → the four pieces are lifted away again one at a time onto the ledge and the pinned fourteen stays lit above the bench throughout, with the pieces on the ledge and the pieces on the bench laid side by side and plainly the same size. The bench then asks for the answer to be laid out from the child's writing and there is no piece size to lay it in, so nothing can be built; the five pieces still on the bench are counted and written over the pinned number instead, and the child's writing stays beside it.
- counts the five pieces left and writes them over the nine the heap started with. → the five remaining pieces are laid into an empty whole outline ruled into fourteen and they fill five of its fourteen places, with nine places left amber. Beside it an outline ruled into nine is drawn and the same five pieces will not fit its places at all, because a ninth is bigger than a fourteenth, and one of each is lifted out and laid together to show it. The child's answer stays in the slot and both outlines stay drawn.
**Misconception to break:** Subtracting the lower numbers too and writing five over nothing at all.

### 3 · When the answer is more than one whole
**Screen:** a heap that has filled one whole outline with pieces still over
**Does:** gathers the full whole aside and reads what is left beside it
**Win:** the whole ones stand apart from the loose pieces and the mixed number is written under them
**Feels:** the pieces fill a whole one and the ones that did not fit are still lying there, still the same size
**Goal:** an answer whose top is not smaller is written as a mixed number
**Observe:** The tidy rail greyed a station out and printed why, rather than quietly stepping over it.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- leaves the answer as fifteen ninths, because the adding was done correctly and the sum looks finished. → the fifteen pieces are pushed into the outline behind the bench, it closes at the ninth and lifts clear as one whole with six pieces still loose under it. The fifteen ninths the child wrote stays on the stage and the rail writes the other readings beside it, joined by a rose tie rather than replacing it: one whole one and six ninths, and then five thirds and one whole one and two thirds once the reducing station has run. Nothing is crossed through.
- gathers the whole one and writes the leftover pieces over the count the heap had before gathering, giving one whole and six fifteenths. → the six loose pieces are laid into a second outline ruled into ninths, where they fill six of its nine places, and the pinned nine is lit above the bench as the size every piece on it still is. An outline ruled into fifteen is drawn beside it and one of its places is laid against one loose piece, where the piece plainly overhangs. The child's writing stays on the stage with the tray's reading written under it.
**Misconception to break:** Leaving the answer as fifteen ninths because the adding was done correctly.

### 4 · Tidying what is left
**Screen:** the tidy rail with a written answer waiting at its first station
**Does:** walks the answer along the rail, running only the steps it actually needs
**Win:** the tidied answer is written beside the untidied one, and a station that was not needed is shown skipped
**Feels:** the sum was over some time ago and the answer still had one more thing owing on it
**Goal:** an answer sharing a factor is reduced, and some answers are whole numbers
**Observe:** One sum came out as a whole number with no fraction part left to reduce.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- writes six twelfths and stops, because the subtraction itself was finished. → the answer walks the rail with the child's writing carried along beside it. The gathering station greys out with its reason printed, that the upper number is the smaller one so no whole is filled; the reducing station lights, because six and twelve share a factor above one, and it writes one half beside the six twelfths with a rose tie between them. Both writings stay on the stage and the six twelfths is never crossed through.
- reduces by dividing the upper number of the answer and leaving the lower number as it was. → the reducing station draws both numbers lit together and refuses to write a rung on one of them, as the ladder did. The one twelfth the child built is laid out on a second bench, where a whole one takes twelve pieces and one of them fills a single place, against the six twelfths on the first bench filling six. Both benches stay drawn, and dividing both numbers by six is then run on a third, landing on one half with its single piece covering exactly the same length as the six.
**Misconception to break:** Writing six twelfths and stopping, because the subtraction itself was finished.

## Delight
The third phase, the moment the heap passes one whole. The pieces are pushed together one at a time and the empty outline behind the bench takes them in order, each piece dropping into its own ruled place, and at the ninth piece the outline closes: the green bracket spanning it comes round, the last cut line inside it fades to leave one unbroken circle, and the whole one lifts clear of the bench and stands apart while the six pieces that did not fit stay loose below it. Both readings write themselves at the same instant, fifteen ninths on one line and one whole one with six ninths beside it on the other, and the nine in each of them is drawn arriving from the pinned size at the top rather than from anywhere in the counting. Push the whole one back down and it opens into nine pieces again. Nothing else on the bench moves and the voice says nothing at all while it happens.

**General miss line:** Push them together again slowly, and keep looking at the size written above the bench.

## Answer key
To add like fractions, add the upper numbers and keep the lower number. To subtract them, subtract the upper numbers and keep the lower number. The lower number is not added and not subtracted, because it says how big each piece is and pushing pieces together does not change their size. The answer is then tidied. If its upper number is greater than or equal to its lower number it is written as a mixed number, and if it divides exactly it is written as a whole number with nothing left to reduce. If its two numbers share a factor above one it is reduced to lowest terms. Some answers need both steps and some need neither, so every answer is looked at rather than tidied by reflex.

## Student controls
- **Lay two fractions**: puts two sets of pieces of one size on the bench
- **Push together**: makes one heap and climbs the running count above the bench
- **Lift away**: takes the second fraction off the heap piece by piece
- **Piece size lock**: holds the lower number above the bench and refuses to change it mid-working
- **Tidy rail**: reads the answer at three stations and runs whichever steps it needs
- **Gather the wholes**: collects full wholes out of the heap and writes the mixed number
- **Crossword bay**: opens a small grid of like fraction subtractions, one clue at a time

## Textbook (this topic)
**Pages:** 82, 83, 84, 85
**Key terms:** like fractions, add, subtract, lowest terms
Like fractions are added and subtracted by working on the numerators alone, and answers are tidied. Add or subtract the numerators and keep the denominator, because the pieces stay the same size. If the answer is more than one whole, write it as a mixed number. If its two numbers share a factor, reduce it to lowest terms. It stays as it is. Add only the numerators, giving seven ninths. The nine says how big each piece is, and putting pieces together does not make them a different size. Subtract the numerators and keep the denominator, giving three elevenths. Subtraction follows the same rule as addition, because the pieces are still the same size throughout. Not quite. The top is bigger than the bottom, so it is improper. Divide to turn it into a mixed number, and it reads as one and one thirteenth. Reduce it. Twelve and eight share four, so it becomes three halves, which is one and a half. Sometimes the tidying gives a whole number instead, and then there is nothing left to reduce.
