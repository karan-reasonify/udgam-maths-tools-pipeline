## What it is
**Description:** Divide both numbers of a fraction by the same factor, rung by rung, until only one is left in common.
**Why:** Take a fraction down to its tidiest writing by dividing both of its numbers by the same thing, and watch the amount stay exactly where it was.
**Look:** A fraction at the top of a ladder down the middle of the stage, factor lists under each of its two numbers, and a filled strip down the right.
**Context:** Grade 4, the topic that decides what a tidy fraction looks like. An adult sees cancelling, which is a mechanical move. What is being settled is that both numbers have to travel together, and that reducing is a change of writing and not a change of amount. A child who divides only the upper number has produced a different fraction and will not see it, because the working still looks like cancelling.
**For the child:** Open the two factor lists and look for a number in both. Drop that factor on the fraction to divide both numbers. You cannot move one number without the other. Keep stepping down while anything above one is shared. Watch the strip on the right as you go.
**Teaching notes:** List the factors of both numbers by hand before the tool is opened, in two columns, and ring the shared ones. That listing is the part children skip and it is the part that makes the rest possible. Then use the tool for the two things paper will not do. The first is the refusal: let somebody try to divide just the upper number and let the ladder hand it back, and ask what would have happened to the amount if it had gone through. The second is the value bar, which should be on from the first rung to the last. A class that has watched the strip stay exactly still while the numbers halve twice has been given the reason the move is allowed, and will not later think a reduced fraction is a smaller one. Open the second ladder for a fraction with several routes down, such as thirty-two over forty-eight, and put a small-step child beside a largest-factor child. Both are right and one is quicker, and saying that plainly protects the child who halves and halves again. Finish by laying the first and last rungs together, and read them as one amount with two names, because that sentence is the bridge into the next topic.

## Phases (what each mount shows and does; the words are in tool.tsx)

### 1 · Dividing both numbers by the same thing
**Screen:** one fraction on the top rung with its two factor lists open
**Does:** picks a shared factor and drops it on the fraction, then tries to move one number alone
**Win:** the next rung is written with both numbers smaller, and the single number drag is handed back
**Feels:** one factor, used on both numbers in the same movement, and no other arrangement of the two will go through
**Goal:** both numbers are divided, and by the same factor
**Observe:** The numbers on the last rung were far smaller than the numbers on the first, and the strip beside them filled to exactly the same place.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- divides the upper number by seven and leaves the lower number standing as it was. → the drag is handed back with nothing written on the rung below and both numbers lit together. The ladder then draws what that move would have produced on a scratch rung set off to one side, two over twenty-one, with its own value bar beside it; that bar fills to barely a tenth while the bar for fourteen over twenty-one stands at two thirds of the strip, and the guide line runs across both. The child's drag is not repeated for them and nothing is cleared.
- divides the two numbers by different factors, taking two out of the fourteen and three out of the twenty-one. → the two divisions are written out under their own numbers and both are correct arithmetic, so neither is crossed through. What they build, seven over seven, is drawn on a scratch rung with its value bar, and that bar fills the whole strip because seven parts of something cut into seven is one whole; the bar for fourteen over twenty-one stops two thirds along, and the two are left standing together with the shared factor seven still lit in both lists.
**Misconception to break:** Dividing the upper number by seven and leaving the lower one alone.

### 2 · Knowing when to stop
**Screen:** a fraction part way down the ladder with the divider tray still holding tiles
**Does:** checks the two factor lists for anything shared above one and steps again if there is
**Win:** the tray empties, the done light comes up and the last rung is ringed
**Feels:** the numbers being small is not the test at all, and the two lists are
**Goal:** the fraction is finished when its two numbers share only one
**Observe:** A drag aimed at one of the two numbers was handed back every single time.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- stops at six over nine because the numbers have got small and the working looks finished. → both factor lists open under the rung the child stopped on and three comes up drawn the same on each, with the divider tray refilling itself to hold it. The six over nine the child wrote stays on its rung, the next rung is left ruled and empty rather than filled in for them, and the done light stays dark with the tray plainly not empty. Nothing is marked.
- drags a factor that goes into only one of the two numbers, trying two on six over nine because two goes into six. → the tile springs back without a rung being written, because the tray only ever holds factors standing in both lists. Both lists open with two lit in the six's row and plainly absent from the nine's, and nine is written out against two underneath, going four times with one left over. The tray is then shown holding three, the one factor the two numbers really do share, and nothing is dropped for the child.
**Misconception to break:** Stopping as soon as the numbers look small, without checking the two factor lists.

### 3 · The long way and the short way
**Screen:** two ladders side by side, both starting from the same fraction
**Does:** takes small steps down one ladder and the largest step down the other
**Win:** both ladders reach the same last rung and the step counts are written beside them
**Feels:** two ladders of different lengths, standing on exactly the same last rung
**Goal:** a smaller factor used twice reaches the same rung as the largest used once
**Observe:** Small steps and one large step reached the same last rung, and one ladder had three rungs where the other had two.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- decides the longer route must have gone wrong somewhere, because it took three lines where the other took one. → the two ladders are left standing side by side with every rung on both still written, and the drawn line joins their two last rungs straight across, both reading two over three. The step count under each is printed as a count and not as a mark, three steps and one step, and the value bars beside the two ladders are drawn at the same fill on every rung of both. Neither ladder is ringed and neither is called the better one.
- reaches for thirty-two, the largest factor of the upper number, rather than the largest factor the two numbers share. → the tile is not in the tray to be dragged, because the tray holds shared factors only, and the reason is drawn rather than stated: forty-eight is written out against thirty-two and goes once with sixteen left over. Both lists are then read down together and the last number standing in both is sixteen, which the tray offers; the child's reach is left drawn as a faint line so the two candidates can be seen at once.
**Misconception to break:** Believing that a longer route must have gone wrong somewhere because it took more lines.

### 4 · The amount does not move
**Screen:** the whole ladder with the value bar redrawn beside every rung
**Does:** runs back up and down the ladder watching the filled strip
**Win:** every strip lines up exactly with every other one and the first and last rungs are laid together
**Feels:** the numbers shrink right down the ladder and the strip beside them does not move at all
**Goal:** reducing changes the writing and leaves the value alone
**Observe:** The tray emptied itself while there were still factors written in both lists.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- says five eighths must be less than twenty thirty-seconds, because its two numbers are so much smaller. → the two rungs lift out of the ladder and settle side by side with their value bars drawn against each other and the guide line running through both fills. The strip for twenty over thirty-two carries thirty-two cuts and the strip for five over eight carries eight, and the colour stops in exactly the same place on both. The child's answer stays in the slot and both bars stay drawn.
- takes four off both numbers instead of dividing both by four, and writes sixteen over twenty-eight. → the sixteen over twenty-eight the child built is drawn on a scratch rung with a value bar of its own, and it stops visibly short of the guide line that the whole ladder stands on. Beside it the same starting fraction is divided by four in both numbers, landing on five over eight with its bar on the line. Both are left standing, neither is crossed through, and the two moves are printed underneath in the words that tell them apart, taken off and divided by.
**Misconception to break:** Thinking that smaller numbers must mean a smaller amount.

## Delight
The fourth phase, when a finger runs down and back up the finished ladder. The numbers on the rungs shrink from twenty over thirty-two to five over eight and the value bar is redrawn at every rung as the finger passes it, and it does not move. Not a little, not smoothly, not at all: the fill lands on the guide line each time it is drawn, and the guide line was set by the very first rung. Run the finger back up and the numbers grow again and the bar is redrawn again, still on the line. Then the first rung and the last rung lift out of the ladder and settle side by side with their two bars drawn against each other, twenty over thirty-two beside five over eight, two fills ending in the same place. Nothing else on the stage moves and the voice says nothing at all while it happens.

**General miss line:** Open both factor lists again and look for a number standing in each of them.

## Answer key
A fraction is in its lowest terms when the only factor its two numbers share is one. To get there, divide the upper number and the lower number by the same common factor, and keep going until nothing but one is left in common. Both numbers must be divided, and by the same thing: dividing one of them alone changes how much the fraction is worth. Using the largest common factor finishes in a single step, and using a smaller one takes more steps and arrives in the same place. Reducing changes the way the fraction is written and never the amount it stands for, which is why the reduced fraction may be put in place of the one it came from.

## Student controls
- **Factor lists**: opens the factors of both numbers and lights the ones they share
- **Divider tray**: offers the shared factors as tiles that can be dropped on the fraction
- **Step down**: divides both numbers by the chosen factor and writes the next rung
- **Second ladder**: opens a parallel ladder from the same starting fraction
- **Value bar**: draws a strip filled to the amount of the fraction and redraws it at every rung
- **Done light**: comes up when the two numbers share no factor but one
- **Undo a rung**: climbs back one rung with the working left in place

## Textbook (this topic)
**Pages:** 75, 76
**Key terms:** lowest terms, common factor, cancel, reduce
A fraction is reduced by dividing both numbers by a common factor until only one is left in common. Four goes into both, giving four fifths. You must divide the top and the bottom by the same number. Divide only one and you have changed the amount, not just the writing. Because four and five share no factor except one. That is what lowest terms means. List the factors of both and look for anything bigger than one they have in common. Yes, both reach one half. Dividing by the biggest common factor finishes in one step. Dividing by a smaller one takes more steps and lands in the same place. No, they are the same amount written two ways. Reducing tidies the writing and leaves the value alone. That is why you may replace one with the other at any point.
