## What it is
**Description:** Send a pair of fractions down a rail with two gates, and park the pairs that neither gate will take.
**Why:** Find out which pairs of fractions each of this chapter's two rules can settle, and what to do with a pair that neither one reaches.
**Look:** A rail running across the stage with two named gates on it, a parking bay at its end and a line-up board underneath.
**Context:** Grade 4, and this is the topic where the printed pages promise more than they give. An adult sees two comparison rules. What is actually being taught is which pairs each rule reaches and which pairs neither reaches. The chapter's own headings promise a general method for unlike fractions and then deliver the shared-numerator case, so a child who trusts the heading will apply a rule where it does not hold. The rail refuses that move and gives the refusal somewhere to go.
**For the child:** Look at the pair and say what the two fractions share. If they share the bottom number, use the first gate. If they share the top number, use the second. If they share neither, send the pair to the bay. Parking a pair there is a real answer, not a miss.
**Teaching notes:** Peg fraction cards along a string by hand first, with nothing at one end and one whole at the other, and wait for two children to disagree. Then open the tool and make each gate say what it tests before it opens. The gates are named for what they compare, not for which number is bigger, because a class that learns them as rules about big and small numbers will apply the wrong one within a week. Say the shared-numerator rule for any pair that share a numerator rather than for unit fractions alone, and prove it on a pair where neither upper number is one, because the narrower version does not cover the chapter's own worked comparison. Then spend real time on the bay. Hand out a pair that shares nothing, let both gates refuse it, and accept the answer that it cannot be settled yet. There is a later method that rewrites both fractions over a common denominator, and it is not in this chapter at all, so do not reach for it and do not let a confident child reach for it either: a wrong rule applied fast is harder to undo than an honest pause. Finish on the fraction written over one, which the printed exercises set without preparing, and read it with the whole number reader as that many complete wholes.

## Phases (what each mount shows and does; the words are in tool.tsx)

### 1 · Same pieces, so count them
**Screen:** a pair whose lower numbers match waiting at the head of the rail
**Does:** sends the pair through the first gate and reads the two strips inside it
**Win:** the gate opens, the strips are drawn with the same cuts, and the fuller one is lifted out
**Feels:** the pieces are the same size on both strips, so there is only one thing left that can separate them
**Goal:** like fractions are compared by their upper numbers
**Observe:** Each gate said what it tested before it would open, and neither label mentioned a bigger or a smaller number.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- compares the two lower numbers, which are identical and cannot decide anything. → the gate opens and draws both strips cut the same number of times, with the eleven cuts counted out along each and printed as equal. The two lower numbers are then lit together and read out as the same number, so nothing about them separates the pair, and the two upper numbers light next with the filled parts counted along each strip. The child's answer stays in the slot and both strips stay drawn.
- adds the two numbers of each fraction together and compares the totals. → the child's two totals are printed where they were worked out and are not crossed through, because on this pair they happen to agree with the strips. The rail then loads three quarters against three eighths and runs the same move on it: the totals come to seven and eleven, so the move picks three eighths, and the second gate is opened on the same pair with both strips cut and filled, where three quarters plainly reaches further. Both runs are left standing together.
**Misconception to break:** Comparing the lower numbers, which are the same and decide nothing here.

### 2 · Same count, so size the pieces
**Screen:** a pair whose upper numbers match, refused by the first gate
**Does:** sends the pair on to the second gate and compares the two strips
**Win:** the second gate opens and the strip cut into fewer parts is shown reaching further
**Feels:** the same number of pieces taken both times, so the only thing left to decide it is how big a piece is
**Goal:** fractions sharing an upper number are compared by their lower numbers
**Observe:** Inside the second gate the strip cut into fewer parts reached further, and it did that whether the shared upper number was one or three.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- picks the fraction with the larger lower number, choosing 5/12 over 5/6 because twelve is more than six. → the gate opens and cuts both strips from the same left edge, one into six parts and one into twelve, with a single part of each lifted out and laid side by side so the difference in piece size is the thing on the stage. Five parts are then filled on each strip, one at a time and in step, and the run of five bigger pieces stops further along. The child's choice stays in the slot and both strips stay drawn.
- refuses the pair 3/4 against 3/8 at the second gate because neither of its upper numbers is one, and sends it on to the bay. → the bay hands the pair straight back and prints why, because the bay takes only pairs that share neither number and this pair shares its upper number. The second gate's label is read out as it is printed, a sentence about a shared upper number with no mention of what that number has to be, and the gate then opens on the pair and cuts both strips, three parts filled on each. The child's send is left drawn as a faint line along the rail.
**Misconception to break:** Choosing 5/12 because twelve is the larger number.

### 3 · Putting a list in order
**Screen:** four fractions on the line-up board with the direction set to ascending
**Does:** finds what the whole set shares, then slides the cards into order
**Win:** the cards settle in order, the direction label is read aloud and the strips beside them grow the same way
**Feels:** one list has its bottom numbers running downwards while the fractions themselves run up
**Goal:** a set is ordered by whatever its members share
**Observe:** In one ascending list the denominators came out running downwards.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- orders a set of like fractions by their lower numbers, which are all identical and carry no information at all. → the four cards are laid in a row with their lower numbers lit together and read out as one number repeated four times, and the board will not accept an order built on them. The upper numbers light next and a small strip is drawn under each card, filled from its own fraction, so the four strips grow in the same direction as the four upper numbers. The child's arrangement stays on the board while the strips are drawn beneath it.
- puts a shared-numerator set in order by writing its lower numbers out in increasing order, and hands in the reverse of what was asked. → a strip is drawn under every card, all of one length, each cut into its own lower number and filled with six parts, and the four strips are stood in the order the child chose. The fills plainly shorten from left to right while the direction switch reads ascending, and the switch is lit rather than moved. The child's order stays on the board and the strips stay drawn beneath it.
**Misconception to break:** Ordering by the lower numbers, which are identical and carry no information.

### 4 · Pairs these two rules cannot settle
**Screen:** a pair sharing no number at all rolling past both gates
**Does:** tries each gate, sees both refuse, and sends the pair to the bay
**Win:** the pair parks in the not yet bay with the reason printed beside it and nothing guessed
**Feels:** both gates look at this pair and neither of them is asking about anything the two fractions have
**Goal:** a pair sharing neither number cannot be settled by this chapter
**Observe:** Some pairs rolled past both gates and came to rest in the bay with a reason printed beside them.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- settles the pair anyway on its lower numbers, picking 3/7 over 5/8 because seven is the smaller of the two. → the pair is sent back to the head of the rail and each gate says again what it tests, with the two numbers it would have compared lit on both fractions: the lower numbers are seven and eight and do not match, the upper numbers are three and five and do not match either. Neither gate opens and no strips are drawn, because there is no rule here to draw them for. The pair rolls on into the bay with its reason printed, and the child's verdict stays in the slot beside it, unmarked.
- sends a pair that one of the two gates does take to the bay, because the numbers look unfamiliar. → the bay hands the pair back and prints the test it failed to fail: it takes only pairs that share neither number, and 9/1 against 9/4 shares its upper number. The second gate then opens on the pair, cuts both strips and fills nine parts on each, and the whole number reader draws the one written underneath as nine complete circles in green. The child's send is left drawn as a faint line along the rail and nothing is marked.
**Misconception to break:** Guessing from the lower numbers alone, as though the shared-numerator rule applied here.

## Delight
The second gate, the moment it opens on three quarters against three eighths. The two strips do not appear side by side already filled. They are cut while a hand watches, the upper one into four parts and the lower one into eight, from the same left edge and to the same length, and then three parts are filled on each, one at a time and in step, so the same count is plainly being taken both times. The two fills stop in different places, and a measuring line drops from the longer one down to the shorter to say how much further. Neither shared upper number is one, which is the point of running it on this pair: the gate is not a rule about halves and quarters and thirds, it is a rule about taking the same number of pieces out of wholes that were cut differently. Nothing else on the rail moves and the voice says nothing at all while it happens.

**General miss line:** Send it back and read each gate's label out. Every gate says what it tests.

## Answer key
Two rules, and only two. When two fractions have the same lower number they are like fractions, the parts are the same size, and the one with the larger upper number is the larger fraction. When two fractions share an upper number the same count of parts is being taken either way, so the one cut into fewer parts wins: for any pair that share a numerator, the smaller denominator gives the larger fraction, whether or not the shared upper number happens to be one. A fraction written over one is simply the whole number above the line. To order a set, first find what the whole set shares, then use the matching rule. If a pair shares neither of its numbers, neither rule reaches it and the correct answer is that it cannot be settled with what this chapter teaches. A later method rewrites such a pair over a common denominator, and that method is not taught anywhere in these pages, so the pair parks in the not yet bay and is left there.

## Student controls
- **Load a pair**: puts two written fractions at the head of the rail
- **Same bottom gate**: opens for a pair whose lower numbers match and compares their upper numbers
- **Same top gate**: opens for a pair whose upper numbers match and compares their lower numbers
- **Not yet bay**: parks a pair that neither gate takes, with the reason printed beside it
- **Strip view**: draws both fractions as strips inside whichever gate opened
- **Line-up board**: orders three or more fractions, ascending or descending
- **Whole number reader**: draws a fraction written over one as that many complete wholes

## Textbook (this topic)
**Pages:** 81, 82
**Key terms:** like fractions, same numerator, ascending, descending
Fractions sharing a denominator are compared by numerator, and fractions sharing a numerator by denominator. If two fractions share a denominator, the one with the bigger numerator is more. If they share a numerator, the one with the smaller denominator is more, because its pieces are bigger. A pair that shares neither needs a method you have not met yet. The denominator, so the pieces are the same size. Then the one with more pieces is bigger, and five ninths wins. Comparing like fractions is just comparing the top numbers. So the piece size decides it. Quarters are bigger than eighths, because the whole was cut into fewer parts. Three quarters is the bigger amount, and the smaller denominator wins. Check first what they share. If they share a denominator, order them by the top numbers. If they share a numerator, order them by the bottom numbers, largest denominator first. Ascending goes up and descending goes down. Neither, because they share no number at all. The two rules here need a shared top or a shared bottom. This pair needs a method you have not met yet, and saying so is the right answer for now.
