## What it is
**Description:** Sort measures into length, weight and capacity, see why handspans disagree, lay a thing on a ruler from its zero mark, and pick a unit before estimating.
**Why:** Sort each measure into its kind and its units, find out why handspans disagree, measure from the zero mark, and choose a unit before guessing a size.
**Look:** A workbench: a tray of everyday things and three bins at the left, two hands at a bench top in the middle, and a ruler with no 0 at the right.
**Context:** Grade 4, the first working topic of the measures chapter. An adult sees things sorted and a ruler read, which a real ruler does perfectly well. What is actually being settled is which kind of measure a thing needs, why everyone must use the same unit, and where a measurement starts. The page tells the child to put one end at the zero mark. The ruler it prints carries no 0: its first mark is labelled with the unit, and its numbers begin at one. A child looking for a 0 starts at the one and gets a length one centimetre too long.
**For the child:** Put each thing in the bin for its kind. Give it a unit from that bin. Lay the object's end on the first mark. That mark is the zero mark. Read the other end. Pick a unit, then guess, then measure.
**Teaching notes:** Measure one desk in handspans before the tool is opened, with a large hand and a small one, and let the class argue over the two counts before a tape settles them. That is why standard units are used. Then hand out real rulers and ask where the 0 is. The page says to lay one end at the zero mark, and the ruler it draws prints no 0: its first mark carries the unit's name and its numbers start at one. Say that the first mark is the zero mark wherever a ruler leaves the 0 off. Then have a child lay an eraser from the mark for one and see the reading come out a centimetre too long. The recap on the opening page asks for a unit in each of fifteen sentences. Its list leaves out millimetres, and three of its sizes are poor guides: a door four metres high, a pencil four centimetres long and a carton of cricket balls weighing thirty kilograms. The units are still the right ones; ask the class whether the sizes are. The chapter's first sentence says each higher unit is ten times bigger, which holds only for millimetres and centimetres, so do not repeat it here. The next topic gives each step its own number. The estimate table asks for a guess and then a measure. Write both, and ask how close the guess was rather than whether it was right.

## Phases (what each mount shows and does; the words are in tool.tsx)

### 1 · A unit for every kind of measure
**Screen:** the tray of things, the three bins, and the unit cards in a row
**Does:** drops each thing in the bin for the measure asked, then gives it a unit card from that bin
**Win:** every thing settles in its bin wearing a unit of that kind, and a card of the wrong kind will not stick
**Feels:** each kind of measure has its own family of units, and a card from the wrong family will not stay on
**Goal:** length, weight and capacity each have their own units, and a thing's measure takes a unit of its own kind
**Observe:** The kilograms card slid off the rope and went home to the weight bin.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- puts the bottle of water in the length bin, because the bottle is tall and its height is easy to see. → the question's words, how much water it holds, light under the slot. The length bin's four cards are held up against those words one at a time, and none of them can say an amount of water; the capacity bin's two cards, millilitres and litres, then light beside it. The bottle stays in the bin where the child set it, nothing marked.
- gives the rope a kilograms card, reaching for the unit heard most often at the shop. → the card slides off the rope and home to the weight bin's row, and the length bin's label lights with its four cards. The tape then runs out along the rope, reading it in centimetres, a length's own unit, and the child's card stays lit in the weight bin's row, nothing marked.
**Misconception to break:** Giving a measure a unit of the wrong kind, such as writing the bag's weight in centimetres or the milk in metres.

### 2 · Why everyone uses the same units
**Screen:** one bench top with a large hand and a small hand beside it, and the centimetre tape coiled at one end
**Does:** stamps handspans along the bench with each hand in turn, counts each, then runs the tape along it
**Win:** the two handspan counts stand apart, and the tape gives one reading for both hands
**Feels:** the bench stayed the same, and only the hand measuring it changed the count
**Goal:** handspans change from person to person, and a standard unit gives everyone the same reading
**Observe:** The small hand's row held more stamps than the large hand's, and the tape gave one reading under both.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- decides the child who counted nine handspans measured wrongly, because the other child counted only six for the same bench. → both hands stamp along the one bench, the large hand's six outlines above it and the small hand's nine below it, and both rows end exactly at the bench's right end. The tape then runs along the bench and reads one hundred and eight centimetres, the same under either row, and the child's choice stays lit on the word pad, nothing marked.
- tells a friend with a larger hand that a bench is nine handspans long, and expects the friend to make the same bench. → the large hand stamps nine of its own handspans along a fresh strip laid beside the bench, and the strip runs past the bench's end by three of the large hand's spans. The tape reads the bench, one hundred and eight centimetres, and then the strip, one hundred and sixty-two, and both readings stay on the stage with the child's answer beside them.
**Misconception to break:** Deciding that one child counted wrongly, or that the bench changed, when only the size of the hand changed.

### 3 · Measuring from the zero mark
**Screen:** the ruler with no numeral at its first mark, and an object lying with one end on the mark for one
**Does:** reads the other end, counts the centimetres the object covers, then slides it back to the first mark and reads again
**Win:** the zero mark lights under the object's end, and the reading matches the count
**Feels:** the object did not grow when it sat on the mark for one; only the reading did
**Goal:** a length is read from the zero mark, the first mark on the ruler, even where no 0 is printed
**Observe:** Sliding the object back to the zero mark made the reading drop by one, while the bracket's count stayed the same.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- reads nine for a crayon laid from the mark for one to the mark for nine, and gives nine centimetres as its length. → the bracket opens under the crayon and counts the centimetre spaces it covers one at a time, eight of them, and writes eight in sky blue beside the reading of nine. The crayon then slides back until its left end sits on the first tick, which lights vermilion as the zero mark, and its other end now sits on eight. The child's nine stays in the slot beside it.
- reads seven for a pencil laid from the zero mark whose far end lies on the short mark halfway between six and seven, giving the nearest printed number. → the short mark under the pencil's end lights vermilion, halfway between the long marks for six and seven, and the bracket counts six whole centimetre spaces and then half of the seventh, drawn as half a space. The measure is written six centimetres five millimetres, because half of the ten millimetres in a centimetre is five, and the child's seven stays in the slot beside it.
**Misconception to break:** Looking for a 0, not finding one, and laying the end on the mark for 1, which makes a length read one centimetre too long.

### 4 · Choosing a sensible unit
**Screen:** one thing on the bench, the unit picker offering three sizes, and the estimate and measure cards empty
**Does:** picks the unit that suits the thing, writes an estimate in it, then measures from the zero mark
**Win:** the estimate and the measure stand side by side, with the gap between them drawn as a short bar
**Feels:** the unit comes first, and a guess in that unit can then be held up against the measure
**Goal:** a unit is chosen to suit the size before measuring or estimating
**Observe:** Once a unit was picked to suit the thing, the estimate and the measure stood in the same unit, with the gap drawn between them.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- picks kilometres for the length of a classroom, because a classroom feels big to walk across. → the picker lays its pictures beside the classroom: the road between two towns for a kilometre, with the classroom a small dot on it, and one big step for a metre. A metre stick is laid along the drawn classroom and fits eight times, and the child's kilometres stay lit on the picker, nothing marked.
- writes the estimate after measuring, or rubs it out and writes the measure in its place, so the gap comes to nothing. → the estimate card locks once the measure card is filled, and a new number set on it is written on a fresh card beside the first, both kept. The gap bar is drawn from the first estimate, a pencil box guessed at twenty-five centimetres and measured at twenty-one, four centimetres, and the voice asks only how close it was.
**Misconception to break:** Picking centimetres because the number 10 is small, instead of asking how big the thing is.

## Delight
The third phase, the moment the object is slid from the mark for one back to the zero mark. The bracket under the ruler keeps its count, space for space, while both ends of the object glide one centimetre left together; the reading mark steps down one tick, the first tick lights vermilion, and the reading and the count come to the same number and hold. Slide it back to the mark for one and the reading climbs by one while the bracket's count does not move. It can be slid back and forth as often as a finger likes, nothing is marked, and the voice says nothing at all while it happens.

**General miss line:** Look at what is being measured, then at where the measuring starts.

## Answer key
Length is measured in millimetres, centimetres, metres and kilometres, weight in grams and kilograms, and capacity in millilitres and litres. A thing's measure takes a unit of its own kind: the length of a rope is never given in kilograms. Handspans, footsteps, cubits and paces change from person to person, so standard units are used, and they give everyone the same reading. A length is measured by laying one end of the object on the zero mark of the ruler and reading the mark at the other end. The zero mark is the first mark on the ruler, even where no 0 is printed there. A unit is chosen to suit the size before measuring or estimating: millimetres or centimetres for small things, metres for a room, kilometres between towns.
