## What it is
**Description:** Add and take away capacities in litres and millilitres, carrying or breaking a litre, answer in the question's words, and count the jars that fill a bottle.
**Why:** Add and take away capacities in litres and millilitres, answer what the question asks in its own words, and count the jars that fill a bottle.
**Look:** A tank at the left, a litres and millilitres frame in the middle, and a jar shelf with two bottles and an empty results table at the right.
**Context:** Grade 4, the chapter's closing topic, where the column work is done a third time and then put to use. An adult sees the weight columns again with new units. What is actually being taught is the question's own words. Example 17 asks how much water is left and ends by calling its answer the difference. Exercise 11.6 asks who sold more and how much more. The Critical Thinking box uses petrol, buys more and uses again, and in its second week the petrol has to be bought before it is used. Otherwise the tank would go below empty. The Math Lab prints its results for the one litre bottle before anyone pours, and this tool shows no count until the pouring is done. Apply Your Skills asks three questions about facts the chapter does not give; this tool answers none of them.
**For the child:** Fill all three millilitres boxes. Work the millilitres first. Carry or break a litre as a thousand. Answer in the question's words. Pour and count before you write.
**Teaching notes:** Before the tool is opened, have groups fill a one litre and a two litre bottle with jars of different sizes. Each group writes its counts in a table before anyone compares. ## Phases (what each mount shows and does; the words are in tool.tsx)

### 1 · Three places for millilitres
**Screen:** the frame with two capacities waiting, one with a two-digit number of millilitres
**Does:** types each capacity into the frame, filling every millilitres box, then flips the unit switch to see the same three boxes for grams and metres
**Win:** both rows stand with every millilitres box filled, and the three boxes stay under grams and metres too
**Feels:** a thousand joins each of these pairs, so the smaller unit keeps three boxes under every heading here
**Goal:** beside litres, millilitres take three places, as grams and metres do
**Observe:** Flipping the unit switch changed the headings to kilometres and metres and to kilograms and grams, and the three boxes stayed.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- types the fifty-seven millilitres into the first two millilitres boxes, so the row reads five hundred and seventy millilitres. → the row is read back aloud as it stands, five hundred and seventy millilitres, and a jug beside it is filled to that line, over half a litre, beside the card's own small measure. The five and the seven slide one box to the right, and a grey zero drops into the hundreds box at full size. The child's row stays drawn above the new one, nothing marked.
- after switching to kilometres and metres, types twelve kilometres seventy-five metres with the seventy-five in two boxes, as if the metres were centimetres beside metres. → the metres column keeps its three boxes under the new heading, and the seven and the five slide into the tens and the ones with a grey zero in the hundreds. The joining sign reads a thousand, the same as for litres, and the child's row stays drawn above the new one.
**Misconception to break:** Writing 40 mL under the hundreds and tens of 685, which turns it into 400 mL.

### 2 · Carrying and borrowing a litre
**Screen:** a sum whose millilitres come to more than a thousand, and a difference with too few millilitres on top

**Feels:** a litre is a thousand millilitres whichever way it travels, into the litres or out of them
**Goal:** a thousand millilitres are carried as one litre, and a litre is broken into a thousand millilitres to borrow
**Observe:** A full thousand millilitres rose as one litre in the sum, and a broken litre poured in as a thousand in the take-away.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**

- takes the smaller millilitres from the larger, four hundred and eighty less a hundred and twenty, and writes eighteen litres three hundred and sixty millilitres. → the millilitres column stays shut on the hundred and twenty on top, and the child's three hundred and sixty is written small beside it. ### 4 · How many small jars fill a bottle
**Screen:** the jar shelf, an empty bottle, and the results table with every cell blank
**Does:** chooses a jar, fills and pours it until the bottle is full, counting each pouring, and writes the count in the table
**Win:** the bottle fills, the count drops into its cell, and the next jar waits with its cell still empty
**Feels:** a small jar goes back and forth many times, and a big one needs only a few
**Goal:** a jar fills a bottle in a number of pourings, and a smaller jar needs more pourings
**Observe:** Each jar's count appeared only when its bottle was full, and the smaller jar's count was the larger.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- predicts that the larger jar will need more pourings to fill the bottle, because it holds more. → both jars are poured into the same bottle one after the other, each pouring a sky blue mark on the counter, and each count drops into its cell only when the bottle is full. The larger jar's cell holds the smaller count. The child's prediction stays written beside the table, nothing marked.
- writes a count in a cell before pouring, copying it from another group or guessing it. → the cell stays shut and the child's number waits beside it, written small. The jar is dipped and poured, each pouring marked on the counter, and when the bottle reaches its neck line the counted marks drop into the cell. Both numbers stay drawn, the child's beside the poured one.
- counts a mug of four hundred millilitres as filling a jug of one litre five hundred millilitres in four pourings, when the fourth pouring stops with water still in the mug. → three full pourings bring the jug to one thousand two hundred millilitres, and the fourth stops at the neck line with a hundred millilitres still drawn in the mug. The counter marks that pouring as part of a mug, and the mug is seen not to go into the jug exactly. The child's four stays beside the counter.
**Misconception to break:** Thinking a bigger glass needs more pourings because it holds more, or counting the 2 L as 2 glasses.

## Delight
The third phase, when the story has run to its end and the run control is pressed again. The tank plays the story backwards, each amount used pouring back in and each amount added lifting out, line by line up the story card, and the level climbs back to exactly the starting mark and stops there. The answer the child wrote stays on the card through it all. It can be run forwards and back as often as a finger likes, nothing is marked, and the voice says nothing at all while it happens.

**General miss line:** Read the question once more, and work the millilitres first.

## Answer key
Beside litres, millilitres take three places, as metres do beside kilometres and grams beside kilograms. Add or take away the millilitres first. Each full thousand millilitres is carried as one litre. When the millilitres on top are too few, one litre is broken into a thousand millilitres and the litres on top go down by one. An answer says what the question asks: how much is left, which is more and by how much, or the total, in the units the question uses. When a story uses and adds amounts in turn, work them in the order it gives. When a jar's capacity goes exactly into a bottle's, the pourings needed are the number of jars that make the bottle's capacity. For the same bottle, a smaller jar needs more pourings. Lengths, weights and capacities in two units are added and taken away the same way, each pair with its own joining number.
