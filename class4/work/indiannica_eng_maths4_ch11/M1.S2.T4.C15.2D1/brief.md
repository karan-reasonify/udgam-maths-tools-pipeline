## What it is
**Description:** Add and take away lengths in columns, the smaller unit on the right in fixed places, carrying or breaking one larger unit by the number that joins the two.
**Why:** Add and take away lengths in two units, writing the smaller unit in its own places and carrying or borrowing by the number that joins the units.
**Look:** A two-column frame with a unit switch and a joining sign above it, carry chips at its side, and length bars along the bottom.
**Context:** Grade 4, and the chapter's first adding and taking away of measures. An adult sees column addition and subtraction, which chapters 2 and 3 taught. What is actually being taught is that the columns now meet at a different number for each pair of units. Beside kilometres the metres take three places, and the page's Example 9 writes seventy-five metres as zero seven five in its table without a word about it. Example 10's Step 3 says the centimetres are regrouped into metres, when its own table breaks one metre into a hundred centimetres. One exercise item borrows ten millimetres for a centimetre, and another carries a metre for a hundred centimetres. Every worked regrouping is at a thousand, except one borrow at a hundred.
**For the child:** Put each unit in its own column. Fill every box of the smaller unit. Work the smaller unit first. Check the joining sign. Carry or break one larger unit by that number.
**Teaching notes:** Lay two tapes end to end on the floor before the tool is opened, one measured in metres and centimetres and one in metres, and measure the joined length. Then write the same sum in columns. Example 9 writes seventy-five metres as zero seven five in its table and no step says so; beside kilometres the metres take three places, so say it out loud every time. Example 10's Step 3 says the centimetres are regrouped into metres. Its table does the opposite: it breaks one metre into a hundred centimetres, so eighty-seven metres six centimetres becomes eighty-six metres one hundred and six centimetres. Say what the table does. The summary box names only a thousand metres to a kilometre. Exercise 11.2 then carries a metre for a hundred centimetres in one item and borrows ten millimetres for a centimetre in another, which no example shows. The page's own rule covers both: carry or borrow by the number that joins the two units. The Concept Linking box sends the child back to the opener's throws. Its second question has two differences, one metre eighty-eight centimetres and one metre eighty-seven centimetres, and the second needs a metre broken into a hundred centimetres. Its first question asks for a throw the page never prints, and the honest answer is that the page does not give it.

## Opening (first frame of every mount)
Every mount comes up already furnished for its phase with nothing in front of it. On the first phase the frame is set to kilometres and metres, with two lengths waiting beside it on cards, one of them with a two-digit number of metres. On the second two lengths already stand in the frame whose metres will add up past a thousand, the chip row empty. On the third the frame is set to metres and centimetres, holding a take-away whose centimetres on top are fewer than those below, the centimetres column shut. On the fourth the switch has moved to centimetres and millimetres, with more millimetres to take away than there are on top. The running count at the top left holds the columns worked against the columns in the frame, and the answer slot and the pad stand empty down the right. One thing moves, once: the short card, or the metres column, or the break control, or the joining sign, gives one slow pulse while the voice asks how many boxes the smaller unit takes. That pulse is the only invitation, and a finger on it is the whole way in.

## Phases (what each mount shows and does; the words are in tool.tsx)

### 1 · Lining up the units
**Screen:** the frame set to kilometres and metres, with two lengths waiting to go in, one with a two-digit number of metres
**Does:** types each length into the frame one digit to a box, putting zeros in front of the short metres
**Win:** both rows stand with every metres box filled and the zeros at full size
**Feels:** the metres get three boxes beside kilometres whatever their number, and a short number waits behind zeros
**Goal:** each unit has its own column, and beside kilometres the metres take three places
**Observe:** Six kilometres thirty-eight metres went in as six and zero three eight, the zero at full size.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- types the thirty-eight metres of six kilometres thirty-eight metres into the first two metres boxes, so the row reads six kilometres three hundred and eighty metres. → the row is read back aloud as it stands, three hundred and eighty metres, and its bar beside the card's is drawn three hundred and forty-two metres too long. The three and the eight then slide one box to the right, into the tens and the ones, and a grey zero drops into the hundreds box at full size. The child's row stays drawn above the new one, nothing marked.
- types six, three, eight into the three metres boxes and leaves the kilometres column empty, as if the whole length were metres. → the row is drawn as a bar under the card's bar, six hundred and thirty-eight metres, less than one kilometre beside more than six. The six then moves across into the kilometres column in deep blue, and the thirty-eight go into the metres boxes with a zero in front. The child's row stays drawn beside the new one.
**Misconception to break:** Writing the 9 m under the hundreds of 640, which turns 9 m into 900 m.

### 2 · Carrying a whole unit
**Screen:** two lengths in the frame whose metres add up to more than a thousand
**Does:** adds the metres, carries a kilometre chip, writes what is left, then adds the kilometres with the chip
**Win:** the chip rides over the kilometres column, and the sum stands with fewer than a thousand metres
**Feels:** a thousand metres are too many for three boxes, so they climb over and become a kilometre
**Goal:** when the smaller unit adds up to the joining number or more, one larger unit is carried for each full joining number
**Observe:** When the metres passed a thousand, one kilometre chip rose over the kilometres and only what was left went down.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- writes all of one thousand two hundred and twenty-three in the metres column, and gets sixty-five kilometres and a four-digit number of metres. → the carry box shows one thousand two hundred and twenty-three metres, and a thousand of them gather into one amber kilometre chip that rises into the chip row, leaving two hundred and twenty-three below. The child's four digits are laid over the three metres boxes and the first has no box to stand in. The kilometres are added again with the chip, and the child's answer stays in the slot.
- carries twelve kilometres and writes twenty-three metres, splitting the sum at a hundred as for centimetres. → the joining sign lights, a thousand, and the length bars lay the sum's metres out: one full kilometre and two hundred and twenty-three metres more. Twelve kilometres would need twelve thousand metres, and the bars hold only one kilometre's worth. One chip rises, and the child's seventy-seven kilometres twenty-three metres stays in the slot beside the new sum.
- carries the kilometre but forgets to add it, and writes sixty-five kilometres two hundred and twenty-three metres. → the chip over the kilometres column glows amber, and the kilometres are added again with it travelling down into the sum: thirty-eight and twenty-seven make sixty-five, and one more makes sixty-six. The length bars show the child's answer a whole kilometre short, and it stays in the slot beside the new sum.
**Misconception to break:** Writing 1302 in the metres column, or carrying at a hundred as centimetres would.

### 3 · Borrowing a whole unit
**Screen:** the frame set to metres and centimetres, taking a length with more centimetres from one with fewer
**Does:** tries the centimetres column, then breaks one metre into a hundred centimetres and takes away
**Win:** one metre splits into a hundred centimetres, the metres go down by one, and the centimetres can be taken away
**Feels:** the metre does not vanish; it moves across the line as a hundred centimetres
**Goal:** when the smaller unit on top is too small, one larger unit is broken into the smaller units
**Observe:** The centimetres column stayed shut until a metre was broken, and then the metres on top showed one less.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- takes the smaller centimetres from the larger, forty less fifteen, and writes thirty-five metres twenty-five centimetres for sixty-three metres fifteen centimetres less twenty-eight metres forty centimetres. → the centimetres column stays shut on the fifteen on top, and the child's twenty-five is written small beside it. The break control then strikes through sixty-three and writes sixty-two above it in amber, and a metre chip splits into a hundred strips that join the fifteen, one hundred and fifteen. Forty is taken from the hundred and fifteen, and the child's answer stays in the slot.
- breaks one metre into a hundred centimetres but leaves the sixty-three metres as they were, and writes thirty-five metres seventy-five centimetres. → the sixty-three on top is lit with nothing struck through it, beside a hundred centimetres that came from nowhere, and the length bars show the top length grown by a whole metre. The sixty-three is then struck through and sixty-two written above it, the take-away runs again, and the child's answer stays in the slot a metre too long.
- types seven metres six centimetres with the six in the first centimetres box, as seven metres sixty centimetres, and takes three metres forty-eight centimetres to get four metres twelve centimetres. → the row is read back, seven metres sixty centimetres, and its bar is drawn fifty-four centimetres longer than the card's. The six slides into the second box and a grey zero fills the first, and the centimetres on top, six, are now fewer than forty-eight, so a metre is broken into a hundred centimetres. The answer comes to three metres fifty-eight centimetres, and the child's row stays drawn above.
**Misconception to break:** Taking 27 from 64 in the centimetres column because it is easier, which gives 37 cm and a wrong answer.

### 4 · The number depends on the units
**Screen:** the frame switched to centimetres and millimetres, with more millimetres to take away than there are on top
**Does:** breaks one centimetre, counts the millimetres it gives, and takes away
**Win:** the centimetre splits into ten millimetres, and the joining sign glows
**Feels:** the same break makes ten here and a hundred one pair up, because the joining sign says so
**Goal:** carry or borrow by the number that joins the two units: ten, a hundred or a thousand
**Observe:** Switched to centimetres and millimetres, the broken chip split into ten pieces, not a hundred.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- breaks the centimetre into a hundred millimetres, as a metre breaks into centimetres, and writes twenty-six centimetres ninety-seven millimetres. → the joining sign lights, ten, and the chip splits into exactly ten slivers that join the four on top, fourteen millimetres. The child's ninety-seven is laid over the one millimetres box and its second digit has no box to stand in. Seven is taken from fourteen, and the child's answer stays in the slot beside twenty-six centimetres seven millimetres.
- adds twenty-seven centimetres eight millimetres and forty-five centimetres five millimetres, and writes thirteen in the millimetres column for seventy-two centimetres thirteen millimetres. → the carry box shows thirteen millimetres, and ten of them gather into one amber centimetre chip that rises over the centimetres, leaving three below, while the joining sign glows, ten. The centimetres are added with the chip, seventy-three, and the child's answer stays in the slot beside seventy-three centimetres three millimetres.
**Misconception to break:** Breaking the centimetre into a hundred millimetres, as a metre breaks into centimetres, and writing 17 cm 95 mm.

## Delight
The fourth phase, when the unit switch is flipped while a chip waits on the break control. The chip splits in front of the child into exactly the joining number of pieces for the pair now set: ten slivers for a centimetre; flipped to metres and centimetres it gathers and splits again into a hundred strips; flipped to kilometres and metres it falls into a thousand metres, ten bars of a hundred. The joining sign changes with it each time, and the smaller column gains or loses boxes to match. It can be flipped as often as a finger likes, nothing is marked, and the voice says nothing at all while it happens.

**General miss line:** Read the joining sign again, and work the smaller column first.

## Answer key
Write each unit in its own column with the smaller unit on the right. Beside kilometres the metres take three places, beside metres the centimetres take two, and beside centimetres the millimetres take one. A smaller part too short for its places is written with zeros in front, so 75 m beside kilometres is 075. Add or take away the smaller unit first. When the smaller unit adds up to the number that joins the two units or more, carry one larger unit for each full joining number and write what is left. When the smaller unit on top is less than the one below it, break one larger unit into the joining number of smaller units. The larger units on top go down by one, and the smaller unit on top grows by the joining number. The joining number is ten for centimetres and millimetres, a hundred for metres and centimetres, and a thousand for kilometres and metres.

## Student controls
- **Unit switch**: sets the pair to kilometres and metres, metres and centimetres, or centimetres and millimetres
- **Column frame**: two headed columns, with fixed boxes for the smaller unit
- **Joining sign**: shows the number that joins the pair: a thousand, a hundred or ten
- **Digit boxes**: take one digit each, with zeros in front of a short smaller part
- **Carry chip**: one larger unit carried for each full joining number of the smaller unit
- **Break control**: splits one larger unit into the joining number of smaller units
- **Length bars**: draw each length as a bar, end to end for a sum and one along the other for a difference
