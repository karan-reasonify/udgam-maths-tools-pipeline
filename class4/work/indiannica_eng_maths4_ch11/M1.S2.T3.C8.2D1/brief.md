## What it is
**Description:** Step a length up and down a ladder of units, each step painted with its own number, keeping the remainder in the smaller unit and a zero in every empty place.
**Why:** Find the number that joins each pair of length units, and use it to change a length both ways without losing what is left or an empty place.
**Look:** A ladder of four rungs with a number painted on each step, a remainder slot on each rung, and a place board underneath.
**Context:** Grade 4, and the topic the rest of the chapter leans on. An adult sees numbers multiplied and divided by ten, a hundred and a thousand, which is chapter 5's work. What is actually being taught is which number joins which two units, and what to do with what is left. The chapter's first sentence says each higher unit is ten times bigger, which holds only for millimetres and centimetres. Its summary box says to multiply or divide by 10, 100 or 1000 without saying which goes where. A division that leaves a remainder is written as equal to a measure in two units, and the step that makes the answer is never said. The quotient is the larger unit, and the remainder stays as the smaller unit.
**For the child:** Find the number on each step. Step down to multiply. Step up to divide. The remainder stays in the smaller unit. Put a zero in any empty place.
**Teaching notes:** Lay a metre stick beside a centimetre ruler before the tool is opened and count how many centimetres it covers, then count the millimetres in one centimetre. The two numbers are different, and that is the whole topic. The page's first sentence says each higher unit is ten times bigger, which holds only for centimetres and millimetres; a child who believes it writes three metres as thirty centimetres. Give each step its own number every time: ten, a hundred, a thousand. The summary box says to multiply or divide by 10, 100, 1000 and so on without saying which goes where. So ask which two units are being joined before any number is used. The page writes a division that leaves a remainder as equal to a measure in two units, and never says how the answer is read. It is chapter 5's move for dividing by ten, a hundred and a thousand: the quotient is the larger unit and the remainder stays as the smaller unit. A child who divides and writes a decimal gets five point seven for five hundred and seventy centimetres and may read it as five metres seven centimetres. Example 4 starts from six point two five metres. Six point two five is six and twenty-five hundredths, and a hundredth of a metre is a centimetre, so it is six metres twenty-five centimetres. Ask nothing more with decimals. Exercise 11.1 has four kilometres sixty-six metres, which is four thousand and sixty-six metres, with a zero in the hundreds, and it asks for the cloth in metres and centimetres, both units. The opener's first question asks for a throw at an event the page gives no distance for. It cannot be answered from the page; say so, and supply no figure. Changing a throw the page does print into centimetres is the same work.

## Phases (what each mount shows and does; the words are in tool.tsx)

### 1 · Each step has its own number
**Screen:** the ladder with its three steps unpainted, three number tiles beside it, and the ruler, the metre stick and the road sign at their rungs
**Does:** sets each number tile on the step it belongs to, checking it against the ruler and the metre stick
**Win:** each step carries its own number, and ten sits only between millimetres and centimetres
**Feels:** each gap on the ladder is a different size, so each step carries a different number
**Goal:** ten millimetres make a centimetre, a hundred centimetres make a metre and a thousand metres make a kilometre
**Observe:** The ten tile would sit only on the lowest step, and the metre stick lined up with a hundred centimetres on the ruler.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- sets the ten tile on the step between centimetres and metres, and writes four metres as forty centimetres. → the tile slides back off the step, and the metre stick beside the metre rung lights, laid against the centimetre ruler: forty centimetres cover less than half of one stick, while four metres are four whole sticks laid end to end. The hundred tile then settles on that step, and the child's forty stays in the slot, nothing marked.
- sets the hundred tile on the step between metres and kilometres, and writes two kilometres as two hundred metres. → the tile slides back, and the road sign's kilometre is laid out beside the ladder in metre sticks: two hundred metres reach only a fifth of the way along the first kilometre. The thousand tile settles on the step, and the child's two hundred stays in the slot beside the road.
**Misconception to break:** Using ten for every step, as the page's first sentence does when it says each higher unit is ten times bigger; that holds only for centimetres and millimetres.

### 2 · Larger to smaller: multiply
**Screen:** a card in kilometres and metres on the kilometre rung
**Does:** steps the card down to the metre rung, multiplies the kilometres by the step's number and adds the metres
**Win:** the card lands on the metre rung with the kilometres turned into thousands of metres and the metres added on
**Feels:** the kilometres change their name as they step down, and the metres just come along
**Goal:** a larger unit becomes a smaller one by multiplying by the number that joins them, and the smaller part is added on
**Observe:** Stepping down, the kilometres became thousands of metres and the metres on the card were added on.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- multiplies the kilometres by a hundred and writes eight hundred and eighteen metres for five kilometres three hundred and eighteen metres. → the card is handed back to the kilometre rung with the step's own number, a thousand, lit on its panel. The step is taken again: five thousand metres drop down beside the three hundred and eighteen, and the card lands on the metre rung reading five thousand three hundred and eighteen. The child's eight hundred and eighteen stays in the slot, nothing marked.
- multiplies the kilometres correctly but leaves the metres behind, and writes five thousand metres. → the three hundred and eighteen metres stay lit on the kilometre rung after the card has stepped down, left behind. They then step down after it, already metres, and join the five thousand, and the card reads five thousand three hundred and eighteen. The child's five thousand stays in the slot beside it.
**Misconception to break:** Multiplying by a hundred and writing 700 m for 7 km, or forgetting to add the 245 m.

### 3 · Smaller to larger: divide and keep the rest
**Screen:** a card in centimetres alone on the centimetre rung, with an empty remainder slot beside it
**Does:** steps the card up, divides by a hundred, sends the quotient up as metres and drops the remainder in the slot
**Win:** the metres stand on the upper rung and the centimetres left sit in the slot, and together they rebuild the card
**Feels:** the whole metres climb away, and what cannot make a metre stays behind in centimetres
**Goal:** divide by the number that joins the units; the quotient is the larger unit and the remainder stays as the smaller unit
**Observe:** Stepping up, the whole metres climbed to the rung above and seventy centimetres stayed in the slot.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- divides five hundred and seventy by a hundred to get five point seven, and reads it as five metres seven centimetres. → the step up drops the remainder into the centimetres slot, seventy, and the quotient climbs to the metre rung, five. The child's five metres seven centimetres is rebuilt down the ladder beside it, five hundred and seven centimetres, which stands sixty-three short of the card's five hundred and seventy. Both stay drawn, and the child's answer stays in the slot.
- writes eight metres for eight hundred and forty-six centimetres and stops, leaving the remainder out. → the remainder slot on the centimetre rung holds forty-six in green, lit, beside the eight metres on the rung above. The rebuild control walks the eight metres back down, eight hundred centimetres, and lays them beside the card, forty-six centimetres short. The forty-six then join them and the card is whole again, the child's eight metres still in the slot.
- writes four kilometres six hundred and fifty metres for four thousand and sixty-five metres, pushing the sixty-five to the front of the three places. → the remainder slot writes sixty-five as a number, and the rebuild puts it back: four thousand metres and sixty-five metres make four thousand and sixty-five. The child's answer is rebuilt beside it, four thousand six hundred and fifty metres, five hundred and eighty-five too many, and both stay drawn.
**Misconception to break:** Dividing to get 5.7 and reading it as 5 m 7 cm. The remainder is 70 cm, not 7 cm.

### 4 · A zero that holds a place
**Screen:** a card in kilometres and a two-digit number of metres, and the place board with four empty boxes
**Does:** steps the card down and writes the answer on the place board, one digit to a box
**Win:** a zero drops into the empty hundreds box and the answer reads with every place filled
**Feels:** the empty place does not disappear; it waits for a zero to hold it open
**Goal:** when the smaller part does not fill its places, a zero holds each empty one
**Observe:** The metres filled only the tens and the ones, and a zero dropped into the empty hundreds box.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- writes eight thousand four hundred and seventy metres for eight kilometres forty-seven metres, putting the forty-seven straight after the eight. → the place board takes the eight thousands in its thousands box and the forty-seven in the tens and the ones, and the hundreds box stays empty and pulses until a grey zero drops into it at full size: eight thousand and forty-seven. The child's number is laid on a second board beside it, four hundred and twenty-three metres more, and both stay drawn.
- writes four metres sixty centimetres for four hundred and six centimetres, reading the zero as if it came after the six. → the step up drops the remainder into the centimetres slot as a number, six, and the four metres climb. The place board shows the card's zero in its tens box, holding that place, and the child's four metres sixty centimetres is rebuilt beside it, four hundred and sixty centimetres, fifty-four too many.
**Misconception to break:** Writing 38 straight after the 6 to get 6380 m, with no zero holding the hundreds.

## Delight
The third phase, the moment the rebuild control is pressed after a step up. The whole metres on the upper rung step back down one at a time, each unrolling into a strip of a hundred centimetres that lays itself along the lower rung, and the remainder slides out of its slot and joins the end of the row. The card that began the phase is written again beside the answer, digit for digit. It can be stepped up and rebuilt as often as a finger likes, nothing is marked, and the voice says nothing at all while it happens.

**General miss line:** Find the step you are crossing, and read the number painted on it.

## Answer key
Ten millimetres make a centimetre, a hundred centimetres make a metre, and a thousand metres make a kilometre. So each step between units has its own number, and ten is the number only between centimetres and millimetres. To change a measure from a larger unit to a smaller one, multiply the larger part by the number that joins the two units and add the smaller part. To change a measure in a smaller unit into the larger and smaller units, divide by the number that joins them. The quotient is the larger unit and the remainder stays as the smaller unit. The smaller part fills the last places of the answer, three beside kilometres, two beside metres and one beside centimetres, and a zero holds any of those places it leaves empty. An answer asked for in two units gives both.

## Student controls
- **Ladder**: four rungs from millimetres to kilometres, each step painted with its own number
- **Step down**: multiplies the larger part by the step's number and adds the smaller part
- **Step up**: divides by the step's number, the quotient climbing to the larger unit
- **Remainder slot**: holds what is left after dividing, labelled with the smaller unit
- **Place board**: shows the answer in thousands, hundreds, tens and ones, with a zero in every empty place
- **Measure card**: a length in one unit or two, set on its rung
