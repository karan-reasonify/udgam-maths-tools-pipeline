## What it is
**Description:** See that a hundred paise make one rupee, read the dot that separates rupees from paise, write an amount in paise alone, and compare two amounts once both are in the same unit.
**Why:** Break a rupee into a hundred paise, read the dot on a price tag, write any amount in paise alone, and compare two amounts once both are in paise.
**Look:** A rupee tray and a paise tray side by side under a price tag, a paise meter below them, and two tags with a sign slot between them at the right.
**Context:** Grade 4, the first working topic of the money chapter, built on the recap exercise that opens it. An adult sees rupees turned into paise, which looks like Class 3 revision. What is actually being settled is the one fact that every later topic with paise in it leans on: a hundred paise make one rupee. The chapter never states it, though its recap cannot be done without it and its first subtraction example breaks a rupee by it. Paise here are an amount on a price tag and never a coin, so the tool shows them as cells of a rupee strip and not as small change.
**For the child:** Break a rupee into a hundred paise. Read the tag: rupees before the dot, paise after it. Turn rupees into paise to write an amount in paise. Put both amounts in paise before you compare.
**Teaching notes:** Hand round play rupee coins and a sheet of paper strips ruled into a hundred cells before the tool is opened, and swap a coin for a full strip by hand. Say the fact first, in these words: a hundred paise make one rupee. The chapter never states it, yet its recap cannot be done without it and its first subtraction example breaks a rupee by it. It is Class 3 knowledge, and every later topic with paise in it uses it. Paise are an amount on a price tag, not a coin in a purse, so no small coin appears here, only cells on a strip. When a tag has fewer than ten paise, the first place after the dot holds a zero, because the paise always take two places. The recap's comparing question sets two pairs whose amounts come out the same, and there the equals sign is the answer. Put both amounts into paise before any sign is chosen.

## Phases (what each mount shows and does; the words are in tool.tsx)

### 1 · A hundred paise make a rupee
**Screen:** a few rupee coins in the rupee tray, an empty paise tray, and the paise meter reading nothing
**Does:** breaks one rupee into a full strip of cells, watches the meter climb, breaks the rest, then says how many paise the rupees made
**Win:** each strip fills with a hundred cells, and the meter climbs by exactly one hundred every time
**Feels:** every rupee opens into the same long strip, and the meter jumps a whole hundred for each one
**Goal:** a hundred paise make one rupee, so rupees become paise when multiplied by a hundred
**Observe:** The meter climbed by exactly one hundred each time a rupee was opened.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- writes three hundred and sixty paise for Rs 36, as if ten paise made a rupee. → the break control opens the first of the thirty-six coins at walking pace, its strip filling block by block, and the meter climbs by one hundred, not by ten. Two more coins open the same way, and the child's three hundred and sixty is shown on the meter's scale: it falls sixty past the third hundred, three rupees and sixty paise, with thirty-three coins still waiting in the tray. The child's number stays in the slot, nothing marked.
- answers thirty-six, the number the question began with, and writes it as paise. → thirty-six cells are shaded on one strip in the paise tray, less than half of it, beside the thirty-six coins still standing in their rows. One coin is then opened beside them, and its strip alone runs far past all thirty-six cells. The unit tag under the slot lights paise, and the child's thirty-six stays written in it.
**Misconception to break:** Writing 360 paise, as if ten paise made a rupee, or leaving the answer as 36.

### 2 · The dot between rupees and paise
**Screen:** a price tag with a dot in it, and the two trays empty
**Does:** builds the tag's amount, rupees into the rupee tray and shaded cells into the paise tray, then writes a tag for fewer than ten paise
**Win:** the trays match the tag, and a small number of paise settles with a zero in the first place after the dot
**Feels:** the two places after the dot hold one number of paise between them, and a small one needs a zero in front
**Goal:** in an amount written with a dot, rupees come before the dot and the paise take the two places after it
**Observe:** Every tag had two paise boxes, and eight paise filled the first one with a zero.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- reads Rs 40.25 as forty rupees and two paise, taking only the first place after the dot as the paise. → the trays build the child's reading, forty rupees in the rupee tray and two cells shaded on a strip, and the tag it makes is written beside the question as Rs 40.02, with a zero in the first place. The two boxes of the question's tag then light together as one number, twenty-five, and twenty-five cells shade. Both readings stay built side by side, nothing marked.
- reads Rs 40.25 as four thousand and twenty-five rupees, reading straight past the dot. → the dot on the tag glows vermilion and the digits part at it: the forty before it goes to the rupee tray as forty coins and the twenty-five after it to the paise tray as twenty-five cells. The break control then opens the forty coins one after another, and the meter climbs to four thousand and twenty-five paise: the child's digits, but a number of paise, a hundred times less than four thousand and twenty-five rupees. The child's reading stays in the slot.
- writes sixty-four rupees and eight paise as Rs 64.8, leaving the second place after the dot empty. → the tag keeps both paise boxes drawn, and with the eight in the first box and the second left open, the strip shades eighty cells, which is what Rs 64.8 would say. A zero is then set in the first box and the eight moves to the second, and the shading falls back to eight cells. Both tags stand side by side, and the child's is still written in the slot.
**Misconception to break:** Reading 08 as 80 paise, or writing 64 rupees and 8 paise as Rs 64.8, which reads as 80 paise.

### 3 · An amount in paise alone
**Screen:** a filled board, some rupees and one part-shaded strip, with the paise meter covered
**Does:** says how many paise the rupees make, adds the paise on the strip, then uncovers the meter, and matches amounts written as sums to the same amounts written with a dot
**Win:** the meter uncovers on the child's number, and each matched pair of tags is joined
**Feels:** the rupees turn into hundreds, and the paise just join on at the end
**Goal:** an amount is written in paise alone by turning the rupees into hundreds of paise and adding the paise
**Observe:** When the flap lifted, the meter stopped on a hundred for every rupee with the strip's paise added on.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- adds fifty-three and forty and writes ninety-three paise for Rs 53.40. → the flap lifts and every coin opens into its strip in one sweep, fifty-three strips and forty cells more, and the meter climbs past five thousand and stops at five thousand three hundred and forty. The child's ninety-three is shaded as ninety-three cells on one strip beside the stack, less than a single rupee, and stays in the slot.
- drops the last zero and writes five hundred and thirty-four paise. → the child's five hundred and thirty-four is built in the trays beside the question's amount: five strips and thirty-four cells, five rupees and thirty-four paise, set against fifty-three rupees and forty paise. The meter reads the two amounts one after the other, and the child's number stays in the slot.
**Misconception to break:** Adding 53 and 40 to get 93 paise, or dropping the last zero and writing 534 paise.

### 4 · Comparing two amounts
**Screen:** two tags side by side, one in rupees and one in paise alone, with the sign slot empty
**Does:** flips the same-unit switch to write both tags in paise, compares the two numbers, and sets the sign
**Win:** both tags show paise, and the sign drops into the slot the right way round
**Feels:** the sign cannot be chosen until both amounts count the same thing
**Goal:** two amounts are compared once both are in the same unit, and equal amounts take the equals sign
**Observe:** The sign slot stayed shut until both tags were in the same unit.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- sets greater than after ninety rupees, against nine thousand two hundred and thirty paise, because rupees are worth more than paise. → the sign slot stays shut, and the child's sign waits beside it. The same-unit switch then rewrites ninety rupees as nine thousand paise, and the two tags stand in one unit, nine thousand against nine thousand two hundred and thirty. The slot opens with the child's sign still waiting beside it, nothing marked.
- compares the bare numbers seventy and six thousand nine hundred and fifty, and decides the paise amount is bigger. → the slot stays shut and the switch rewrites seventy rupees as seven thousand paise, which stands above six thousand nine hundred and fifty. The same habit of comparing by the digits gives the right sign for ninety rupees against nine thousand two hundred and thirty paise, and both pairs are then set side by side in paise to show that it was luck there. The child's sign stays beside the slot.
**Misconception to break:** Comparing the bare numbers 70 and 6950 and deciding that the paise amount is bigger.

## Delight
The third phase, the moment the flap is lifted. Every rupee still in the rupee tray lifts in turn, left to right, crosses the gap and opens into its strip, the strips stacking in the paise tray while the meter races up a hundred at a time; the part-shaded strip is counted last, cell by cell, and the meter stops on one number and holds. The child's own number stands in the slot beside it the whole time. Dropping the flap sends the coins home again, and it can be lifted and dropped as often as a finger likes, nothing is marked, and the voice says nothing at all while it happens.

**General miss line:** Open one rupee again, and watch how far the meter climbs.

## Answer key
A hundred paise make one rupee, so an amount in rupees becomes paise when it is multiplied by a hundred, and every full hundred paise is one rupee. In an amount written with a dot, the rupees come before the dot and the paise take the two places after it, so eight paise are written with a zero before the eight. An amount in rupees and paise is written in paise alone by turning the rupees into hundreds of paise and adding the paise. An amount made up of several smaller amounts is worth their sum. Two amounts are compared once both are in the same unit, and when both come to the same number of paise they are equal and the equals sign goes between them.

## Student controls
- **Rupee tray**: holds rupees as notes and coins, each showing only its value
- **Paise tray**: holds paise as shaded cells on rupee strips of a hundred cells each
- **Break a rupee**: opens one rupee into a full strip of a hundred paise
- **Trade a full strip**: turns a full strip of a hundred paise back into one rupee
- **Price tag**: writes the amount with rupees before the dot and paise in two places after it
- **Paise meter**: counts the paise in the paise tray, the whole amount once every rupee is opened, and can be covered
- **Same-unit switch**: writes both compared tags in paise
- **Sign slot**: takes greater than, less than or equals between two tags in the same unit
