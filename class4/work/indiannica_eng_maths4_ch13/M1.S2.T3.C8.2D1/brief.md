## What it is
**Description:** Sort notes and coins by the value printed on them, count a group of one value by multiplying, add the groups to a total, and lay the total against a price or another amount.
**Why:** Sort notes and coins by their value, count each group by multiplying, add the groups, and decide whether the total is enough or which amount is more.
**Look:** A counting tray of value slots across the canvas, a heap of notes and coins above it, a sum rail along its foot, and a price card with an enough gauge at the right.
**Context:** Grade 4, the chapter's first section, on notes and coins. An adult sees notes and coins being sorted, which a table top does perfectly well. What is actually being settled is that money is counted by value and not by pieces: six coins can be worth less than one note, and many notes of one value are counted by multiplying before any groups are added. The printed exercise then asks whether some money is enough when it is exactly enough, and who has more money when both have the same. Exactly enough is enough, and when two amounts are equal neither is more. This tool shows every note and coin by its value alone.
**For the child:** Put each note and coin in the slot for its value. Multiply to count a group. Add the groups. Lay the total against the price.
**Teaching notes:** Tip a class bank of play notes and coins onto the desks before the tool is opened, sort it by value, and count each group aloud. Then use the tool for what the desk cannot hold still: each group's count and value written together, and a total laid against a price. Teach every note and coin by the value printed on it and nothing else. The chapter lists its notes and coins in a table of designs with a paragraph on which older ones are still accepted, and the opening box says who puts the notes out and whose picture they all carry. None of that is mathematics, some of it is out of date, and this plan does not repeat it. Two exercise questions need a word said. One asks whether a child carried enough money when the total is exactly the price: exactly enough is enough. The other asks which of two children had more money when both had the same: the answer is neither. The On Your Own box can be read three ways. Read it as the book's own answer does, one note each of five hundred and two hundred rupees and two notes each of one hundred and fifty rupees.

## Phases (what each mount shows and does; the words are in tool.tsx)

### 1 · The value of each note and coin
**Screen:** a small heap of notes and coins tipped out above the empty tray
**Does:** sorts every piece into the slot for its value, then sets one note beside a handful of coins and decides which is worth more
**Win:** every piece sits in its own slot, and the larger amount glows, on the note or on the coins
**Feels:** the printed value decides, and neither the number of pieces nor paper against metal does
**Goal:** each note and coin is worth the value printed on it, and more pieces need not mean more money
**Observe:** The six coins made a shorter bar on the gauge than the one note.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- chooses the purse with six coins of ten rupees over the one with a single note of one hundred, because it holds more pieces. → the six coins drop into the ten rupee slot and the note into the hundred rupee slot, and the group card under the coins reads six coins of ten rupees making sixty rupees. The gauge lays both lots as bars from one line, and the coins' bar stops well short of the note's. The child's choice stays lit on the word pad, nothing marked.
- says a note of ten rupees is worth more than a coin of twenty rupees, because it is a note. → both pieces drop into their slots, the coin into the slot marked twenty and the note into the slot marked ten, and their printed values light in deep blue. The gauge lays the coin's bar twice as long as the note's, and the child's choice stays on the word pad beside it.
**Misconception to break:** Choosing the purse with six coins because it holds more pieces.

### 2 · Counting a group of one note
**Screen:** one slot holding several notes of one value, with its group reader empty
**Does:** counts the notes, multiplies by their value, and writes the group's amount
**Win:** the reader writes the count times the value, and the group's amount glows under the slot
**Feels:** a group of one value is that value again and again, so the count says how many times
**Goal:** many notes of one value are counted by multiplying the number of notes by the value
**Observe:** The group reader wrote the count beside the value, and the amount matched laying the value down that many times.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- adds nine and fifty and writes fifty-nine rupees for nine notes of fifty. → the group reader lays the nine notes along the sum rail one at a time, each adding a bar of fifty, and the running total climbs fifty, one hundred, one hundred and fifty, and on to four hundred and fifty. The child's fifty-nine is drawn as a bar beside the rail and ends partway along the second note. The child's number stays in the slot.
- answers nine, the number of notes, when the question asks for the money. → the group card under the slot lights its count, nine, in sky blue, and the unit tag under the answer slot lights rupees in deep blue beside the child's nine. The reader then writes nine notes of fifty rupees, nine times fifty, make four hundred and fifty rupees, and the child's nine stays in the slot.
**Misconception to break:** Adding 9 and 50 to get 59, or answering 9 because there are nine notes.

### 3 · Adding the groups
**Screen:** three or four slots filled with groups of different values, and the sum rail empty
**Does:** reads each group's amount and slides the groups onto the sum rail one at a time
**Win:** the running total grows with each group and settles at the end of the rail
**Feels:** a group is only ready to add once its count has been turned into rupees
**Goal:** a total is found by working out each group's value first and then adding the groups
**Observe:** The sum rail took each group's amount, and would not take a count.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- adds one of each value, two hundred, twenty and five, and writes Rs 225, as if each group held one piece. → the group cards light their counts in sky blue, three, four and six, and each group slides onto the sum rail whole: six hundred, then eighty, then thirty, the total climbing to seven hundred and ten. The child's two hundred and twenty-five is laid as a bar beside the rail, ending partway along the first group, and the child's number stays in the slot.
- adds the counts, three, four and six, and writes thirteen rupees. → the sum rail refuses the sky blue counts: each one slides up to the rail and back to its card. The thirteen pieces are then lit in their slots, three notes of two hundred, four notes of twenty and six coins of five, three different values, and each group's amount goes onto the rail in deep blue instead. The child's thirteen stays in the slot beside the total.
**Misconception to break:** Adding one of each value, 200, 20 and 5, to get Rs 225, and forgetting how many there are of each.

### 4 · Is it enough?
**Screen:** a filled tray with a price card beside it, the enough gauge waiting, and a second tray ready
**Does:** finds the total, lays it against the price and reads the gauge, then sets two trays side by side and says which holds more
**Win:** the gauge settles on its reading, and two equal trays sit level with neither more
**Feels:** the gauge does not care how the money is made up, only how far its bar reaches
**Goal:** a total is compared with a price or with another amount, exactly enough is enough, and when two amounts are the same neither is more
**Observe:** When the two ends met, the gauge read exactly enough, and two equal trays read neither.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- says no, the money is not enough, because nothing will be left over after paying three hundred and fifty rupees. → the gauge lays the total, three hundred and fifty rupees, under the price's bar, and the two ends meet at the same mark with no gap and no overhang. The gauge's word settles on exactly enough, the key reading exactly enough lights on the pad, and the child's no stays where it was pressed.
- decides six pieces must be enough for a book of two hundred and fifty rupees, without finding their total. → the two notes of one hundred and the four coins of ten go into their slots, the rail adds two hundred and forty, and the gauge lays that bar under the price's. It stops ten rupees short of the price's end, the gap bracketed in near-black, and the child's answer stays on the pad beside it.
- names the child with sixteen notes of one hundred rupees as having more money than the child with eight notes of two hundred. → both trays fold out and the gauge lays two bars, one thousand six hundred rupees each, whose ends meet at the same mark. The gauge's word turns to neither, the key reading neither lights, and the child's choice stays on the pad beside it.
**Misconception to break:** Saying no because nothing will be left over, when exactly enough is enough.

## Delight
The fourth phase, when a tray of eight notes of two hundred rupees is set against a tray of sixteen notes of one hundred. The two bars grow on the gauge side by side and their ends meet exactly. Then the notes themselves move: each note of two hundred rupees slides out, and two notes of one hundred rupees from the other tray pair up beside it, eight pairs in a row with not one note left over on either side, and the gauge's word turns to neither. It can be tried with any two trays as often as a finger likes, nothing is marked, and the voice says nothing at all while it happens.

**General miss line:** Read the value on each piece again, and count the group, not the pile.

## Answer key
Every note and coin is worth the value printed on it, and money is counted by value, not by the number of pieces; a coin and a note of the same value are worth the same. Many notes or coins of one value are counted by multiplying the number of pieces by the value. The total of a mix is found by working out each group's value and adding the groups. A total is then compared with a price: less is short, the same is exactly enough, and more is more than enough, and exactly enough counts as enough. When two amounts are the same, neither is more.

## Student controls
- **Heap**: tips out a mix of notes and coins above the tray
- **Value slots**: take each piece only in the slot for its value
- **Group reader**: writes a slot's count, its value and what they make together
- **Sum rail**: adds the group amounts one at a time into a running total
- **Price card**: sets a price beside the tray
- **Second tray**: sets another amount beside the first to compare
- **Enough gauge**: reads short, exactly enough or more, or which tray holds more, and neither when the two are the same
