## What it is
**Description:** Cut a number at its point, name the two halves, and build the sentence that reads it aloud.
**Why:** Cut a number where its dot is, name what each side is doing, and build the sentence that reads the whole thing aloud.
**Look:** A number lying along a bar with its point standing between the halves, a sentence builder under it and a second reading lane below that.
**Context:** Grade 4, and this is where the writing of a decimal and the meaning of it become one skill. An adult sees a number being read out loud. What is actually being taught is that the last word of the spoken sentence names a place, and that the place name says how many digits the decimal part has to have. A child who has only ever read decimals digit by digit can say a number perfectly and still write five and ninety-six thousandths with two digits after the point.
**For the child:** Cut the number where the dot is. Name the part on each side. Read the whole number part first. Say and for the dot. Then count the digits after the dot and name the place the last one sits in.
**Teaching notes:** The chapter reads 32.198 out in full as thirty-two and one hundred ninety-eight thousandths, and then writes seventeen and three tenths back as 17.3. Those two together are the whole topic: one job run in each direction. Read the first one aloud before the tool is opened, slowly, and stop hard on the last word. Ask what would change if there were only two digits after the point, and let the class hear the last word change with it. Insist that the two readings are never mixed. Eight point sixty-four is the reading that hides the place, and it is the one that comes out of a class that has only ever read prices aloud. Both readings are allowed, but each one has to be said whole. Running the rule backwards is where the work is, so spend the time there. The place name is heard first and the digits second, which is the opposite of the order they get written in, and the empty places control is what keeps the gap visible while the digits are being laid in. A packet out of a kitchen cupboard is worth bringing in for the last of it. Every nutrition panel on one is decimals, read aloud by somebody every day with the place names left off, and asking for five of them written down is the quickest way to find out whether the reading has stuck.

## Phases (what each mount shows and does; the words are in tool.tsx)

### 1 · The point divides the number in two
**Screen:** a number lying along the bar with the point standing between its halves
**Does:** cuts the bar at the point and drops a label on each half
**Win:** both labels land, the left half lit as whole ones and the right half as pieces smaller than one
**Feels:** one dot, and everything on one side of it is whole things while everything on the other side is pieces
**Goal:** the two parts of a decimal are named and each is doing its own job
**Observe:** The place name slot would not take a name that did not match the digits counted after the point.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- reads the whole thing straight through as one long number and loses the dot somewhere in the middle. → the bar cuts itself at the point and the two halves slide apart along the bar with the standing divider left between them, and each half is read out on its own with its label dropping under it. The number the child said stays written in the slot, and a card carrying that number with no point in it at all is laid under the bar so the two can be read against each other.
- labels the right hand half as though its digits counted whole ones too, so a decimal part of three digits is read as hundreds. → the right hand half is carried down to the grid and the strip, where its digits are laid out as pieces smaller than one and take up less than a single whole between them. The child's label stays exactly where it was dropped, the whole number part is drawn out beside it in whole ones, and the two sit on the stage together at their real sizes.
**Misconception to break:** Reading the whole thing as one long number and losing the point somewhere in the middle of it.

### 2 · Saying the point out loud
**Screen:** the sentence builder with the joining slot empty between the two parts
**Does:** drops the joining word in, then runs the everyday lane and compares the two
**Win:** both lanes read the same number, one with the word and, one with the word point
**Feels:** two readings, both of them allowed, and each one has to be said all the way through
**Goal:** the point is read as the word and, with the everyday reading allowed beside it
**Observe:** Both lanes read the same number and neither of them was the correct one.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- says the word point and then reads the decimal part as one whole number, mixing the two readings into one. → both lanes run the number as it stands, the upper with the word and and the decimal part as one number, the lower with the word point and the digits singly, and the child's reading is written on a third line under them exactly as it was said. The place name that each proper reading ends on is lit, and the mixed line is left with nothing lit at its end.
- puts the word and in the middle of the whole number part as well, so the joining word turns up twice in one sentence. → the sentence is read back with each slot lighting as it is spoken, and the joining slot lights alone on the word and while the whole number part is read out of a single slot. The child's sentence stays standing underneath, with both of its ands drawn in, and the point on the bar is lit against the one that is doing that job.
**Misconception to break:** Saying eight point sixty-four, which mixes the two readings and hides which place the four is in.

### 3 · Naming the last digit's place
**Screen:** the sentence built as far as the decimal part, with the place name slot waiting
**Does:** counts the digits after the point, then chooses the place name for the last one
**Win:** the place name lands and the counted digits light from the point outwards to match it
**Feels:** the sentence ends on the place of the last digit, and the count that finds it starts at the dot
**Goal:** the last word of the sentence names the place the last digit sits in
**Observe:** The heard sentence gave its last word first and its digits second, which is the opposite of the writing order.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- names the place of the FIRST digit after the point and ends the sentence on tenths. → count the places runs from the point outwards with each digit lighting and being numbered as it is passed, and the count holds on the last digit at the far end. The child's name stays in the slot, the name the count calls for is offered from the tray beside it, and both names are left standing under the same number.
- counts the places from the left hand end of the whole number, so the count starts before the point. → the place chart slides in behind the number with the point column drawn in it, and the counting is run twice: once from the left hand end of the number, which stops in the whole number part, and once from the point outwards. Both runs stay drawn with their counts written above the digits, and the number is then given two digits in front of the point instead of one, where the two runs plainly part.
**Misconception to break:** Ending on tenths, because the first digit after the point is the one in the tenths place.

### 4 · Writing one down from what you hear
**Screen:** a spoken sentence in the hearing bay with its last word lit and the places drawn empty
**Does:** reads the place name, lays out the empty places, then fills them from the left
**Win:** every place is filled, with a zero taking the place the digits heard did not reach
**Feels:** the last word settles how many places there are before there is a single digit to put in them
**Goal:** the place name says how many digits the decimal part needs
**Observe:** A place the heard digits did not reach stayed drawn and open until a zero went into it.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- writes seven and eighty-five thousandths with two places after the point, giving seven point eight five. → the last word of the heard sentence lights and three empty places are drawn to the right of the point, and the child's two digits are walked into the two places furthest from the point, leaving the place nearest the point drawn open. The child's number stays written underneath, and the number the places call for is read back with its last digit in the thousandths place.
- fills the third place by hanging a zero on the right hand end of the eighty-five instead of on its left. → both numbers are built out to three places and read back by their last digits, the child's ending in the thousandths place with an eight standing in the tenths, and the proper one with the eight in the hundredths. A short arrow is drawn running inward from the point to the place that was actually empty, which is the one nearest the point and on the LEFT of the digits, and the two numbers stand together with the grey zero walked across into that place.
**Misconception to break:** Writing 7.85, which has two places and reads as seven and eighty-five hundredths.

## Delight
The third phase, when the place name lands. The name settles into the last slot and the digits to the right of the point light back the other way, one at a time from the point outwards, with the count drawn over each as it lights, and the last of them holds lit under the name that has just been chosen. Pull a digit off the end of the number and the whole thing runs again on its own: the count is one shorter, the name in the slot lifts out and the tray offers the name that now fits. The sentence can be lengthened and shortened as often as a finger likes, nothing is marked, and the voice says nothing at all while it happens.

**General miss line:** Count the digits after the dot again, and leave the last word until last.

## Answer key
A decimal has two parts separated by the point. The whole number part is on the left and counts whole ones; the decimal part is on the right and counts pieces smaller than one. To read one properly, read the whole number part as usual, say the word and for the point, read the decimal part as though it were a whole number, and then name the place the LAST digit sits in. The everyday reading says point instead of and and reads the digits after the point one at a time, and both readings are allowed. Writing one down from words runs the same rule backwards: the place name at the end says how many digits the decimal part must have, and a number too short for them is filled out with a zero in front of it.

## Student controls
- **Cut at the point**: separates the bar into its two parts and labels each one
- **Sentence builder**: takes the whole number part, the joining word, the decimal part and the place name in order
- **Count the places**: counts and lights the digits standing to the right of the point
- **Place name tray**: offers tenths, hundredths and thousandths for the last slot
- **Everyday lane**: runs the second reading, with point in the joining slot and the digits read singly
- **Hearing bay**: hands over a spoken sentence and takes the number written from it
- **Empty places**: shows the places still waiting when the digits heard do not fill them
- **Say it again**: reads the finished sentence back with its last word lit

## Textbook (this topic)
**Pages:** 88, 89
**Key terms:** whole number part, decimal part, and, place name
A decimal splits into a whole number part and a decimal part, and the chapter gives three rules for reading it. The point splits a decimal into a whole number part and a decimal part. To read it in the proper way, say and for the point, and end with the name of the last place. In the everyday way, say point and read each digit after it. The left side is the whole number part and counts whole ones. The right side is the decimal part and counts pieces smaller than one. The point marks where one stops and the other starts. As the word and. Read the whole part first, then say and, then read the decimal part. The everyday way is to say point instead and read the digits one by one. The name of the place the last digit sits in. With three digits after the point that is thousandths. Read the decimal part as a whole number, then name where it ends. Three, because thousandths is the third place. Ninety-six is only two digits, so a zero goes in front of it to fill the tenths place. The place name tells you how many to write.
