## What it is
**Description:** Read a weight exactly as the scale prints it, count the places that are really there, and build the fraction from them.
**Why:** Turn a decimal back into a fraction by counting the places that are printed, and find out what a zero on the end is doing there.
**Look:** A kitchen scale at the left with a printing display, a bench of three working steps beside it, and a balance beam under the two rows.
**Context:** Grade 4, the closing topic, and the one the whole chapter has been protecting. An adult sees a conversion done backwards. What is actually at stake is the difference between an amount and the way an amount is written. A weight printed as two point seven five zero kilograms has three decimal places by this chapter's own counting rule, so its denominator is a thousand. A child who tidies it first gets the same weight over a hundred, which is right about the weight and wrong about the question, and has no way at all of seeing the difference, because the two really are equal.
**For the child:** Read the number exactly as it is printed. Take the dot out to get the top number. Count the places after the dot, and count any zero on the end too. Write one and that many zeros underneath.
**Teaching notes:** Bring a kitchen scale if there is one, and let the class read the display exactly as it stands. The chapter's own worked example turns nine point two into 92/10, and that is the easy half of this topic. The hard half is the shopping question, where a weight is printed as two point seven five zero kilograms and becomes 2750/1000 because three decimal places are printed. Say it as two separate questions and ask them in that order. Is it the same amount? Yes, and the beam holds level. Is it the same writing? No, and the writing is what was asked for. A class that only ever hears the first question will tidy every number in sight, and a class that only ever hears the second will decide that the two weights are different, which is worse. Ask both, every time. The closing self assessment is six statements long, and it is worth running against the bench rather than in the abstract: a child who has built both rows can point at the work that answers each line. The same trap runs the other way earlier in the chapter, where an amount given as seventy hundredths wants two decimal places written out, and writing only one of them throws away the very place the question named.

## Phases (what each mount shows and does; the words are in tool.tsx)

### 1 · Dropping the point to get the top
**Screen:** a weight standing on the scale display with the first working step empty
**Does:** carries the weight to the bench and drops the point out of it
**Win:** the digits settle as the numerator with nothing else about them changed
**Feels:** the point comes out and the digits stay exactly as they were, in the same order and all of them
**Goal:** the numerator is the digits with the point taken out and nothing else changed
**Observe:** The numerator was the digits of the display with the point taken out, and nothing else about them changed.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- keeps the point in the numerator, so the top of the fraction is still a decimal. → the point is lifted out of the number along a drawn path and set down beside the bench, where it stays visible, and the digits close up into the numerator with none of them moving out of order. The child's version stays standing in the first step, and both are read out as rows of digits so that the only difference between them can be heard.
- writes only the digits from one side of the point, keeping the whole number part and dropping the rest. → the display is carried across again at walking pace with every digit lighting in turn from the left, and the digits settle into the numerator one at a time with the count of digits written above the row. The child's shorter numerator stays exactly where it was written, and the two rows are stood one above the other with the digits that went missing lit in the display.
**Misconception to break:** Dropping a digit along with the point, or writing the whole number part on its own.

### 2 · Building the bottom from the places
**Screen:** the numerator laid down with the denominator slot empty under it
**Does:** counts the decimal places and writes one and that many zeros
**Win:** the denominator settles with its zeros drawn in one at a time under the count
**Feels:** the bottom number is built rather than remembered, one zero for each place counted
**Goal:** the denominator is one followed by a zero for every decimal place
**Observe:** The zeros of the denominator were drawn in one at a time under the count of places.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- writes ten underneath whatever the number of places, because ten was the first denominator the chapter met. → the place counter is run again with each printed digit after the point lighting in turn and the count written under the row, and the third step then draws the zeros in one at a time under that count, stopping when they match. The child's denominator stays standing where it was written, and the built one is stood beside it with the count drawn above both.
- writes the number of places underneath instead of one followed by that many zeros, so two places give a two. → the count stays lit in the second step and the third step is walked through slowly: a one is written first, then a zero for each place, each zero landing under the place it belongs to. The child's denominator stays in the row, the built denominator is written beside it, and both fractions are read out with the size of the pieces each one is counting drawn underneath them.
**Misconception to break:** Writing ten underneath whatever the number of places, because ten was the first denominator met.

### 3 · A zero on the end still counts
**Screen:** a weight with a zero on its end, lit on the display
**Does:** counts the printed places with that last zero included and builds the denominator from them
**Win:** the count takes in the last zero and the denominator comes out one zero longer
**Feels:** the zero on the end is not spare, it is standing in a place and the counting reaches it
**Goal:** a printed zero on the end takes a decimal place of its own
**Observe:** The zero printed at the end of the weight lit in the counting like every other digit.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- rubs the zero off the end of the weight before counting, because it looks as though it is doing nothing. → the weight is carried back to the display and read again exactly as the scale printed it, with the last zero drawn at full strength and lit like every other digit, and the place counter runs from the point outwards taking it in. The child's shorter count stays written under the bench and both counts are left standing, with the denominator built again a zero at a time under the longer one.
- counts the printed zero as a place but leaves the denominator the length it would have been without it. → the count and the denominator are drawn one directly above the other with each zero lined up under the place it stands for, and the last place is left with nothing under it and held lit. The child's denominator stays exactly where it was written, the missing zero is walked into the row under the lit place, and both denominators are read out with the size of their pieces drawn underneath.
**Misconception to break:** Rubbing the last zero out before counting, because it looks as though it is doing nothing.

### 4 · The right amount can still be the wrong answer
**Screen:** both rows on the bench, the printed one above and the tidied one below it
**Does:** runs the tidy first control and reads the two fractions and the beam together
**Win:** the beam holds level while the two written forms stand plainly different beside it
**Feels:** the beam will not move, and the two written answers are still not the same answer
**Goal:** the tidied number gives the same amount and a different written answer
**Observe:** The beam held dead level while the panel beside it stood the two written forms plainly apart.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- decides the two answers cannot both be right and rubs one of them out. → nothing is rubbed out. Both fractions swing down on to the beam, one at each end, and the beam holds dead level and stays level, while the panel beside it writes the two forms one above the other with their places counted off and their lengths plainly different. Both rows of the bench stay standing above them for as long as anybody wants to look.
- tidies every weight in sight before converting it, as a habit, including an amount that was given in words as seventy hundredths. → the words are laid out along the bench with the last word lit, the two places that word asks for are drawn empty to the right of a point, and the child's tidied answer is walked into them, where it reaches only the place nearest the point and leaves the far one open. The beam still holds level under the two amounts, the panel still stands the two written forms apart, and both answers stay on the stage with the place that was thrown away left lit.
**Misconception to break:** Believing the two answers cannot both be right, when they are equal in amount and different in written form.

## Delight
The fourth phase, when tidy first is pressed. The weight lifts off the display a second time, sheds the zero from its end on the way across, and runs the three steps of the lower row on its own: the point drops out, the places count themselves one short of the row above, and the denominator is built a zero at a time under the shorter count. Then both fractions swing down on to the beam, one at each end, and the beam holds absolutely level and stays there, while beside it the panel writes the two forms one above the other with their places counted off and their lengths plainly different. Nothing tips, nothing is marked, and the voice says nothing while it happens.

**General miss line:** Carry it back to the scale and count the printed places again, every one of them.

## Answer key
To turn a decimal into a fraction, write the digits without the point and that is the numerator. Count the decimal places. Write one followed by that many zeros underneath. The count is of the places that are PRINTED, not of the places a tidy answer would have had. A weight printed as 2.750 has three decimal places, so it becomes 2750/1000. Tidying it to 2.75 first gives 275/100, which is the same weight and not the number the question was about. A trailing zero changes the written form and leaves the value alone, and this chapter is asking about the written form.

## Student controls
- **Scale display**: prints a weight exactly as it reads, any zero on the end included
- **Drop the point**: lays the digits down without the point as the numerator
- **Count the places**: counts the printed decimal places and lights each one, zeros included
- **Build the bottom**: writes one and then a zero for every counted place
- **Tidy first**: drops a zero off the end and runs the whole bench again in a second row
- **Balance beam**: holds the two amounts level, because they are equal
- **Written form panel**: stands the two written forms side by side, because they are not the same
- **Can do list**: carries six statements to be ticked against the work standing on the bench

## Textbook (this topic)
**Pages:** 92, 93, 94
**Key terms:** decimal places, numerator, denominator, trailing zero
A decimal becomes a fraction by dropping the point and writing one followed by as many zeros as there were places. Take the point out to get the top number. Count the digits after the point, and write one followed by that many zeros underneath. A zero on the end is printed, so it takes a place and counts. Ninety-two. Write the digits with the point taken out and nothing else changed. That is the whole of the first step. One followed by one zero, which is ten. Count the decimal places and write one followed by that many zeros. Two places gives a hundred and three gives a thousand. Three. The zero on the end is printed, so it takes a place. The denominator is a thousand. That zero changes nothing about the amount and everything about how it is written. You get two hundred and seventy-five over a hundred. That is the same amount, but the question asked about the number as printed. Convert first and tidy afterwards, if at all.
