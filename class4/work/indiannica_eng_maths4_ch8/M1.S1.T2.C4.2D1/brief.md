## What it is
**Description:** Cut one whole into ten equal parts, shade some of them, and write what is shaded two ways.
**Why:** Cut one whole into ten equal parts, shade some of them, and write what is shaded as a fraction and again with a point.
**Look:** A long strip lying across the stage with a cutter along it, a naming slot above it and two writing slots beside it.
**Context:** Grade 4, the first working topic of the decimals chapter. An adult sees a strip being shaded, which squared paper does perfectly well. What is actually being settled is that a tenth is a share of a named whole, and that the two ways of writing it say one amount and not two. A share with no whole behind it is the gap in the reasoning box later in the chapter, where three shares of somebody's spending are handed over and the amount being spent is never named at all.
**For the child:** Say what you are cutting before you cut it. Cut the whole into ten equal pieces. Shade the pieces you want. Write the shaded amount over ten, then write it again with a point. Put the zero in front of the point every time.
**Teaching notes:** Cut a real strip before the tool is opened, and cut one badly on purpose. A strip folded into ten pieces that are not the same size is the quickest way to settle what the word equal is doing in the rule, and it takes a minute. Then use the tool for the thing paper will not do: it refuses to cut anything until the child has said what is being cut. Keep that habit, because the reasoning box later in the chapter hands over three shares of somebody's spending and never says one tenth of what. The only amount printed beside them is a bank balance, which is what is left rather than what is being shared out, so a child who reaches for it is being led somewhere the question does not intend. Ask the class what is missing before anybody starts working, and let them find that it is simply not there. Insist on both writing slots every time. A child who writes only the fraction, or only the decimal, has learnt one form and not the tie between them, and the rest of the chapter is that tie run in both directions. Finish on the zero in front of the point, and say what it is holding rather than that it must be written. A child who writes it as a rule will drop it the first time they are hurrying.

## Phases (what each mount shows and does; the words are in tool.tsx)

### 1 · Splitting one into ten equal parts
**Screen:** an uncut strip with an empty naming slot above it and the cutter waiting
**Does:** names the whole, runs the cutter, then measures the pieces against one another
**Win:** ten pieces of one length settle along the strip and the measure control finds no difference
**Feels:** a tenth is a share of something, and the something has to be named before the share says anything
**Goal:** one whole cut into ten equal parts gives tenths, and the parts must match
**Observe:** The slots would not take a share until the strip above them had been given a name.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- cuts the strip into ten pieces by eye, counts ten of them, and calls any one of them a tenth. → the ten pieces lift off the strip and stand end on against the rule in the order they were cut, and the two that overhang the rest are left standing there with the bracket drawn between the longest and the shortest. The child's strip is left exactly as it was cut, a second strip is cut by the cutter beside it and measured the same way, and both sets of pieces stay standing on the rule together with nothing marked.
- shades some pieces and writes the share down while the naming slot above the strip is still empty. → both writing slots hand the answer back and stay empty, with the naming slot lit above the strip and the shaded pieces left exactly as the child shaded them. The voice then asks the question the reasoning box later in the chapter never asks, which is one tenth of what, and the slots stay shut until the strip has been called a ribbon, a chapati or a litre of milk.
**Misconception to break:** Counting ten pieces that are not the same size and still calling one of them a tenth.

### 2 · The same amount, written with a point
**Screen:** the cut strip with some pieces shaded and both writing slots empty
**Does:** writes the shaded amount in the fraction slot and then in the decimal slot
**Win:** both slots fill and a bar is drawn joining them, because they carry one amount
**Feels:** two ways of writing it down, one strip, and the same pieces coloured under both of them
**Goal:** a share is written over ten and with a point, and the two carry one amount
**Observe:** Ten pieces cut by eye stood at different heights on the rule, and ten cut properly stood level.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- writes the fraction, leaves the slot that takes the point empty, and reports the strip finished. → both slots stay on the stage with the child's fraction standing exactly where it was written and the second slot still empty, and the joining bar is drawn as a faint outline waiting to fill. The strip is read out again with the shaded pieces lighting one at a time, and the same count is asked for in the other slot with nothing cleared.
- takes four tenths and the decimal for it to be two different sizes, because one is written with two numbers and the other with one. → the two written forms are carried down to the strip together and laid under the very same four shaded pieces, neither of them shading anything more or anything less. The joining bar fills between them, and a second strip is drawn under the first and shaded to the child's other reading, which asks for pieces this strip does not have; both strips are left standing.
**Misconception to break:** Treating 4/10 and 0.4 as two different sizes because they look different on the line.

### 3 · Counting more than one tenth
**Screen:** a strip with several pieces shaded and the decimal slot waiting
**Does:** counts the shaded pieces and writes the digit that goes after the point
**Win:** the digit lands and the shaded pieces light one at a time as it is counted back
**Feels:** the digit after the point is a count of pieces, and nothing else on the line moves when it changes
**Goal:** the digit after the point counts how many tenths there are
**Observe:** The fraction and the decimal were written from the very same shaded pieces and the bar joined them.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- puts both of the fraction's numbers on the line, writing seven point one zero for seven pieces of ten. → the number the child wrote is carried to a fresh strip and read off it place by place. The seven lands in the ones column as seven whole strips, and seven whole strips are drawn out along the stage beside the single strip that was shaded. Both stand together, nothing is cleared, and the shaded strip is then read back with the count of pieces spoken into the tenths place on its own.
- counts the pieces that are not shaded, because the plain run of them is the longer one. → the shaded pieces light one at a time from the left with the count written above them as it climbs, and the plain run is left plain and uncounted. The child's digit stays in the slot, a copy of the strip is drawn underneath with the child's count shaded instead, and the two strips stand together with plainly different amounts coloured in.
**Misconception to break:** Writing seven tenths as 7.10 or as 10.7 by putting both numbers on the line.

### 4 · What the zero in front is for
**Screen:** a decimal written into the slot as a bare point and a digit, with nothing in front of it
**Does:** adds the zero, then reads the number aloud with the ones column lit
**Win:** the zero settles in the ones column and the column lights to show it is empty of whole ones
**Feels:** the zero is not filling a gap, it is holding a column open where there are no whole ones
**Goal:** the zero before the point holds the ones place open
**Observe:** The ones column in front of the point stayed drawn and open until something was put in it.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- leaves the ones place empty, because there are no whole ones to write in it. → the ones column stays drawn as an open box in front of the point and stays open, with the child's bare point and digit left exactly as they were entered. The number is then read out from that column rightwards, the reading stops on the open box, and the voice names the column the box belongs to. A grey zero is offered from the tray and the same reading is run again with it in place.
- writes the zero in and slides the digit along to make room, turning five tenths into five hundredths. → both readings are carried down to strips. Five pieces of ten are shaded under the first, and under the second the whole is cut into a hundred and five of those cells are shaded, so the two pictures stand side by side and are plainly ten times apart. The child's number stays exactly where it was written, and the grey zero is then walked into the ones column with the five held still in the tenths place the whole way.
**Misconception to break:** Leaving the ones place blank because nothing is there, and losing the point along with it.

## Delight
The first phase, the moment the measure is run on a strip that was cut by eye. The ten pieces lift off the strip together and stand themselves end on against the rule along the foot, in the order they were cut and not sorted, so the two that overhang the rest are plainly out of line; the bracket is drawn between the longest and the shortest with nothing written in it. Run the same measure on a strip the cutter settled, and the ten stand level, the bracket closes to nothing and the strip re-forms itself into one piece with the pieces sliding back into place. Both runs can be made as often as a finger likes, nothing is marked either way, and the voice says nothing at all while it happens.

**General miss line:** Stand the pieces against one another, then count the shaded ones again.

## Answer key
One whole cut into ten equal parts gives tenths, and the parts have to be the same size or none of them is a tenth. A share is written as a fraction over ten and also with a decimal point, and the two say the same amount. The digit after the point counts how many tenths. The zero before the point holds the ones place and says there are no whole ones, and it is written every time. A share of something is not an amount until the something has been named.

## Student controls
- **Name the whole**: asks what is being cut before the cutter will run
- **Cutter**: folds the strip into equal pieces and settles only on ten
- **Measure the pieces**: lays the pieces against one another to show whether they match
- **Shade**: colours a piece by tapping it and uncolours it by tapping again
- **Fraction slot**: takes the shaded amount written over ten
- **Decimal slot**: takes the same amount written with a point
- **Zero guard**: lights the empty ones column when the decimal is written without it
- **Uneven cut**: cuts ten pieces that are not the same size and asks whether one is a tenth

## Textbook (this topic)
**Pages:** 86, 87
**Key terms:** decimal fraction, tenths, decimal point, equal parts
A fraction with denominator ten is written with a decimal point, and one tenth becomes zero point one. One tenth of the space. Every part is the same size, and ten of them rebuild the whole. Splitting into ten is what every place in this chapter is built from. As zero point one. The dot is called the decimal point, and the digit after it counts tenths. The two ways mean exactly the same amount. Zero point three. The digit after the point says how many tenths, so three tenths is zero point three. Nothing else about the writing changes. Because the zero holds the ones place and says there are no whole ones. It also stops the point being missed when the number is read quickly. Write it every time.
