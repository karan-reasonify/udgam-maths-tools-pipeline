## What it is
**Description:** Count the zeros underneath, walk the point in from the right, and fill the empty places on the left when the digits run out.
**Why:** Turn a fraction into a decimal by counting the zeros underneath, and find out which side the filling zeros go on when the digits run out.
**Look:** A fraction at the top with its denominator lit, the numerator laid out in digit cards below it, and a second lane under that running the other way.
**Context:** Grade 4, and this is the step where a printed rule and its own worked example disagree with each other. The rule as printed sends the filling zeros to the wrong side of the numerator. The worked example directly beneath it puts them on the left and says so three separate times. Zeros hung past the right hand end of a nine build nine hundred, and the point counted in from there lands on nine whole ones where nine hundredths was wanted, which is a hundred times too big and looks perfectly tidy written down. Every answer printed in this chapter is arithmetically right, so nothing that checks the sums would ever find this.
**For the child:** Count the zeros in the bottom number. Start the point at the right hand end. Walk it in one digit for each zero. If the digits run out, fill the empty places on the left. Never hang a zero on the right hand end.
**Teaching notes:** Say the direction out loud every single time, and make the class say it back. The filling zeros go on the LEFT of the digits, between them and the point, because the places still empty are the ones nearest the point. The printed rule for this step sends them the other way, and the worked example directly beneath it does the opposite and says the word before three separate times: a zero placed in front of the nine to give 0.09, two zeros in front of the four to give 0.004, and a zero in front of the sixty-five to give 0.065. Teach the example, not the sentence above it. Then run the wrong side once, in public, all the way to its answer, so that it is never a mystery. Nine hundredths with the zeros hung on the right builds nine hundred, and the point counted in from there lands on nine whole ones, which is a hundred times what was wanted and looks completely tidy written down. That is what the second lane is for: the two answers stand together and the gap between them can be measured rather than asserted. Every answer printed in this chapter is arithmetically right, so nothing that checks the sums would ever have caught this one. Keep the zero counter locked on until the counting is automatic, and read every finished decimal back with its places counted from the point, because that is the same count run in reverse and it catches a slipped point in seconds.

## Phases (what each mount shows and does; the words are in tool.tsx)

### 1 · Counting the zeros underneath
**Screen:** a fraction at the top with its denominator lit and the counter still empty
**Does:** counts the zeros underneath and sets the counter to that many places
**Win:** the counter settles and that many empty places are drawn to the right of a point
**Feels:** the bottom number is doing the deciding, and only its zeros are being counted
**Goal:** the zeros in the denominator count the decimal places
**Observe:** The counter read the zeros of the denominator, not its digits, and never showed anything else.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- counts the digits of the denominator instead of its zeros, so a hundred asks for three decimal places. → the counter runs again slowly with each zero of the denominator lighting as it is counted and the leading one left plain and uncounted, and the number it finds is written beside the child's count rather than over it. That many empty places are then drawn to the right of a point, and the child's count is drawn as a second row of places underneath, one longer.
- counts the zeros of the numerator, or its digits, because the top number is the one being worked on. → the denominator lights on its own with the bar drawn heavier between the two, and the counter is walked across from the top number to the bottom one along a drawn path before it counts. The child's count stays written beside the counter, the numerator is dimmed to nothing but its outline while the counting runs, and both numbers stay on the stage afterwards.
**Misconception to break:** Counting the digits in the denominator instead of its zeros, and asking for three places.

### 2 · Placing the point from the right
**Screen:** the numerator laid out in digit cards with the point card at its right hand end
**Does:** slides the point card leftwards one digit at a time until the count is met
**Win:** the point drops on the counted digit and the decimal is read back from the point outwards
**Feels:** the walk starts at the right hand end every time, and the counting is what stops it
**Goal:** the point goes in from the right hand end of the numerator
**Observe:** The point card always started at the right hand end of the digits and walked leftwards.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- counts the point in from the LEFT hand end of the numerator, so a three digit numerator over a thousand comes out ten times too big. → both walks are run on the same row of cards with both points left standing where they were dropped, one counted from the left and one from the right, and each answer is read back with its places counted outward from its own point. The leading digit of the child's answer is lit in the ones column, where it is standing for whole ones, and the two answers stay on the stage together.
- drops the point after the first digit every time, because the first few answers happened to come out looking like that. → the counter is read out again and the point card is sent back to the right hand end and walked in with each step numbered above the card it passes, and it will not drop until the count is met. The child's point stays drawn where it was put, a second row of cards is laid underneath with the counted point in it, and both rows are read back from their own points outwards.
**Misconception to break:** Counting in from the left instead, and writing 6.17, which puts the six in the ones place where it is worth a whole one.

### 3 · When the numerator is too short
**Screen:** a short numerator with places still empty between it and the point
**Does:** takes zeros from the tray and fills the empty places on the left
**Win:** the zeros settle in front of the digits and every place is filled from the point outwards
**Feels:** the gap is always on the side nearest the point, so that is the side the zeros go in on
**Goal:** the filling zeros go on the left of the digits, between them and the point
**Observe:** When the digits ran out, the places left empty were the ones nearest the point.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- hangs the filling zeros on the right hand end of the digits, so nine hundredths is built out of nine hundred. → the child's zeros stay hanging exactly where they were hung and the row is read out as the number it now is, which is nine hundred rather than nine. The empty places are then lit again between the point and the digits, on their left, with the short arrow drawn running inward from the point, and a second row is laid underneath with the grey zeros walked into those places instead. Both rows stay standing.
- lays the short numerator down and drops the point without filling anything, so three over a hundred comes out as three tenths. → the counter is read out again against the row, and the empty place nearest the point is drawn and held lit with the arrow running inward to it, so that the number of places the answer actually has can be counted off against the number the counter is holding. The child's answer stays written, a grey zero is offered from the tray, and both answers are read back with their places counted outward from the point.
**Misconception to break:** Hanging the zero on the right of the three and writing 0.30, which is ten times too big.

### 4 · Why the side matters so much
**Screen:** the same fraction loaded into both lanes with the distance bar still empty
**Does:** runs the wrong side lane to its answer and measures the gap between the two
**Win:** both answers stand together and the distance bar says how many times out the wrong one is
**Feels:** the wrong side does not look wrong, it looks finished, and that is exactly what makes it worth measuring
**Goal:** zeros on the wrong side make the number bigger instead of smaller
**Observe:** The second lane reached a tidy answer that was a hundred times too big, and nothing about it looked wrong.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- takes the second lane's answer as the right one, because it came out a tidy whole number and the working was done carefully. → both answers are carried down to the grid and the strip and drawn at their real sizes, one as a few cells of a hundred and the other as nine whole ones laid out along the stage, so the two are seen rather than compared on the line. The distance bar writes how many times apart they are, both answers stay standing where they were made, and nothing is marked on either of them.
- decides both answers must be right in their own way, since each lane followed a rule it was given. → the question the fraction asked is read out again at the top of the stage and both answers are held against it in turn, each one turned back into a fraction on the bench and stood beside the fraction it started from. One comes back to it and one does not, and the one that does not is left standing beside what it actually came to, with the distance bar still drawn between the two answers.
**Misconception to break:** Following the rule as it is written rather than as the worked example below it does it.

## Delight
The fourth phase, when the second lane is let go. The same fraction runs down both lanes at once and in step: above, the zeros walk into the empty places on the left of the digits and the point card comes in from the right hand end; below, the zeros hang themselves past the right hand end and that lane's point card comes in from the right of what they built. Both lanes reach a finished answer on the same tick and neither is marked. The distance bar then draws itself between the two answers and writes how many times apart they are, and the two sit there side by side for as long as anybody wants to look at them, with the voice saying nothing about either of them.

**General miss line:** Send the point card back to the right hand end and walk it in again, counting.

## Answer key
Count the zeros in the denominator. That is how many decimal places the answer has. Then slide the point into the numerator that many digits in from its right hand end. If the numerator has fewer digits than that, the places still empty are the ones nearest the point, so the filling zeros go on the LEFT of the digits already there, between them and the point. A zero put there is a padding zero, and it always goes in front of the digits and never on the right hand end. Nine hundredths needs two places and nine supplies one, so one zero goes in front of the nine and the answer is 0.09. Four thousandths needs three, so two zeros go in front of the four. Sixty-five thousandths needs three and has two, so one zero goes in front of the sixty-five and the answer is 0.065. Zeros hung on the right hand end instead make the number bigger, which is the opposite of what this step is for.

## Student controls
- **Zero counter**: counts the zeros in the denominator and sets how many decimal places are needed
- **Digit cards**: lays the numerator out one digit to a card
- **Point card**: slides in from the right hand end, one digit at a time
- **Empty places**: lights the places still waiting, on the left of the digits, between them and the point
- **Zero tray**: hands over zeros for the empty places and refuses a drop past the right hand end
- **Wrong side lane**: runs the same fraction with the zeros hung on the right, all the way to its answer
- **Distance bar**: draws the gap between the two answers and writes how many times out the wrong one is
- **Read it back**: says the finished decimal with its places counted outward from the point

## Textbook (this topic)
**Pages:** 91
**Key terms:** numerator, denominator, decimal places, padding zero
A decimal fraction becomes a decimal by counting the zeros below and placing the point that many digits from the right. The zeros underneath tell you how many digits go after the point. Count that many digits in from the right end of the top number, and put the point there. If the top number is too short, fill the empty places with zeros on the left. One, because there is one zero in the ten underneath. The zeros in the denominator count the decimal places. Two zeros means two places, three means three. One digit in from the right of the seven, which gives zero point seven. Always count in from the right hand end of the numerator, never from the left. Put a zero before the nine, making zero point zero nine. The padding goes on the left, between the point and the digits you already have. You would have ninety hundredths, not nine, which is ten times too big. Hang two zeros there and it is worse still, because nine hundred with the point counted in lands on nine whole ones. Zeros on the left make a number smaller and zeros on the right make it bigger.
