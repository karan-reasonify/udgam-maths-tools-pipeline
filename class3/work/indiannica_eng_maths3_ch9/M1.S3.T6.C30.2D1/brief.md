## What it is
**Description:** Pour water into real jugs, cups and bottles, read the level at a mark, and sort real containers by whether they hold more or less than a litre.
**Why:** Choose millilitres or litres for a real container, pour small vessels into a one litre jug and read the level at a mark, find half a litre and a quarter of a litre, and sort real containers by whether they hold more or less than a litre.
**Look:** A glass measuring jug with its marks at the left, a row of labelled standard vessels beside it, a shelf of real containers at the right, mL and L tags, a litre line across the table, and a pour counter at the top left of the canvas.
**Context:** Grade 3, the first working topic on capacity. An adult sees a jug being filled, which a child can do at a tap. What is being settled is which unit fits which container, and how big a litre is: small vessels poured one after another reach the litre mark exactly, and half a litre and a quarter of a litre are the middle and the quarter of the jug. A child who has only met the word litre on a bottle will call every container a litre, and will say a bucket holds the same as a cup because both are full.
**For the child:** Pour water into a container. Drop it on mL or L. Then pour small vessels into the one litre jug and count them.
**Teaching notes:** Pour real water before the tool opens: a litre bottle into small cups, then into a bowl, and say which container held more. Let a teacup and a bucket stand beside the litre bottle so the class can see a bucket hold more and a teacup hold less. Millilitres are for small amounts and litres for large ones, and one litre is one thousand millilitres. When the class pours small cups into the litre bottle, ask them to count the pours before the water reaches the top, and read the level at a mark, never between two marks. Say about whenever a bowl of curd, a jug of water, a glass of buttermilk or a juice packet is set beside half a litre or a quarter of a litre: they are about those amounts, never exactly. Do not ask for the exact capacity of a thing. Use fresh numbers: the exercise's seven containers and its cup problem are the child's to try.

## Phases (what each mount shows and does; the words are in tool.tsx)

### 1 · Capacity: litres and millilitres
**Screen:** a teacup, a bucket and a bottle on the shelf, the glass jug empty, and the mL and L tags hanging over them
**Does:** pours water into each container and drops it on the mL tag or the L tag
**Win:** the container settles on its tag and the unit fact one litre beside one thousand millilitres lights in a vermilion ring
**Feels:** a teacup barely wetting the bottom of the jug while a bucket fills it many times over
**Goal:** small amounts are measured in millilitres and large amounts in litres
**Observe:** Small vessels poured one after another brought the water to the litre mark exactly, and the pour counter stopped there.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- drops the teacup on the litre tag because a drink is measured in litres at the shop → the teacup is poured into the litre jug and the water barely covers the first mark, and the unit fact one litre and one thousand millilitres lights in a vermilion ring
- drops the bucket on the millilitre tag → the bucket is poured into the litre jug again and again and the jug fills many times, the number of pours counted aloud
**Misconception to break:** Choosing litres because litre is the word used for drinks.

### 2 · Standard vessels: from 50 mL up to 1 L
**Screen:** the empty one litre jug and a row of vessels marked fifty, a hundred, two hundred and five hundred millilitres
**Does:** taps a vessel to pour it in, counts the pours until the water reaches the litre mark, and reads the level at a mark
**Win:** the water meets the litre mark exactly and the pour counter lights in a green tag
**Feels:** the water rising one equal step at a time until it meets the litre mark and stops
**Goal:** small vessels poured into a one litre jug reach the litre mark exactly when their amounts add up to one litre
**Observe:** A teacup, a mug and a bucket all dropped on a tag, and the bucket went to the litre side.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- reads the level between two marks → the water settles at a mark and a small near-black pointer comes to rest on that mark while the reading is spoken aloud
- says the number written on the vessel as the number of vessels → the vessels are poured one at a time and a small counter above the jug ticks once for each, with the water rising by the same step each time
**Misconception to break:** Saying five hundred, the number on the vessel, instead of how many vessels.

### 3 · Half a litre and a quarter of a litre
**Screen:** the one litre jug with its half way and quarter way marks, and a bowl of curd, a jug of water, a glass of buttermilk and a juice packet waiting beside it
**Does:** pours to the half way mark and to the quarter way mark, then sets each real thing beside the mark it is about the same as
**Win:** the water settles at the mark and the thing stands beside it with the word about
**Feels:** the water stopping at the middle of the jug and a bowl of curd standing beside it, about the same
**Goal:** half a litre is the middle mark of the jug and a quarter of a litre is a quarter of the way up, and real things hold about those amounts
**Observe:** The half way mark sat in the middle of the jug and the quarter way mark a quarter of the way up.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- sets a bowl of curd exactly on the half way mark and calls it exactly half a litre → the bowl and the mark sit side by side with the word about written between them, and the spoken line says about half a litre
- pours to the quarter way mark and says it is half a litre → the half way mark lights beside the quarter way mark and the water is poured on up to it, so the two heights are seen together
**Misconception to break:** Giving one hundred millilitres, a guess, because half of a litre was not worked out.

### 4 · More than or less than one litre
**Screen:** a shelf of real containers, a litre line across the table with a More side and a Less side, and a pan with a cup beside it
**Does:** drags each container to the More side or the Less side, then fills the pan cup by cup and keeps the count
**Win:** the sorted shelf stands on two sides of the litre line and the count of cups lights in a green tag
**Feels:** a shelf of containers standing on two sides of one litre line, and a pan filling cup by cup
**Goal:** a container holds more than a litre if it fills the litre jug more than once, and less if it does not fill it
**Observe:** A bucket dragged past the litre line went to More, and a spoon went to Less.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- puts the car fuel tank on the Less side because it is drawn small on the shelf → the tank is poured into the litre jug and the jug fills again and again, the number of pours counted aloud, and the tank moves to More
- fills the pan with cups and stops at the number written on the cup → the cups are poured into the pan one at a time and a small counter ticks once for each, with the level rising by the same step
**Misconception to break:** Saying the cup, because a cup feels full and the drum is only a picture.

## Delight
Two small surprises. On the second phase, as the last vessel is poured, the water climbs to the litre mark and stops with a tiny ripple, and the vessels lined up beside the jug light together like cups on a shelf. On the fourth phase, when the pan is filled cup by cup, each cup tips in with a splash and the level in the pan rises by one equal step every time.

**General miss line:** Look at the water again. Read the level at the nearest mark.

## Answer key
Capacity is how much liquid a container can hold. Small amounts are measured in millilitres and large amounts in litres, and one litre is one thousand millilitres. Small vessels poured into a one litre jug reach the litre mark exactly when their amounts add up to one litre: ten of one hundred millilitres, five of two hundred, two of five hundred. A level is read at a mark, never between two. Half a litre is five hundred millilitres and a quarter of a litre is two hundred and fifty millilitres, and a bowl of curd, a jug of water, a glass of buttermilk and a juice packet are only about those amounts. A bucket and a drum hold more than a litre, and a spoon and a teacup hold less.

## Student controls
- **Tap**: pours water into a container or the measuring jug, and the level rises to the next mark
- **Standard vessels**: tapping one pours it into the measuring jug and adds one to the pour counter
- **mL and L tags**: dropping a container on one chooses the unit for its capacity
- **Half way and quarter way marks**: tapping one pours the jug to that mark and sets real things beside it
- **More and Less sides**: dropping a container on one sorts it against the one litre line
- **Number pad**: types a count of vessels or an amount in millilitres
