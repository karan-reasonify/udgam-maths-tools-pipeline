## What it is
**Description:** Put a real thing on a balance, choose grams or kilograms, make the pans level with iron weights, and sort things against one kilogram.
**Why:** Weigh real things on a balance with standard weights, choose grams or kilograms for a thing, see that four equal packets make one kilogram, so two make half and one makes a quarter, and tell whether a thing weighs more or less than one kilogram.
**Look:** A balance on a shelf with a rack of iron weights and a kitchen scale, a table of twelve real things, a Given tag that adds the weights on the right pan, an Answer tag, a unit chooser, two bins, and on the third phase four packets of sugar and a one kilogram bar.
**Context:** Grade 3, the first weight topic of the metric measures chapter. An adult sees a balance being read, which a real balance and a few mangoes do well. What is actually being settled is how heavy a kilogram feels and how a thing is compared with it: a child who has only met the word kilogram believes that a bigger thing is a heavier thing, and will say that a pen is heavier than a bag of salt because the pen is longer. The tool puts the thing on the pan and lets the beam decide.
**For the child:** Put a thing on the left pan. Pick grams or kilograms. Add iron weights until the beam is level. Then sort the things as heavier or lighter than one kilogram.
**Teaching notes:** Hold a one kilogram bag of salt in one hand and a pencil box in the other before the tool opens, and let each child say which is heavier and which is bigger, because they are not always the same thing. Hang two bags from a coat hanger as a balance and see it tilt. Say about every time a benchmark is used: a loaf of bread is about half a kilogram and a cup of yogurt about a quarter, and never ask for the exact weight of one. For the Math Lab idea, put the one kilogram weight on one pan and ask pairs to make the other pan level with different weights, then ask how many different ways were found and what each way adds up to. Wait for the child to say the beam is level before they read the weights. Use fresh numbers: the exercise's potatoes and its missing weights are the child's to work out.

## Phases (what each mount shows and does; the words are in tool.tsx)

### 1 · Heavy and light: kilograms and grams
**Screen:** a watermelon and a pen on a table, the balance level and empty, and the unit chooser showing grams and kilograms
**Does:** puts a thing on the left pan, watches the beam tilt, and picks grams or kilograms for it
**Win:** the beam tilts, the chosen unit lights deep blue on the Given tag, and the Unit fact ring shows a kilogram as a thousand grams
**Feels:** a heavy melon dipping the pan low and a pen barely moving the other one, one slow tilt at a time
**Goal:** heavy things are weighed in kilograms and light things in grams, and one kilogram is one thousand grams
**Observe:** The beam tilted down on the side with the heavier thing, and swung back as weights were added to the other pan.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- picks grams for the watermelon → the unit chooser shows the watermelon on the pan with a thousand grams drawn as ten small bags beside it, and the Unit fact ring lights round the words one kilogram
- picks kilograms for the pen → a pen is set on the pan and the beam hardly moves, and a whole kilogram weight is set beside it to show how much heavier a kilogram is
**Misconception to break:** Choosing grams because a number of grams is bigger, which sounds like a more careful answer.

### 2 · A balance and standard weights
**Screen:** a parcel on the left pan, the beam tilted down on the left, the rack of seven iron weights and an empty Given tag
**Does:** drags iron weights onto the right pan until the beam settles level, and reads the weights off the Given tag
**Win:** the beam settles level and the needle stands upright, and the Answer tag names the weight in green
**Feels:** the beam leaning, then slowly coming to rest as each iron weight goes on until the needle stands up straight
**Goal:** a balance is level when both pans hold the same weight, so the iron weights tell the weight of the thing
**Observe:** The Given tag grew by each iron weight that went on, and the Answer tag named the same total when the beam was level.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- reads only the biggest weight on the pan → the Given tag adds the weights one at a time as the child's finger touches each one, and the total is read aloud
- stops while the beam is still tilted → the pointer needle swings a little toward the heavier pan and a thin near-black line marks where level would be
**Misconception to break:** Giving only the biggest weight, 2 kg, and forgetting to add the 1 kg.

### 3 · Half a kilogram and a quarter of a kilogram
**Screen:** four equal packets of sugar, a one kilogram weight on the right pan, and a Quarter bar button
**Does:** puts two packets on the left pan and a half kilogram weight on the right, then one packet against a quarter, and taps Quarter bar
**Win:** two packets level against the half kilogram weight, the Quarter bar shows two of four quarters in deep blue, and the word about appears beside a loaf
**Feels:** four packets filling a kilogram, two of them balancing a half kilogram weight and one balancing a quarter
**Goal:** four equal packets make one kilogram, so two make half a kilogram and one makes a quarter, and some things weigh about half or about a quarter
**Observe:** Two of the four equal packets balanced a half kilogram weight, and one packet balanced a quarter.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- says one packet weighs half a kilogram → one packet goes on the left pan and a half kilogram weight on the right, and the beam dips far down on the right so the packet is seen to be lighter
- says a loaf weighs exactly half a kilogram → the loaf goes on the left pan, the beam wobbles near level and settles a little off, and the word about is written beside the Answer tag
**Misconception to break:** Saying two apples weigh a quarter of a kilogram, because two is a small number.

### 4 · More than or less than one kilogram
**Screen:** a table of twelve real things, the one kilogram bag of salt on the right pan as a guide, and two bins
**Does:** puts a thing on the left pan against the guide, watches the beam, and drops the thing into the right bin
**Win:** the thing lands in its bin, the beam rests tilted the right way, and the counter reads how many things have been sorted
**Feels:** the beam dipping down on a backpack and rising on a pen, each thing found by the way it leans
**Goal:** a thing weighs more than one kilogram when it pulls the beam down against the one kilogram weight, and less when it does not
**Observe:** The backpack pulled the beam down against the one kilogram bag and the pen did not.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- puts the pen in the heavier bin because it is the longer thing → the pen goes on the left pan against the guide salt and the beam rises on the pen's side, so the pen is seen to be lighter
- puts the backpack in the lighter bin because it is full of soft things → the backpack goes on the left pan against the guide salt and the beam dips far down on its side
**Misconception to break:** Saying the pen, because it is the longer thing on the table, when what counts is how heavy it is.

## Delight
On the second phase, the moment the last iron weight makes the pans equal, the beam settles with one slow bounce like a seesaw coming to rest and the needle stands straight up. On the third phase the four packets of sugar slide together into the one kilogram bar and the quarters light one by one.

**General miss line:** Look at the beam. Is it level yet? Count the weights on the pan again.

## Answer key
Weight is how heavy something is. Grams measure light things and kilograms measure heavy things, and one kilogram is one thousand grams. A balance is level when both pans hold the same weight, so the weight of a thing is the weight of the iron weights that make the beam level. Shops keep weights of 5 kg, 2 kg, 1 kg, 500 g, 200 g, 100 g and 50 g. Four equal packets that weigh one kilogram in all mean two packets weigh half a kilogram and one packet a quarter of a kilogram. A loaf of bread weighs about half a kilogram and a cup of yogurt about a quarter of a kilogram, but only about. A thing weighs more than one kilogram when it needs more than the one kilogram weight on the other pan to level the beam, and less than one kilogram when the one kilogram weight pulls the beam down.
