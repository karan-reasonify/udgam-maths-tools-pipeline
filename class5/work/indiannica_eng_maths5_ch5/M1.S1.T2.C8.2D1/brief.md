## What it is
**Description:** Cut a strip into ten equal parts, shade tenths, read them as a fraction and a decimal, and watch ten tenths close into one whole.
**Why:** Write tenths of a whole, and wholes with tenths, as fractions and as decimals, and trade ten tenths for one whole to count lots of tenths into ones and tenths.
**Look:** Strips of one length stacked across the working area, a fraction card and a decimal card to their right, and Cut, Shape, Trade and Lots of buttons under them.
**Context:** Grade 5, the first pages on tenths. An adult sees a fraction written a new way. What the pages are doing is cutting one whole into ten equal parts, naming one part a tenth, and writing wholes and tenths either side of a point, with ten tenths making one whole. The opening box's question can be counted in tenths; the child does that with its own numbers, and no one answers it for them.
**For the child:** Cut the strip into ten equal parts. Shade some parts. Read them as a fraction and as a decimal. When ten tenths are shaded, trade them for one whole.
**Teaching notes:** Cut a paper strip into ten equal parts before any decimal is written, and check the parts are one width. Say each amount three ways, in words, as a fraction and as a decimal. Point at the decimal point every time and name the whole number part and the decimal part. Trade ten tenths for one whole on the desk before the tool does it. The opening box's question is counted in tenths the same way; leave that count to the child, with the box's own numbers, and do not work it for them. Multiplying decimals comes later.
**Placement:** Explore

## Opening (first frame of every mount)
Every mount comes up already furnished for its phase with nothing shaded yet. ## Phases (what each mount shows and does; the words are in tool.tsx)

### 1 · One tenth
**Screen:** one whole strip, not yet cut, with the fraction card and the decimal card blank
**Does:** taps Cut, checks that the ten parts are one width, and shades one part
**Win:** the fraction card shows 1/10, the decimal card shows 0.1 in green, and the voice reads zero point one
**Feels:** one strip splitting into ten parts of exactly one width
**Goal:** a whole cut into ten equal parts gives tenths, and one part is 1/10, written 0.1
**Observe:** The ten parts of a cut strip were all exactly one width.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- shades one part before tapping Cut, so the whole strip fills → the whole strip fills deep blue and the decimal card reads one whole; the Cut button pulses once, and cutting splits the shading into ten shaded parts, all still counted
- reads one tenth as one → the decimal card writes the digit after the point in amber and a 0 before it, and one whole strip is drawn beside the part strip so the two sizes are seen together
**Misconception to break:** Calling one part of a strip cut into unequal pieces a tenth, though tenths come only from ten equal parts.

### 2 · Tenths as decimals
**Screen:** one strip already cut into ten equal parts, with none shaded
**Does:** shades parts one tap at a time and reads both cards after each tap
**Win:** nine parts shaded, 9/10 on one card and 0.9 on the other, with a 0 before the point for no whole ones
**Feels:** the digit after the point ticking up with every part shaded
**Goal:** a number of tenths is written as a decimal with that digit after the point
**Observe:** Each tap on a part changed only the digit after the point.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- types the count of shaded parts before the point, as a whole number with a zero after it → a whole strip for every unit the child typed is drawn beside the part strip, and the shaded run of the part strip is lit amber, so the two amounts stand side by side
- counts the unshaded parts instead of the shaded ones → each shaded part gets its own number in turn, from the left, and the unshaded parts stay warm white and unnumbered
**Misconception to break:** Writing 8.0 for eight tenths, which puts the 8 in the ones place and means 8 whole strips.

### 3 · Wholes and tenths together
**Screen:** two whole strips shaded, and a third strip cut into ten parts with none shaded
**Does:** shades tenths of the third strip and reads the decimal card, whole number part and decimal part
**Win:** the card reads 2.9 with Whole number part under the 2 and Decimal part under the 9
**Feels:** the wholes and the tenths falling into place on either side of the point
**Goal:** whole strips give the whole number part and the tenths give the decimal part, either side of the point
**Observe:** Whole strips changed the digit before the point, and part strips the digit after it.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- writes the wholes and the tenths as one number after the point → the whole strips light deep blue one by one and their count moves in front of the point on the decimal card, under the words Whole number part
- counts the part strip as one more whole → the part strip's unshaded parts are lit amber, and the words Decimal part are drawn under its shaded run
**Misconception to break:** Writing 0.29 because 2 and 9 were both counted, though the 2 is whole strips and belongs before the point.

### 4 · Ten tenths make one whole
**Screen:** an empty strip cut into ten parts, the Lots of button set to nine tenths, and the Tenths tally at nothing
**Does:** taps Lots of three times, trades each ten tenths for a whole strip, and reads what is left
**Win:** twenty-seven tenths close into two whole strips and seven tenths, and the card reads 2.7
**Feels:** ten loose tenths clicking together into one whole strip
**Goal:** ten tenths make one whole, so a count of tenths is read as ones and tenths
**Observe:** Every time ten tenths were shaded, they closed into one whole strip.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- writes the count of tenths as a whole number → the tally's tenths slide together ten at a time into whole strips, and the decimal card fills its two sides as they close
- stops trading after the first whole strip → the loose tenths left over are counted aloud in tens, and each ten lights amber until it is traded
**Misconception to break:** Writing 27 as the answer, which counts the tenths as if each one were a whole.

## Delight
On the fourth phase, the moment the tenth part of a strip is shaded, the ten parts slide together with a soft click, their cut lines melt away, and the strip rises into the row of whole strips while the decimal card's whole number part ticks up by one.

**General miss line:** Count the shaded parts again, one by one, and look at the point.

## Answer key
A whole cut into ten equal parts gives tenths. One part is one tenth, written 1/10 or 0.1. A number of tenths is written with that digit after the point. Whole strips give the whole number part, written before the point, and the tenths give the decimal part, written after it. 10 tenths make 1 whole, so a count of tenths is read as ones and tenths.

## Student controls
- **Cut button**: splits the bottom strip, or circle, into ten equal parts
- **Part tap**: shades a part deep blue, or clears it on a second tap
- **Shape switch**: shows the same whole as a strip or as a circle of ten equal sectors
- **Trade button**: slides ten shaded tenths together and closes them into one whole strip
- **Lots of button**: adds the same number of tenths again each time it is tapped
- **Number pad**: sets a number of tenths up to ninety-nine, or types a fraction or a decimal

## Textbook (this topic)
**Pages:** 74, 75
**Key terms:** tenth, decimal point, decimal form, whole number part, decimal part
A whole cut into ten equal parts gives tenths, written after a decimal point, and ten tenths make one whole. One of ten equal parts of a whole is one tenth, written 1/10, or 0.1 in decimal form. The digit after the decimal point counts tenths, and whole ones go before the point. Ten tenths make one whole. Each part is one tenth of the strip, written 1/10. In decimal form it is written 0.1 and read as zero point one. The dot is called the decimal point. 9/10 means 9 tenths, so it is written 0.9. The 0 before the point shows there are no whole ones. The first digit after the point counts tenths. The wholes give the whole number part, 6. The 8 tenths give the decimal part. So 6 + 8/10 = 6 + 0.8 = 6.8. The point sits between the whole number part and the decimal part. 10 tenths fill one whole, so 1 one is 10 tenths. 23 tenths are 2 wholes and 3 tenths more, so 23/10 = 2.3. Counting 5 lots of 3 tenths gives 15 tenths, which is 1.5.
