## What it is
**Description:** Cover a surface with unit squares row by row and count them, pick the unit square that fits, and compare two patches counted in squares of one size.
**Why:** Tell area, the surface a figure covers, from perimeter, the length of its boundary; pick the unit square that fits; count unit squares by rows; and compare areas only in squares of one size.
**Look:** A figure on plain paper with a tray of violet unit squares under it, a string for the edge, a unit shelf on the second phase and two patches side by side on the last.
**Context:** Grade 5, the first area pages and the squared-paper activity. An adult sees counting. What the pages are doing is naming a new measure: the area is the amount of surface a figure covers, counted in unit squares, and it is kept apart from the perimeter, the length of the boundary.
**For the child:** Cover the shape with squares, no gaps and no overlaps. Count them by rows. Say the unit.
**Teaching notes:** Cover a book with paper squares of one size before the tool opens, and count them. Keep the two measures apart from the first minute: the string measures the length of the boundary and the squares measure the surface. Say the unit every time, sq. cm for a book and sq. m for a floor. Count by rows, the same number in every row, and lay the rows along the longer side, the length. Compare two areas by counting only when the squares are the same size. The printed rectangles and figures are the child's to count.

## Phases (what each mount shows and does; the words are in tool.tsx)

### 1 · The surface covered
**Screen:** a card drawn on the paper, a string beside it and the tray of unit squares
**Does:** lays the string once round the edge, then covers the card with squares, and says which number is which
**Win:** the string reads 16 cm along the boundary and the squares count 15, labelled Area 15 sq. cm
**Feels:** the squares filling the inside that the string only went round
**Goal:** the area is the surface a figure covers, and the perimeter is the length of its boundary
**Observe:** The string went only round the edge, while the squares filled the inside.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- lays squares only along the edge of the card → the empty middle of the card is lit amber, and a ghost row is drawn across it in steps while the child's squares stay
- types the length of the string as the area → the string is lit vermilion with the word Length beside it, and the squares are lit violet with the word Surface beside them
**Misconception to break:** Calling the length of the string the area, because both numbers describe the same card.

### 2 · The unit square
**Screen:** the unit shelf with squares of side 1 cm, 1 m and 1 km, and a picture card of a school playground
**Does:** drags the unit square that fits onto the picture card
**Win:** the 1 m square settles on the playground card and the unit reads sq. m
**Feels:** a unit that fits the surface settling on it
**Goal:** a unit square with sides of 1 cm has an area of 1 sq. cm, and bigger surfaces use sq. m or sq. km
**Observe:** Every row along the length held the same number of squares.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- drags the square with sides of one kilometre onto a playground → the picture card of a city is drawn beside the playground card for a moment, and the square with sides of one metre is lit amber
- drags the square with sides of one centimetre onto a floor → a row of tiny squares starts across the floor card in steps and barely moves, and the square with sides of one metre is lit amber
**Misconception to break:** Choosing sq. km because a playground feels big, though sq. km is for a city or a state.

### 3 · Counting the squares
**Screen:** a rectangle 9 cm long and 4 cm wide on the paper, empty, with the tray and the Lay a row button
**Does:** taps Lay a row until the rectangle is covered, then types the count
**Win:** four rows of nine fill the rectangle, the count reads 36, and the slot reads 36 sq. cm in green
**Feels:** row after row clicking into place until no paper shows
**Goal:** the number of unit squares that cover a surface is its area, counted by rows
**Observe:** A patch of bigger squares had a smaller count but did not cover less.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- stops laying rows one row short and types that count → the uncovered strip is lit amber, and a ghost row is drawn into it in steps while the child's rows stay
- drops a square on top of another → the doubled square is lit amber and slides off to the gap beside it, while every other square stays
**Misconception to break:** Counting only the squares along the edge, which gives a ring round the boundary, not the whole surface.

### 4 · Comparing by counting
**Screen:** Patch A, 10 squares by 2 squares, beside Patch B, 7 squares by 3 squares, both uncounted
**Does:** counts both patches, taps the one that covers more, then tries the Bigger squares switch
**Win:** Patch B lights green with 21 squares against 20, and the bigger squares switch dims the counts to half strength until the sizes match
**Feels:** two counts side by side, and the fair one winning
**Goal:** two areas are compared by their counts only when the squares are the same size
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- taps the longer patch as the one that covers more → both counters are drawn in steps under their patches, and the greater count is lit amber
- compares counts after switching one patch to bigger squares → both counts dim to half strength, the two square sizes are drawn side by side, and the switch is lit amber
**Misconception to break:** Choosing Patch A because it is longer, though a longer patch can cover less.

## Delight
On the third phase, when the last row settles into place, the covered rectangle gives one soft ripple from corner to corner, like a tiled floor being finished.

**General miss line:** Look at the squares. Is there a gap, an overlap or a row missing?

## Answer key
The area of a figure is the amount of surface it covers. A square with sides of 1 cm is a unit square, with an area of 1 sq. cm; area is measured in square units, sq. cm for small surfaces, sq. m for large ones and sq. km for very large ones. The number of unit squares that cover a surface, with no gaps and no overlaps, is its area; count them by rows. Two areas can be compared by counting only when the squares are the same size. A perimeter is the length of the boundary, a length in cm or m.

Area is the amount of surface a figure covers, counted in unit squares, and measured in sq. cm, sq. m or sq. km. The area of a figure is the amount of surface it covers, while its perimeter is the length of its boundary. Area is measured with unit squares, and a square with sides of 1 cm has an area of 1 sq. cm. Count the unit squares, row by row, to find the area. Two areas can be compared by counting only when the squares are the same size. The amount of surface a figure covers is its area. A perimeter is the length of the boundary. An area measures the surface inside that boundary. A square with sides of 1 cm is a unit square. Its area is 1 square centimetre, written 1 sq. cm. Area is always measured in square units. Small surfaces use sq. cm, large ones use sq. m and very large ones use sq. km. There are 3 rows, with 7 squares in each row. 7 + 7 + 7 = 21, so 21 unit squares cover it. The number of unit squares that cover a surface is its area. So the area of a 7 cm by 3 cm rectangle is 21 sq. cm. The first covers 6 x 3 = 18 squares. The second covers 4 x 4 = 16 squares. 18 > 16, so the first covers more. This works only when the squares of both patches are the same size.
