## What it is
**Description:** Work back to a square's side from its area or perimeter, find a missing breadth, and cut an L-shape into two rectangles or lay equal squares together.
**Why:** Work back to the side of a square from its area or its perimeter, find a missing breadth from an area and a length, and find the area of a figure built from rectangles or equal squares.
**Look:** A target card over the working area, a square that grows with the Try dial, a perimeter string on the second phase, a rectangle with the Breadth dial on the third, and an L-shape with the Cut tool on the last.
**Context:** Grade 5, the worked examples on squares and Exercise 8.2's harder questions. An adult sees division and square roots. What the pages are doing is asking what number times a known measure gives the total, and checking by multiplying back, and building a larger area out of rectangles and equal squares whose areas add.
**For the child:** Try a side and see if it fills the target. Cut an L-shape into two rectangles, then add.
**Teaching notes:** Cut an L-shape from squared paper into two rectangles before the tool opens, and count its squares to check. To find a side from an area, try small numbers in turn until one times itself gives the area. From a perimeter to an area, always go through the side. A missing breadth is the number that times the length gives the area; multiply back to check. An L-shape may be cut either way, and both cuts give the same total. Equal squares put together have the count times one square's area. The printed squares, rectangles and L-shape are the child's to work.

## Phases (what each mount shows and does; the words are in tool.tsx)

### 1 · A side from an area
**Screen:** a target card reading Area 256 sq. cm and an empty square outline, with the Try dial
**Does:** turns the Try dial side by side, watching each square fall short or spill past, and types the side
**Win:** the try of sixteen fills the outline exactly, the line reads 16 x 16 = 256, and 16 cm lands green
**Feels:** a try landing on the outline exactly after one too small and one too big
**Goal:** the side of a square is the number that times itself gives its area
**Observe:** Tries that were too small left part of the outline empty, and tries too big spilled past it.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- types half the area as the side → a square of that side is drawn in steps, spilling far past the outline, and the outline is lit amber while the child's number stays in the slot
- stops the Try dial on a side whose square falls just short → the empty strip round the try is lit amber, and the rows are counted beside the target in steps
**Misconception to break:** Halving the area, as if a side were half of it, which gives a side far too long.

### 2 · From perimeter to area
**Screen:** a vermilion string labelled 84 m round an empty square, and the Fold in four button
**Does:** taps Fold in four to find the side, then fills the square with rows and types the area
**Win:** each fold reads 21 m, the line reads 21 x 21 = 441, and 441 sq. m lands green
**Feels:** the string folding into four equal sides and the square filling behind them
**Goal:** from a square's perimeter, find the side first, then the area
**Observe:** The folded string gave the side before any squares were laid.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- types the perimeter times itself → the string is folded into four in steps, and one side is lit amber before any rows are laid
- types the side and stops → the question's word area is lit amber, and the empty square waits for its rows beside the child's side
**Misconception to break:** Multiplying the perimeter by itself, which mixes a length round the edge with a surface.

### 3 · A missing breadth
**Screen:** a rectangle with its length labelled 19 cm, a target card reading Area 152 sq. cm, and the Breadth dial
**Does:** turns the Breadth dial until the rows meet the area, then taps Check
**Win:** eight rows meet the target, the breadth reads 8 cm, and the Check line reads 19 x 8 = 152
**Feels:** the Check line landing exactly on the area
**Goal:** a missing breadth is the number that times the length gives the area, checked by multiplying back
**Observe:** The Check line multiplied the found breadth back and landed on the area.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- types the area take away the length → rows are stacked on the length in steps until the target is met, and the row count is shown beside the child's number
- stops the Breadth dial one row short → the Check line multiplies the child's breadth back, and the gap between it and the target is lit amber
**Misconception to break:** Taking the length away from the area, which mixes a surface with a length.

### 4 · Split and add
**Screen:** an L-shaped floor on squared paper, its outside sides labelled, and the Cut tool
**Does:** snaps a cut along a grid line, finds each rectangle's area, and taps Join to add them
**Win:** the parts read 84 sq. m and 24 sq. m, they slide together, and 108 sq. m lands green
**Feels:** two rectangles sliding apart, each with its own area, and back together
**Goal:** an L-shape is cut into two rectangles whose areas add, and equal squares add by count times one square
**Observe:** Cutting the L-shape a different way gave two different rectangles but the same total.
**Wrong (each is a traceback on the working area):**
- multiplies the two longest outside sides → a rectangle with those sides is drawn in outline over the L in steps, and the corner it adds that is not part of the floor is lit amber
- snaps a cut and finds only one part's area → the second part is lit amber where it sits apart, and the Join button pulses
**Misconception to break:** Multiplying the two longest outside sides together, which counts a corner that is not part of the floor.

## Delight
On the fourth phase, when Join slides the two rectangles back together, the dashed cut line fades away and the floor gives one small bounce, whole again.

**General miss line:** Look at the rows against the outline. Too few, or too many?

## Answer key
The area of a square is side x side, so its side is the number that times itself gives the area. From a square's perimeter, find the side first, since 4 x side is the perimeter, then the area. A missing breadth is the number that times the length gives the area; check by multiplying back. An L-shape is cut into two rectangles, and their areas are added; either cut gives the same total. Equal squares put together have an area of the count times one square's area.

## Student controls
- **Try dial**: sets a side; draws a square of that side filled with rows against the target
- **Fold in four button**: folds a square's perimeter string into four equal sides
- **Breadth dial**: stacks rows on a rectangle of known length until they meet the area
- **Check line**: multiplies the found side back and sets it beside the given measure
- **Cut tool and Join button**: cut an L-shape along a grid line into two rectangles, and slide them back
- **Copies control**: lays a chosen number of equal squares side by side
- **Number pad**: types a side or an area, with cm, m, sq. cm and sq. m keys

## Textbook (this topic)
**Pages:** 126, 127, 128
**Key terms:** side, missing side, breadth, L-shape, split, equal squares
A side is worked back from an area or a perimeter, a missing breadth from an area and a length, and an area is built from rectangles and equal squares. The area of a square is side x side, so look for the number that times itself gives the area. If the perimeter of a square is known, share it into four equal sides, then find the area. In a rectangle the area is length x breadth, so a missing breadth is the number that times the length gives the area. An L-shape is cut into two rectangles whose areas are added. The area of a square is side x side. So look for a number that times itself gives 169. 13 x 13 = 169, so each side is 13 cm. First find the side. 4 x 17 = 68, so each side is 17 m. Then the area of a square of side 17 m is 17 x 17 = 289 sq. m. Area is length x breadth, so 13 x breadth gives 104. 13 x 8 = 104, so the breadth is 8 cm. Check it: the area of a 13 cm by 8 cm rectangle is 104 sq. cm. Cut it along a dashed line into two rectangles. The area of an 11 m by 3 m rectangle is 11 x 3 = 33 sq. m. The area of a 5 m by 2 m rectangle is 5 x 2 = 10 sq. m. Together 33 + 10 = 43, so the floor is 43 sq. m. In the same way, 6 equal squares of 9 sq. cm cover 6 x 9 = 54 sq. cm.
